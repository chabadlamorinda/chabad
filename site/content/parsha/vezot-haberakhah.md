## The Rebbe's teaching

The Rebbe taught that the Torah is the inheritance of every Jew, from the greatest scholar to the youngest child, and that this is why we dance with the closed scroll on Simchat Torah.

Vezot Haberakhah, the last portion of the Torah, is read on Simchat Torah. It contains the verse that is the first a Jewish child learns: "The Torah that Moses commanded us is an inheritance of the congregation of Jacob." In a talk on Simchat Torah, the Rebbe asked why a child is taught this idea first, when he cannot yet understand it. Why not wait?

His answer is that the heart of the idea is that G-d and His Torah are one. This is not an advanced concept built on simpler ones; it is the essence of Torah itself, so it must be the foundation. A newborn can inherit an estate regardless of his age or maturity, and in the same way every Jew shares in this connection, whatever his level.

The Rebbe then explained the dancing. On Simchat Torah the scrolls are wrapped and carried in dance, which seems odd for a holiday about learning. But the joy does not come from how much we have learned. It comes from the Torah's essence, which even an unlearned Jew shares. That is why the scroll is closed.

He also taught, citing the earlier Rebbes of Chabad, that on Simchat Torah the Torah itself wants to dance around the reading platform, but it has no feet. So the Jewish people become its feet. Feet respond instantly to the head, and feet carry the head to places it could not reach alone. When we dance, we commit ourselves to carry out the Torah joyfully and spontaneously, and in doing so we lift the Torah higher.

What this means for us: no one has to qualify to belong. Our connection to the Torah does not depend on how much we know, only on being heirs. See [Simchat Torah for beginners](/learn/simchat-torah-for-beginners) and [hakafot](/learn/hakafot).

## A story

A well-known story; its details cannot be confirmed. It is told in Sipurei Chassidim, a classic collection of stories about the Baal Shem Tov and his students.

One Simchat Torah in the town of Mezibuz, the closest students of the Baal Shem Tov danced late into the night, drinking as they danced. The Baal Shem Tov's wife feared that the wine would run out before Kiddush and Havdalah. She asked her husband to tell them to stop. He smiled and told her to go and tell them herself, saying they would listen and go home.

She opened the door of the room. And she saw the dancers surrounded by fire, hovering above them like a wedding canopy. She said nothing. Instead she went down to the cellar, carried up as much wine as she could, and quietly set it on their table.

In a later year the same scene repeated. At the height of the joy, the shoelaces of one disciple tore, and he could no longer keep dancing. The Baal Shem Tov's daughter Udel, who had been married for many years with only one child, offered him a good pair of shoes if he promised her a son that year. He hesitated, since only the Baal Shem Tov could make such a promise, but he gave his word. In that year, according to the story, she gave birth to a son, Rabbi Baruch of Mezibuz, who became a Chassidic leader.

The tale teaches that the joy of dancing with the Torah is real enough to move even the people closest to holiness, and that this joy has a power of its own.

## Ideas for the Shabbat table

- Ask everyone: what is something you inherited from a parent or grandparent that you cherish, and not because of its value?
- For children: the Torah wants to dance but has no feet. What other things in our home need us to be their "feet"?
- Adults: Do you ever feel you do not know enough to belong? What does it mean that the Torah is an inheritance, not an exam?
- Discuss: why do we dance with the scrolls closed, instead of opening them and reading?
- Talk about it: the portion ends with the death of Moses and the Torah's last words about him. What would you want people to say of your life?
- Try this: this year, take a child to hold the Torah and dance on Simchat Torah, and see [the Simchat Torah guide](/learn/simchat-torah-for-beginners) for how to prepare.

## An essay

An inheritance is a strange kind of gift. You do not earn it, you do not have to ask for it, and you receive it before you are old enough to understand. A baby who inherits a house does not know what a house is. Yet the house belongs to the baby.

This is how the Torah describes itself in the last portion of Deuteronomy: a morasha, an inheritance, of the whole congregation of Jacob. The Rebbe pointed out that this means every Jew, scholar or beginner, owns it equally. Nobody needs to feel like a guest.

Many of us have felt, at some point, that we came to Judaism too late, or know too little, or are not "the type." The idea of inheritance answers this directly. Your place in the Torah was set long before you were born. You do not need to prove that you deserve it. You only need to come and take what is yours.

That may be why Simchat Torah is the most joyful day of the year. It is not a day for proving how much we have learned. It is a day for celebrating that the Torah is ours, and that we are its. We dance with the scrolls closed because the celebration is not about the words inside. It is about the relationship.

And the same Torah that ends with Moses' last words immediately begins again with the first words of Genesis. Our connection never finishes. Every year we start over, as heirs who are still receiving.

## Common questions

### What is Parshat Vezot Haberakhah about?

Parshat Vezot Haberakhah (Deuteronomy 33:1-34:12) is the last portion of the Torah: Moses blesses each of the tribes, ascends Mount Nebo to see the land, and passes away at one hundred and twenty. The Torah testifies that no prophet like Moses ever arose.

### What does the Rebbe teach about Parshat Vezot Haberakhah?

The Rebbe teaches that the Torah is an inheritance given equally to every Jew, which is why we dance with the closed scrolls on Simchat Torah, and that the Jewish people serve as the Torah's "feet."

### When is Parshat Vezot Haberakhah read?

It is read on Simchat Torah, the day we finish the Torah and begin it again. For the exact dates, see [the Jewish holidays](/jewish-holidays) and [Simchat Torah](/holidays/simchat-torah).
