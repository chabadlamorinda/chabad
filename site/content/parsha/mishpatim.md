## The Rebbe's teaching

The Rebbe teaches that G-d's revelation at Sinai came from above and passed, but the lasting change came from the Jewish people's own actions, rising from below, and that is what makes holiness stay.

The Rebbe begins with a puzzle about order. Near the end of Mishpatim, the Torah returns to Sinai with the words, "And to Moses He said, 'Ascend...'" Rashi says this passage was spoken before the Ten Commandments, so it is placed out of order. Why does the Torah put it here? Why does Rashi say "before the Ten Commandments" and not "before the Giving of the Torah"? And why does the story of Sinai seem to be cut in two, with civil laws in between?

His answer is that the Giving of the Torah had two goals. The first was revelation from above: G-d gave the Jewish people the commandments. This is the theme of the portion of Yitro, with the Ten Commandments and the laws Moses received in his forty days. The second was elevation from below: through a covenant, the Jewish people became G-d's servants. This is the theme of Mishpatim, with its covenant, its altar, sacrifices and the people's declaration, "We will do and we will listen." The "Ascend" passage begins this second theme, which started before the first was complete. The word "ascend" fits, because this theme is about the lower world rising toward G-d.

The Rebbe then draws a lesson. The revelation at Sinai was temporary: when it ended, the mountain returned to its ordinary state. But the ascent that the Jewish people achieved through their own deeds in the physical world became part of them, and so it lasts. The Sanctuary is the example. By human effort, its holiness was set into a physical structure, so G-d's presence could dwell among the people permanently.

What it means for us: inspiration that comes from above is precious, but it passes. Lasting growth comes from the concrete, ongoing work of bringing holiness into our everyday, physical life.

## A story

Rabbi Mayer Plotkin, a businessman who later directed many Chabad activities in Montreal, tells this story from his student days. When he first came from Montreal to New York to study at the Torah Vodaas yeshivah in Brooklyn, he lived in the dormitory. Across the hall lived a boy who had attended the Chabad school in Montreal. He was a diligent student who wanted to keep learning Torah, but his mother wanted him to leave the yeshivah for college. She believed the Lubavitcher Rebbe would agree with her, since the Rebbe himself had attended a university, and so she arranged a private audience for the family.

After she explained her concern, the Rebbe was puzzled. Her son was doing well in yeshivah, so why would she want him to leave? "How can I agree to this?" he asked. He tested the boy on several Torah topics, and the boy stayed in yeshivah.

Some time later, a pay phone in the dormitory hallway rang for about twenty minutes. Mayer finally answered it, annoyed. The caller was Rabbi Hodakov, the Rebbe's secretary, who wanted to speak with the boy across the hall. The boy did not believe it, until Mayer convinced him. He came back with an astonishing message: the Rebbe would mention his name the next day at the resting place of the previous Rebbe, on one condition, that he commit to reciting Chitas, the daily Torah study of Chumash, Tehillim and Tanya. He agreed at once.

Mayer says he had his own experience. When he found Tanya difficult and asked whether he should wait until he understood it better, the Rebbe answered with a long letter. It encouraged him to study diligently and persistently, even when something was not fully clear, because understanding comes with time and effort.

Doing first, and understanding along the way.

## Ideas for the Shabbat table

- Ask: what is something you do every day without fully understanding why? What happens when we keep doing it?
- Ask the children: "We will do and we will listen." Why might it be good to say "yes" to something good before we understand everything?
- Discuss: inspiration comes and goes. What helps us keep the good that we felt in a special moment?
- Talk about Chitas and Tanya: is there a short daily practice, five minutes of learning or a Psalm, you could start together as a family?
- Adults: what question about Judaism do you carry? Would you like to keep asking it while taking one small step?
- Light a candle or say a blessing together, and talk about how a physical act can hold holiness.

## An essay

"We will do and we will listen." Those words, said by the Jewish people at Sinai, put doing before understanding. They are sometimes taken to mean "do not ask questions." The Rebbe teaches the opposite. Torah study is a lifelong journey of understanding, and questions are welcome. But our commitment to goodness does not have to wait until we understand everything.

Think of the way we learn almost everything. A child does not understand why she brushes her teeth. A musician plays scales long before she hears the beauty of the music. Understanding grows from doing.

The Rebbe's teaching on Mishpatim goes a step further. There are two kinds of holiness. One comes down as a gift: a moment of inspiration at a wedding, a visit to Israel, a Yom Kippur prayer that opens the heart. It is beautiful, and it fades. The other is built from below, by our own steady deeds: lighting candles every Friday, giving charity, learning a little every day. That kind stays, because it has become part of who we are.

That is why the laws in this portion are so ordinary: fair treatment of workers, honest courts, kindness to the stranger, care for the poor. They do not look like Sinai. And yet they are the way Sinai stays with us, in our offices, kitchens and conversations.

In the story, a Rebbe told a young man that his commitment to daily study mattered, and told another student to keep learning Tanya even when it was not fully clear. Both were being invited to build something lasting from small, regular steps.

## Common questions

### What is Parshat Mishpatim about?
Parshat Mishpatim (Exodus 21:1-24:18) gives many civil and ethical laws, such as fairness to workers, care for the stranger, the widow and the orphan, and honesty in courts. It ends with the Jewish people accepting the covenant with "We will do and we will listen." See [the Ten Commandments](/learn/ten-commandments) and [the book of Exodus](/learn/book-of-exodus).

### What does the Rebbe teach about Parshat Mishpatim?
The Rebbe teaches that the revelation at Sinai was temporary, while the ascent the Jewish people achieved through their own actions is lasting, and that is why lasting growth comes from concrete deeds. Read more in [why we do mitzvot](/learn/why-mitzvot).

### What does "We will do and we will listen" mean?
It means that the Jewish people committed to keep the Torah before fully understanding it, and the Rebbe explains that this does not mean we stop asking questions. It means our commitment to do good does not have to wait for full understanding. See [daily Torah study](/learn/daily-torah-study) and the [parsha page](/parsha).
