## The Rebbe's teaching

Each of us has been given gifts and a set number of days, and the Rebbe teaches us to take an honest accounting of how we use them.

Pekudei begins with an accounting of all the materials used to build the Sanctuary. The Rebbe draws a personal lesson. Every person has been given gifts such as wealth, intelligence, time or ability, and every person is, in a sense, a "master of accounts." These gifts exist to increase the good in the world, so it is wise to stop and ask how they are being used.

Time is the most precious gift. We are given a set number of days, and each day, hour and moment has its purpose: Torah study, helping others, mitzvot. Time cannot be crammed or made up later. Each day's purpose is meant to be fulfilled on that day. A person who counts his moments makes each one count.

The Rebbe also explains why Pekudei follows Vayakhel. Vayakhel is about gathering as one community, where differences of status or ability do not matter. Pekudei is about each individual's unique role. Just as the head, heart and hand of one body have different tasks, every person has a mission that no one else can fill, and if it is not fulfilled, the world is lacking. The order matters: starting with belonging keeps our individuality from becoming self-absorption.

What this means in practice:

- Regularly take stock of your time and abilities, and ask how they serve Torah, kindness and mitzvot.
- Treat each day as having its own purpose, not as something to make up later.
- Begin from a sense of belonging to a community, and then offer your own gifts.

## A story

This story is told as a true account from Jerusalem. In the Beit Yisrael neighborhood, around Passover time, Rabbi Shlomo Zalman Porush, a man of modest means, was secretary of a society that distributed funds from abroad to poor families. When funds from the Minsk community were delayed before Passover, he borrowed 200 napoleons of gold from his elderly neighbor Reb Faivish Stoller, a carpenter, so the families could eat. There was no written contract.

After the holiday, Rabbi Shlomo Zalman repaid 110 napoleons at once, and weeks later the other 90. But Reb Faivish's memory was failing, and he denied receiving the first payment. The case went to Rabbi Shmuel Salant, the chief rabbi of Jerusalem.

Rabbi Shlomo Zalman offered to simply repay the money again. Rabbi Salant refused: since this involved charity funds, the honesty of the one who handled them had to be beyond question. He ordered the oath.

Rabbi Shlomo Zalman fasted and recited Psalms for three days, and then swore that he had paid. Afterward, he sold his house, added his savings, and gave 110 napoleons to a committee building a new synagogue, on condition that his name be left out. He would not profit from money he had kept by oath.

Months later, Reb Faivish arrived with a purse of 110 napoleons he had found in a drawer in his workshop.

## Ideas for the Shabbat table

- Moses gave a detailed accounting of everything given for the Sanctuary. Why is it important that people who handle shared money are open about it?
- Children: if you had a bag of coins to share, how would you show others you were fair?
- What are three gifts you have been given (a skill, time, a home)? How did you use them this week?
- The Rebbe said each day has its own purpose. What would you like to say about today when it ends?
- Adults: is there a time of day when you could do a short, kind, honest review of your day?
- The portion ends with the Divine presence filling the Sanctuary. What do you think "filling" our lives with meaning looks like?

## An essay

Few words make us more uncomfortable than "accounting." We think of audits, spreadsheets, the quiet fear of finding out we did badly. But Pekudei begins with a list: so many talents of gold, so many of silver, so many of copper. The Torah does not hide the math. It celebrates it.

The Rebbe teaches that this is a kind of freedom. A person who counts his gifts and his days is a person who knows he has them. Most of the stress of life comes from not looking: a calendar that fills itself, money that somehow disappears, good intentions we never get around to.

Notice that the accounting in Pekudei is not a punishment. It comes after the Sanctuary is built, and it is open to all. Moses, the most trusted of leaders, still gave a full account. The story of Rabbi Shlomo Zalman echoes this: for a person who handles other people's trust, even a perfectly honest memory is not enough. The standard is "beyond question," and he held himself to it at great cost.

For most of us, the stakes are gentler. We are trusted with the people we love, with our time, with our work. The idea of a regular, honest taking stock, done with joy and not with guilt, is one of the most practical Jewish habits there is. Ten minutes at the end of Shabbat, or at the end of a day, asking: what did I do with what I was given?

The Torah ends this portion with a cloud filling the Sanctuary, a sign that a life of careful stewardship has room for something greater. ## Common questions

### What is Parshat Pekudei about?
Parshat Pekudei is about the accounting of the materials used for the Sanctuary, the making of the priestly garments, and the day the Sanctuary was set up and filled with G-d's presence. See [The Mishkan](/learn/the-mishkan).

### What does the Rebbe teach about Parshat Pekudei?
The Rebbe teaches that every person should take stock of their gifts and time, and use each day for its purpose. He also explains that Pekudei, about each individual's unique role, follows Vayakhel, about belonging to a community.

### What is the main lesson of Parshat Pekudei?
The main lesson is honesty and stewardship: those who handle shared resources should be beyond question, and each of us should account for how we use our time and gifts. Read more about [honesty](/learn/honesty-and-truth) and [business ethics](/learn/business-ethics).
