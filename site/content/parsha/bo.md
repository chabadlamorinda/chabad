## The Rebbe's teaching

The Rebbe teaches that the light a Jew gains through Torah and prayer is meant to enter the "Egypt" of everyday life, where it lifts up what is holy.

The Rebbe begins with the plague of darkness. The Torah says that during it, "all the Children of Israel had light in their dwellings." The Midrash reads this as a miracle: a special light accompanied the Jews wherever they went, and it let them see the hidden gold, silver and treasures of the Egyptians. Rashi reads it differently. For Rashi, the only miracle was the darkness itself, which left the Egyptians helpless, so that the Jews could look through their houses by ordinary light. The Rebbe asks why they differ.

He explains that a basic principle of Jewish life is to do mitzvot through natural means and not through miracles. As an example, the Alter Rebbe waited to say the blessing on the moon until a boat's official stopped the boat willingly, and not by a miracle. Rashi applies the principle here. The Midrash gives reasons why it did not apply to the Egyptian wealth: the principle mostly governs mitzvot given after Sinai, this was a reward promised to Abraham rather than a commandment, and reaching hidden treasures needed a special light.

The Rebbe then gives a deeper reading. The Midrash includes the light in the plague, because the light we gain through Torah and prayer has to enter the "Egyptian homes" of the physical world and bring out the holiness in it. Rashi's reading shows a later stage, when the world has been refined and the soul returns to its true place.

The practical lessons are these. Jewish life has two parts: the inner work of Torah and prayer, and the outer work of making the physical world holy. The inner light should reach the outer work. Even in the darkness of exile, G-d arranges circumstances so that we can serve Him without obstacles, though the mitzvah itself remains natural. And the promise of light in "your dwellings" applies both physically and spiritually.

## A story

This is how the Rebbe's tefillin campaign began, as it is told in the Rebbe's own teachings. In early June 1967, as Egypt closed the Straits of Tiran and two days before the Six Day War began, the Rebbe spoke at a gathering. He cited the Torah's promise that "all the nations of the world will see that the Name of G-d is proclaimed upon you and will fear you," which the Sages read as referring to the tefillin worn on the head. He said that this mitzvah gives the Jewish people added strength in times of crisis.

Chassidim around the world, especially in Israel, answered quickly. They looked for ways to offer soldiers and civilians a chance to put on tefillin. Shortly after the Western Wall was liberated, many Lubavitch chassidim, some in uniform, invited soldiers praying there to put on tefillin. Soon a pushcart with spare pairs stood at the Wall, staffed by approachable chassidim. Many of the hundreds of thousands who later began wearing tefillin first met the mitzvah there.

A London businessman, Benzion Rader, was once asked by a neighbor why the Rebbe had chosen this mitzvah. He had no answer. Later in 1967, during a private audience, he asked the Rebbe himself. The Rebbe gave two answers. First, the Talmud says that a Jew who has worn tefillin on his head even once no longer belongs to a certain category. Second, a Jew in Miami who sees a photo of Jews putting on tefillin at the Wall may be inspired to do the same.

In 1974 Rader visited Miami on business. A local accountant who had not worn tefillin since his bar mitzvah told him that a photo of Jews putting on tefillin at the Western Wall had moved him to begin again.

One photograph, one pair of tefillin, a light carried into a distant home.

## Ideas for the Shabbat table

- Ask: what is "light" in our home? A candle, a kind word, a song, a story?
- Ask the children: the Israelites had light in their homes while it was dark outside. When is our home a bright place for someone who needs it?
- Discuss: "Torah and prayer" are the inner work, and our job and daily tasks are the outer work. How can the first touch the second?
- Talk about the photograph in the story. Has a picture or a story ever inspired you to do something Jewish?
- For children: tefillin remind us to serve G-d with our heart and mind. What would you like to dedicate your heart and mind to?
- Adults: is there a Jewish activity you stopped, like Benzion Rader's friend with his tefillin, that you could begin again?

## An essay

Most of us live in two worlds. One is the world of our quiet moments: a blessing before eating, a Shabbat candle, a few words of Psalms. The other is the world of emails, traffic, deadlines and meals on the run. It is easy to feel that the first world is holy and the second is "Egypt," something to get through.

The Rebbe's teaching on Bo says that the light is meant to move from the first to the second. The plague of darkness shows it: the Israelites had light in their dwellings, and with that light they could see what lay hidden in the Egyptian homes. The point is that the light we gain through Torah and prayer is not for hiding in a corner. It is for going into our workplaces, kitchens and conversations, and finding the holiness already waiting there.

This is why mitzvot are done with real things: a pair of tefillin, a candle, a coin for charity, a loaf of challah. They bring something spiritual into the physical world. In the story, one photograph from the Western Wall carried that light to a man in Miami who had not worn tefillin since his bar mitzvah. The Rebbe understood that a single picture can speak to the soul.

You do not have to leave your life to find the light. Begin with one place that feels "dark," a busy morning, a tense family dinner, a lonely evening, and ask what small light you could bring there: a blessing, a kind word, a minute of quiet before the meal.

## Common questions

### What is Parshat Bo about?
Parshat Bo (Exodus 10:1-13:16) tells of the last three plagues (locusts, darkness, the death of the firstborn), the first mitzvot given to the Jewish people as a nation, the Passover offering and the Exodus from Egypt. Read more in [the ten plagues](/learn/ten-plagues) and [the Passover Seder](/learn/passover-seder).

### What does the Rebbe teach about Parshat Bo?
The Rebbe teaches that the light of Torah and prayer must enter the physical world, and that the Jewish people had light in their homes during the plague of darkness. Learn about the mitzvah the Rebbe encouraged in [tefillin](/learn/tefillin) and about [the Rebbe](/learn/the-rebbe).

### Where does the mitzvah of tefillin come from in Parshat Bo?
The Torah says twice in this portion that the Exodus should be "a sign upon your hand and a reminder between your eyes," which is the source of the mitzvah of tefillin. See [starting tefillin before bar mitzvah](/learn/starting-tefillin-before-bar-mitzvah) or find this week's portion on the [parsha page](/parsha).
