## The Rebbe's teaching

The offering G-d wants is "of you," and the Rebbe teaches that this means ourselves: coming close to G-d by bringing our whole selves.

Vayikra describes the animal offerings in the Sanctuary. The Rebbe asks: with no Temple standing, what do these laws mean for us? His answer is that every Jew has an inner Sanctuary, so the service continues wherever Jews are.

The Torah says, "When a man among you brings an offering." The Rebbe reads the unusual word order as "a man who brings near *of you* an offering": the offering is the self. The Hebrew word for offering, *korban*, comes from a word meaning "to come near." The offering is the act of drawing close to G-d.

The Torah adds, "from the cattle." The Rebbe explains that this points to the animal soul, our physical desires and instincts. The deeper aim is not to destroy these drives but to redirect them, so that the same energy is used for love of G-d. Outward actions remain, but the motive becomes spiritual: "In all your ways, know Him."

The Rebbe also notes that animals were inspected for blemishes. So we must honestly look for flaws in our own character and work to correct them. And he offers a reassurance: when old failings come back into the mind, they do not mean we have failed. They can be transformed by deeper love. The Rebbe's father-in-law taught that the offering depends on the individual and is within reach of every Jew, whatever the past.

In practice:

- Make an honest, not mechanical, review of ourselves.
- Do not be paralyzed by the past.
- Bring holy purpose into ordinary acts: eating on Shabbat, wearing tzitzit, putting on tefillin.

## A story

A well-known story; its details cannot be confirmed. The tzaddik known as the Bnei Yissachar told this story of the Baal Shem Tov, the founder of Chassidism.

One Yom Kippur eve, the Baal Shem Tov entered the synagogue visibly troubled. After Kol Nidrei he paused, then recovered and led the evening prayers with joy. Afterward, he explained to his students.

In a village there lived a Jewish tenant farmer and his wife who were respected by their landlord. When their son was born, they both died soon after. The landlord raised the boy as his own. He gave the child his parents' small keepsakes: a Chumash and the siddur his mother had prayed from.

One autumn, the boy saw Jews traveling to the city for the High Holidays. That night he dreamed of his parents, urging him to return to his faith, and the dreams came every night. The landlord dismissed them.

On the eve of Yom Kippur, the boy took his mother's siddur and ran to the synagogue, arriving as Kol Nidrei began. He saw people in white, weeping, and he wept too, but did not know what to say. He cried out: "I do not know what to pray or how to pray." And he said, "Here, G-d, I give you the whole siddur!" He placed the siddur on the lectern and rested his head on its worn pages, weeping.

His prayer was accepted. The Baal Shem Tov, sensing the sincerity of the child, rejoiced, and began the evening Barchu with great joy.

## Ideas for the Shabbat table

- The Torah says to bring an offering "of you." What is one thing about yourself you could offer to G-d or to others?
- Children: the boy gave G-d "the whole siddur." What would you give if you did not know the right words?
- Korban means "coming close." What brings you closer to the people you love?
- The Rebbe says we should offer our natural drives, not destroy them. How can energy or even stubbornness be used for good?
- Adults: what past mistake still weighs on you? What would it mean to let love transform it?
- Think of an everyday action, like eating a meal. How might you bring a holy purpose to it?

## An essay

Vayikra raises an old question: how do you give a gift to someone who already has everything? G-d needs nothing from us. So what is an "offering"?

The Rebbe's answer is that what G-d wants is us. Not our things, but our closeness. The word *korban* means exactly that. The animals were a way of showing something that cannot be shown directly: I want to be near You.

That makes this ancient section relevant to anyone who has ever wished for a better relationship, with G-d, with family, with their own conscience. In every relationship, the gift that matters is attention that comes from the self. A busy parent who truly listens for five minutes gives more than an expensive present. The Torah says: of you.

The story of the boy in the synagogue is the same idea in a child's voice. He had no knowledge, no words, only a worn siddur and a longing. He offered what he had, and it was enough.

The Rebbe adds two things that make this practical. First, we do not have to erase who we are. Our energy, temper, appetite and ambition are the "animal" in us, and they can be turned toward good. Second, the past is not a jail. Old failings may surface, but they can be redeemed by love.

Try one small offering this week: do something you usually do on autopilot, say a meal blessing or give a phone call, with full attention. That is a *korban*, a coming close.

## Common questions

### What is Parshat Vayikra about?
Parshat Vayikra is about the offerings brought in the Sanctuary: the burnt offering, the grain offering, the peace offering, and offerings for sin and guilt. It begins the book of Leviticus. See [Leviticus](/learn/book-of-leviticus).

### What does the Rebbe teach about Parshat Vayikra?
The Rebbe teaches that every Jew has an inner Sanctuary and that the offering "of you" means offering ourselves and refining our physical drives in G-d's service. Past failings can be transformed by love and need not paralyze us.

### What is the main lesson of Parshat Vayikra?
The main lesson is that G-d wants us to come close and to offer our true selves, not only things. [Prayer](/learn/prayer) is today's way of drawing near, and [teshuvah](/learn/teshuvah) is how we return.
