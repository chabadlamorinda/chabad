## The Rebbe's teaching

Love for every Jew is not a feeling that depends on how the other person behaves; it comes from the deepest level of the soul, and the Torah gives it a shape. The Rebbe, in a talk on Parshat Kedoshim, starts with a puzzle. Rabbi Akiva called "love your fellow as yourself" a great principle of the Torah. Hillel, when asked to teach the whole Torah on one foot, summed it up with this principle ("What is hateful to you, do not do to your fellow") and said that this is the whole Torah. Why do they describe the same commandment so differently?

The Rebbe answers by looking at the soul. Every Jewish soul has an essence that is bound to G-d and goes beyond even the Torah. This is why the Talmud says that a Jew who sins is still a Jew. Love that comes from this essence is unconditional. It does not ask whether the other person is learned or distant, observant or not. And it is not only spiritual; it reaches a person's physical needs too, such as a meal, a job or a visit.

Because a Jew's connection to G-d is expressed through the Torah, this love becomes a mitzvah, a commandment with a shape. That is Rabbi Akiva's view: it is a great general principle among the commandments. Hillel looks at the source. There, the entire Torah exists to reveal the deep unity of the Jewish people, so it is the whole Torah.

The Rebbe adds a practical point. Oneness that is real cannot stay hidden forever. A person who is far from Torah cannot yet feel the oneness he shares with other Jews. This is why the Alter Rebbe, in the Tanya, connects loving others with drawing them closer to Torah: it helps them feel what is already true.

What it means for us: love every Jew without conditions, help with both body and soul, and never use that love as a reason to drop what the Torah asks. Even when our efforts do not seem to work, the mitzvah of loving our fellow Jew is complete.

## A story

A well-known story; its details cannot be confirmed. It is told of Krakow, in a winter when a smallpox epidemic struck the Jewish quarter. Children were dying. The doctors and home remedies did not help, and the rabbi called days of fasting and prayer, but nothing changed.

The elders turned to a mystical practice of asking for a sign in a dream. They fasted, said Psalms and immersed in the mikvah. That night all of them had the same dream: an old man in white told them that Shlomo the butcher should lead the prayers.

The next morning they went to Shlomo's home. He was stunned. He could barely read, he said. They persuaded him to come to the synagogue anyway. There he slipped out and disappeared. A little later he returned, pushing a wheelbarrow that carried his old butcher's scales under a cloth. He set them on the podium, and, crying, he asked G-d to accept his scales, and to heal the children.

That evening the children began to recover. The community kept the scales in a glass case, and when they checked the scales of other merchants, they found them all slightly off, not enough to be dishonest, but off. Shlomo had checked his scales twice every day. According to the story, the scales stayed in the synagogue for over two hundred years, until the Second World War.

## Ideas for the Shabbat table

- Ask: who is someone you find it hard to love? What is one thing they do well?
- For children: if someone in our class has no one to play with, what is one thing we can do on Monday? Let each child answer.
- Talk about honest weights: the Torah says the scales must be exact. Where in our lives is there something we "measure" that should be exact, such as our word, our time, our work?
- Discuss: Rabbi Akiva says love is one great principle. Hillel says it is everything. Which feels truer to you, and why?
- Ask the adults: when did a Jew you hardly knew help you in a time of need?

## An essay

Kedoshim starts with an enormous demand: "You shall be holy." Then, surprisingly, the Torah does not describe mountaintop experiences. It describes ordinary life: respect your parents, keep Shabbat, leave the corner of the field for the poor, do not steal, do not cheat in business, do not hold a grudge, and love your fellow as yourself. Holiness, the Torah is telling us, happens in the details of how we treat one another.

The Rebbe's teaching about love gives us a reason to take this seriously. If every Jew's soul comes from one source, the other person is not a stranger. A neighbor in Orinda, a parent at the school gate in Moraga, a man in line at the grocery store in Lafayette: each carries the same deep bond to G-d we do, even if it is not visible, even if neither of us knows it.

That changes what love looks like. We stop waiting to feel warm before we act. We begin to act, and the warmth often follows. A meal for someone who is ill, an honest word in a business deal, an invitation to a Shabbat table for someone who is new in town: these are the "scales" of the Torah, small, precise acts which we check every day.

This week, pick one person whose soul you will treat as part of your own. Do one thing for them that costs you something. And then see what changes in you.

## Common questions

### What is Parshat Kedoshim about?
Parshat Kedoshim (Leviticus 19:1-20:27) tells the whole community to be holy and then describes what holiness looks like in daily life: honoring parents, keeping Shabbat, helping the poor, honest business and loving your fellow as yourself.

### What does the Rebbe teach about Parshat Kedoshim?
The Rebbe teaches that love for every Jew comes from the soul's essence, so it is unconditional and reaches both physical and spiritual needs. He also teaches that this love is a mitzvah, and it moves us to draw others closer to Torah.

### What does "love your fellow as yourself" mean?
It is a commandment from this portion (Leviticus 19:18) that Rabbi Akiva called a great principle of the Torah. Chassidic teaching explains that all Jewish souls come from one source, so loving another is like loving a part of ourselves.
