## The Rebbe's teaching

No one is ever beyond return, and real change is complete only when it reaches every corner of our lives. The Lubavitcher Rebbe begins with two puzzling details in Parshat Metzora. Why is a portion of the Torah named after "the metzora," a person with an unpleasant affliction? And why does the Torah say the metzora "shall be brought" to the priest, in the passive, as if he does not walk there himself?

The Sages teach that this affliction, tzaraat, came as a result of slander, speech that divides people. Because a Jew's holiness depends on unity, the person who spreads division had to leave the camp. Yet the Alter Rebbe teaches that every Jew will eventually return to G-d. The Rebbe explains that the word "brought" hints at how return begins. At first it is often a gift that comes from beyond us: a moment of awakening that pulls us out of our usual habits, even against our own nature.

Then comes the second stage. The priest goes out to the metzora. The awakening is no longer something that happens to us from outside; it becomes ours. It settles into our character and into the whole of our lives, including the places "outside the camp" that we may think have nothing to do with holiness, such as our business, our speech and our free time.

This is also why the Rebbe teaches that we can call the portion Metzora without fear. Even what looks like the lowest point can become the beginning of something good.

What it means for us: when we feel far from who we want to be, we can trust that the pull to return is real. And once it comes, we do not leave it in a single moment of inspiration. We let it travel into the everyday places of our lives.

## A story

A well-known story; its details cannot be confirmed. A visitor traveled to the Polish town of Radin to meet the famous scholar Rabbi Yisrael Meir HaKohen, known as the Chofetz Chaim, who had devoted his life to teaching the Jewish laws of guarding our speech. On the street, the visitor stopped an elderly man with a beard and asked for directions to the great rabbi's home.

The old man pointed the way. Then he added that the Chofetz Chaim was not really such a great scholar or tzaddik. The visitor was shocked and offended, and in his anger he slapped the man.

When he reached the rabbi's study, he discovered that the man he had struck was the Chofetz Chaim himself. The visitor wept and begged for forgiveness. The Chofetz Chaim answered kindly that no apology was needed. He had deserved it, he said. He had spent his life warning people not to speak badly about others, and now he had learned that one should not speak badly even about oneself.

## Ideas for the Shabbat table

- Ask everyone: what is one kind sentence someone said to you this week that you still remember? What is one unkind sentence?
- For children: if words were like toothpaste, why is it so hard to put them back? Let each child say one kind thing to every person at the table.
- Ask the adults: where in my life do I keep holiness "outside the camp," in business, in the car, on my phone?
- Discuss: the priest goes out to meet the metzora. Who in our family or community might be waiting for someone to come to them?
- Try a family challenge for one week: a day with no complaining about anyone who is not in the room.
- Read more about guarding our words in [what Jewish law says about gossip](/learn/lashon-hara) and [what Tanya teaches about speech](/learn/tanya-on-speech).

## An essay

We usually think of words as light things. They leave our mouths in a second and are gone. Parshat Metzora asks us to take them far more seriously. In the Sages' understanding, the affliction of tzaraat came from careless speech, and the way back began with a stranger: the priest, who left the safety of the camp to meet the person who had been pushed out.

There is a tender idea in that. When someone has done wrong, the Torah does not leave them to figure it out alone. A caring person goes out to meet them. The Rebbe adds that a person is "brought," pulled beyond himself, before he is ready to bring himself. Many of us can remember a moment like that: a phone call at the right time, a kind word from a friend, a Shabbat dinner we almost did not attend.

The second half of the Rebbe's teaching is the practical one. A moment of inspiration is only the beginning. The Torah wants the change to enter our work, our friendships and our speech, the quiet places where we are most tempted to say that Judaism does not apply.

So here is a simple practice for this week. Choose one conversation in which you will speak only what builds. Then choose one person you have not spoken to in a long time, and, like the priest, go out to meet them. You may be surprised how much a single warm word can heal, in both directions.

## Common questions

### What is Parshat Metzora about?
Parshat Metzora (Leviticus 14:1-15:33) describes how a person healed of tzaraat is purified and returns to the community, and it also gives laws of purity for the home and the body. Its theme is that no one is permanently outside the community.

### What does the Rebbe teach about Parshat Metzora?
The Rebbe teaches that the portion is a promise of return: every Jew will come back, first through an awakening from beyond ourselves and then through making that change part of our everyday lives. He also teaches that change is complete when it reaches even the areas of life we think of as far from holiness.

### Why is tzaraat connected to speech?
The Sages teach that tzaraat came as a result of slander, which creates distance between people. You can learn more in our guide to [lashon hara](/learn/lashon-hara) and about [this week's parsha](/parsha).
