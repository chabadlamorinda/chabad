## The Rebbe's teaching

The Rebbe teaches that welcoming guests is so precious that for the Jewish people it stands above even receiving the Divine Presence, because kindness is part of our inheritance from Abraham.

The Talmud learns from our portion that Abraham, who was speaking with G-d, asked G-d to wait while he welcomed three travelers. From this the Sages conclude that hospitality is greater than receiving the Divine Presence. The Rebbe asks why the Rambam cites Abraham's example in his law about hospitality, and why a Zohar teaching seems to say something different. He explains that the Zohar describes the exalted level of Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai, for whom the inner light of G-d's Presence shone continuously. For most people the law is as the Talmud says: hospitality comes first. The Rambam speaks of "the path of kindness which he practiced," meaning that Abraham's kindness, which reached even toward Ishmael, set a path for all his descendants. Through him, every Jew inherits a heart inclined to kindness.

The Rebbe then looks at the tree Abraham planted, an eshel. The Sages explain it as an orchard or an inn offering food, drink and lodging to complete strangers. What does planting a tree add after so much giving? The answer is that Abraham gave more than the bare necessities. He gave pleasure and help with life's problems. Jews are moved to give beyond what is needed, as parents want to give their children far more than just enough, and this applies to spiritual needs as well.

The Rebbe applies this to our own time. The kindness we inherited from Abraham shows itself in how generously we educate children, our own and others', and in giving them not just the basics but everything possible of Torah and mitzvot. He adds that we should give with joy and not with regret.

For us, the practical message is simple. Invite someone, especially for a Shabbat meal. Give a little more than is required. And give the next generation the best of our heritage.

## A story

Yaakov Ben-Tor tells this story in the first person, in a letter adapted by Yerachmiel Tilles for KabbalaOnline.org. It is told as a true personal experience; the page does not independently verify it.

Yaakov lived in Rehovot, Israel, and worked in Tel Aviv. One night, driving home late, he took a back road instead of the main highway. His old car had no interior lights, and the road was very dark. By the roadside he saw an elderly man hitchhiking, and he stopped.

The stranger immediately asked how the Lubavitcher Rebbe was doing. He seemed to know that Yaakov had flown the previous year to see the Rebbe and had received a blessing for his two children, and that he lived near the Chabad synagogue in Rehovot.

Then the old man gave a long talk about hospitality. He described how Abraham kept his tent open on every side and welcomed guests no matter who they were or when they came. Yaakov was astonished, because for years he and his wife had argued about his habit of bringing home unannounced guests from synagogue, often complete strangers. The stranger urged him not to argue with his wife, but to explain the reason for welcoming guests at any time.

Yaakov began to feel uneasy. How could this man know so much? Near Moshav Be'er Yaakov the man said it was his stop, wished Yaakov well and left. When Yaakov got home, his wife saw how pale he was and was startled by what he told her.

Yaakov never saw the man again. He describes him as old, with piercing eyes, heavy brows and a long grey beard, and wonders whether it could have been Abraham or Elijah the Prophet. He writes that since that night in 1983, he has carefully followed the lesson.

## Ideas for the Shabbat table

- Ask the children: if three strangers knocked on our door right now, what would we offer them? Who would help?
- Abraham was in pain and still ran to welcome guests. What does that tell us about kindness when we are tired?
- Whom could we invite for Shabbat in the coming weeks, a neighbor in Lafayette, Moraga or Orinda, a new family or someone who lives alone? Choose a name together.
- The Rebbe says Abraham gave more than the bare necessities. Is there a way we can add a little extra to our hospitality, such as a warm word, a dessert or a seat by the window?
- For adults: when someone arrives unannounced, is our first reaction welcome or worry? What could help us welcome more easily?
- Read the [guide to hosting Shabbat guests](/learn/hosting-shabbat-guests-in-lamorinda) and the [guide for Shabbat guests](/learn/shabbat-guest-guide) for easy ideas.

## An essay

There is a particular kind of tired that comes at the end of a week. The kitchen is a mess, the calendar is full, and the idea of putting another place at the table feels like too much. And yet, the Torah gives us Abraham: ninety-nine years old, recovering from his circumcision, sitting at the door of his tent in the heat of the day, looking out for someone to help.

The Rebbe's teaching makes this more than a nice story. It says that welcoming people is part of who we are. Abraham's kindness, the Rebbe explains, was not only his own. It became a path for all his descendants, and every Jew inherits a heart inclined to give. So when we open the door, we are not doing something unusual. We are being ourselves.

That also helps with the practical worry that we do not have enough. Abraham did not offer a snack. He ran, he brought bread, curd and milk and the calf, and he stood over his guests while they ate. The Rebbe notes that Abraham gave pleasure and not only what was necessary. It is easy to read that as pressure. But it can also be freeing: hospitality does not need to be perfect. It needs to be warm.

The story of the hitchhiker reminds us that these small choices matter in the real world. Yaakov and his wife had argued for years about guests, and a late-night stranger urged him to explain to her why welcoming people matters. Hospitality is something a family can talk about and choose together.

## Common questions

### What is Parshat Vayera about?
Parshat Vayera tells how Abraham welcomes three guests and learns that Sarah will have a son, how Sodom is destroyed, and how Isaac is born. It ends with the Binding of Isaac. See also [the binding of Isaac](/learn/binding-of-isaac).

### What does the Rebbe teach about Parshat Vayera?
The Rebbe teaches that Abraham's hospitality is a path of kindness that every Jew inherits, and that for the Jewish people welcoming guests is greater than receiving the Divine Presence. He also teaches that we should give beyond the bare necessities, especially in educating children.

### What is the main lesson of Parshat Vayera?
The main lesson is to open your home and your heart to others, even when it is hard. Our Sages learn from Abraham that welcoming guests is so great that he interrupted his conversation with G-d to do it. Read more about [hachnasat orchim](/learn/hachnasat-orchim).
