## The Rebbe's teaching

The Rebbe teaches that Joseph's two dreams show how a Jew lives on two planes at once: with feet on the earth, in work and everyday life, and with the head reaching toward heaven.

The question the Rebbe asks is why Joseph had two dreams that mean basically the same thing, that his brothers would bow to him. The Torah explains why Pharaoh's dreams were repeated, but it gives no reason for Joseph's. The Rebbe's answer is that they share one message but speak about two different things.

The first dream takes place on earth: Joseph and his brothers are binding sheaves in a field. The second dream is about the sun, moon and stars. Pharaoh's dreams, by contrast, concern only earthly matters, cows and ears of grain, and they move downward. Joseph's move upward, from the material to the heavenly. A Jew, the Rebbe explains, lives on two planes at once. Even while we are busy in this physical world, we are connected to a higher, spiritual reality, like the ladder of Jacob, rooted in the earth with its top in heaven.

The Rebbe draws practical lessons from the dreams:

- Work is necessary. Joseph's first dream begins with labor. In holiness, blessing comes through effort, and even a small effort is valued as great labor.
- Unite the material and the spiritual. "Binding sheaves" means joining the separate parts of ourselves into one service of G-d. Material concerns should not get in the way of a spiritual life but contribute to it.
- Keep reaching higher. The second dream shows that even when our life is full of spiritual things, there is more to reach. The ultimate goal is that even while living in a body we feel as close to G-d as before the soul came into the world.
- Effort pays back beyond what it seems to deserve. "If you labor, you will find."

For us the message is encouraging: a Jew does not have to choose between a real job and real faith. Both can be part of one life.

## A story

The Rebbe himself told a story about his father-in-law, Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn, the Previous Rebbe, which illustrates "one G-d and two worlds."

In 1927, the Previous Rebbe was arrested at night in his home in Leningrad while eating supper with his family. According to his own diary, he was taken to a prison where the questioning was recorded on long questionnaires. He refused to answer most of the questions and crossed them out. He said he was a religious scholar with no political ties, and he demanded that the promise made at his arrest be kept, so that he could pray with his tefillin. A clerk took his belongings and refused to let him keep his tallit and tefillin, calling them "bourgeois." He was given a prisoner number, and a secretary warned him in private that a serious case was being built against him.

The Rebbe later related that when an interrogator threatened the Previous Rebbe with a revolver, the Previous Rebbe replied that a person who has "one G-d and two worlds" has nothing to fear.

The Rebbe presented this as an example of the way a Jew lives: even in the most difficult place, with a threat in the room, there is a second world, and a connection that cannot be taken away. The Previous Rebbe was freed in 1927. His diary of that time is published, and the anniversary of his release is marked in Chabad communities each year.

## Ideas for the Shabbat table

- Ask the children: Joseph dreamed about sheaves of wheat and then about the sun, moon and stars. What did you dream about recently? How do you think we can know what a dream means?
- Joseph started with work in the field. What is a "field" in your life, a place where you work or learn? How could that be a place where you feel connected to heaven?
- The Rebbe says a Jew lives with "one G-d and two worlds." What do you think this means? How could it help when you feel afraid?
- Joseph never hid that G-d was with him, even as a slave and a prisoner. When is it hard to be openly Jewish? What helps?
- For adults: the Rebbe says even a small effort is valued as great labor. What small effort will you make this week?
- Read about [Joseph and his brothers](/learn/joseph-and-his-brothers) and [dreams in Jewish tradition](/learn/dreams-jewish-view) together.

## An essay

Dreams are a window into what we want. Joseph's first dream is about bread: sheaves, a field and the work of the harvest. The second is about the heavens. He first sees his place in the world, and then he sees the world's place in something bigger.

That pattern is true for many of us. We begin our lives with earthly questions: where we will live, what job we will have, how we will take care of our families. Those are important questions. The Rebbe does not say they are small. He says they are the first dream, and that the story does not end with them. A person who works and builds is also a person who looks up.

What makes this teaching so useful is that it does not ask us to leave the field. Joseph does not trade his sheaves for the stars. He is in the field, and he is under the sky. When a parent makes dinner and says a blessing, when a teacher is patient with a child, when a person works honestly and then stops for Shabbat, they live in both dreams at once.

The Previous Rebbe's story shows how deep this can go. Under arrest, even without his tefillin, he lived by the idea that there is a second world, and that a person with a connection to G-d has something that cannot be taken away. We hope never to face such a test. But we all face smaller ones, a hard diagnosis, a lonely week, a sudden loss. In those moments, the thought that our life has two planes can be steadying.

## Common questions

### What is Parshat Vayeshev about?
Parshat Vayeshev tells how Joseph is favored by Jacob and dreams of his brothers bowing to him, how the brothers sell him into slavery, and how he rises in Potiphar's house and is thrown into prison. It also includes the story of Judah and Tamar. See [Joseph and his brothers](/learn/joseph-and-his-brothers).

### What does the Rebbe teach about Parshat Vayeshev?
The Rebbe teaches that Joseph's two dreams, one on earth and one in the heavens, show that a Jew lives on two planes at once, uniting everyday work with a connection to G-d. He also teaches that effort is necessary and is valued greatly.

### What is the main lesson of Parshat Vayeshev?
Even far from home, we can show that G-d is present through the way we speak, do business and treat others. Rashi explains that Joseph always had G-d's name on his lips. For more, read about [trust in G-d](/learn/bitachon).
