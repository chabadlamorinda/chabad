## The Rebbe's teaching

Chassidic teaching explains that the one blessing of Balaam that has never faded is "How goodly are your tents, Jacob," because it speaks of the Jewish home, the place of prayer and study. (We did not find a specific talk of the Rebbe on this theme to retell here, so this section presents Chassidic teaching and the Talmud's explanation.)

Balak, king of Moab, hired the prophet Balaam to curse Israel, and each time G-d turned the curse into a blessing. Most people stop at the happy ending. The Talmud asks a sharper question: what did Balaam actually want to say? If his curses were turned into blessings, then what he wished for was the opposite of what he said. His blessings speak of strong Jewish kings, a royal dynasty that never ends, a land that stays free, and G-d's presence dwelling among the people. So his true wishes were the fall of the kings, the end of the dynasty, the loss of the land, and the departure of G-d's presence.

The Talmud then looks at history. In the days of David and Solomon the blessings came true. But later the people drifted and split apart through quarrels, the throne fell, the Temple was destroyed, and the nation was exiled for centuries. In a painful sense, Balaam's wishes came back. One blessing, however, has stayed: "How goodly are your tents." The Talmud understands the tents to be the houses of prayer and the houses of study found in every Jewish community. They have never collapsed. Jews still pray three times a day, and Torah is still studied in study halls more than three thousand years after Sinai.

A Chassidic essay on the portion adds a second thought about what Balaam saw. Rashi, it notes, teaches that the Israelites' tents were arranged so that no one could look into a neighbor's home. That small act of modesty and respect for privacy moved even an enemy to bless. The home built with respect, the author suggests, is the foundation of the family, and a private space between husband and wife is holy.

The words "Mah Tovu" now open our morning prayers. The practical lesson: we strengthen what lasts. Holding on to the blessing that survived, our homes, our prayer and our learning, is how we turn the other blessings back into reality.

## A story

A well-known Chassidic story; its details cannot be confirmed. It is told as a tale of the Baal Shem Tov, about the power of a blessing.

Two tailors, Berel and Shmerel, traveled from village to village, saving money to bring home to their families. On the way home they stopped at an inn run by a Jew who managed a landlord's estate. The innkeeper was in distress. The landlord had received precious cloth from a prince and demanded a royal garment that no tailor he had hired could make, and he threatened to jail the innkeeper's family if it was not done.

Berel and Shmerel offered to try. They made a garment the landlord admired, and each was paid 30 rubles. Then the landlord's wife told them about a Jewish family held in the dungeon for unpaid rent, who could be freed for 40 rubles. Shmerel refused: 40 rubles was more than half of his savings, and his family was waiting. Berel gave all he had, borrowed a few rubles more from Shmerel, and the family was freed.

Shmerel went home and prospered. Berel was ashamed to tell his family what he had done, and his poverty deepened. In the end he sat silently on a street corner as a beggar, blessing whoever gave him a coin. A busy merchant, who had dropped a coin in his hand, saw his business flourish and kept coming back for the blessing. People began to say that the beggar's blessings were powerful.

When the Baal Shem Tov came to town, he asked Berel for his story, and Berel told the tale of the 40 rubles. The Baal Shem Tov embraced him, brought him and his family to Medzhibuzh, and Berel became a student and a tzaddik.

The tale is told as a teaching about humility and giving. It reminds us that a sincere blessing comes from a person who is willing to give everything.

## Ideas for the Shabbat table

- Ask the children: Balak sent for someone to curse Israel, and a blessing came out. Has something ever turned out better than you expected?
- For everyone: the Torah says the tents faced so that no one could look into another's home. What does privacy and respect look like in our home?
- Ask: how do you think the words "How goodly are your tents" feel when we say them at the start of morning prayers?
- For the children: what is your favorite part of a home that makes it feel "good"? Name three things.
- Discuss: the Talmud says the houses of prayer and study are the blessing that lasted. How can we make our home one of those houses?
- Activity: each person gives someone at the table a blessing, a sentence that begins "May you..."
- More ideas in [Blessing your children](/learn/blessing-your-children) and [Shalom bayit](/learn/shalom-bayit).

## An essay

The story of Balak is a story about words, and how powerful they are. A king pays a prophet to curse a whole nation, and instead the words come out as blessings. G-d is in control of what is said about us.

But the Talmud does not let us rest on that comfort. It asks what Balaam really wanted, and then it shows how much of it came true in the long and painful history of the Jewish people. What was left? A modest, almost homely thing: tents. Houses where people pray and study. The Jewish people did not survive because of palaces. We survived because of kitchens, synagogues and classrooms, because of people who show up each morning to say a few words to G-d.

This is a hopeful idea for anyone who feels that Jewish life has become too big or too complicated to take on. The strongest part of it is also the simplest: a home, a prayer, a page of Torah. It does not need a huge budget. It needs a place where there is respect. The Chassidic teaching about the tents reminds us that respect and privacy are part of what makes a home good: a family where each person is allowed their own space and dignity.

It is also a reminder about blessing. When we give a blessing, whether to our children on Friday night or to a friend who is ill, we are using the same gift that Balaam misused. The Baal Shem Tov story teaches that a blessing is strongest when it comes from someone who has learned to give, even at a cost.

## Common questions

### What is Parshat Balak about?
Parshat Balak (Numbers 22:2-25:9) tells how Balak, king of Moab, hired the prophet Balaam to curse Israel, how Balaam's donkey saw an angel and spoke, and how Balaam instead blessed Israel three times. The portion ends with the people's failing at Shittim, and Pinchas stopping the plague.

### What does the Rebbe teach about Parshat Balak?
Chassidic teaching, which draws on the Rebbe's talks on the tents (Likkutei Sichos, vol. 13), explains that "How goodly are your tents" refers to our homes, houses of prayer and houses of study, and to the privacy and respect that make a home holy. Find this week's portion on [the parsha page](/parsha).

### What is the main lesson of Parshat Balak?
The main lesson is that G-d protects the Jewish people, turns curses into blessings, and that our homes and houses of prayer and study are the blessing that lasts. Read about [Shalom bayit](/learn/shalom-bayit) for ideas on a peaceful Jewish home.
