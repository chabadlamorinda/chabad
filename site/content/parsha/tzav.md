## The Rebbe's teaching

The fire on the altar must burn continually, and the Rebbe teaches that our love for G-d should be a fire that never goes out and is never hidden.

Tzav mentions the continual fire kept burning on the outer altar. The Rebbe asks what its importance is and what it protects against. He teaches that the altar corresponds to the heart, and the outer altar to the outer layer of the heart, our public face. Our love for G-d should be visible in how we show up in the world, not kept only as a private feeling.

He finds two dangers that the fire guards against. The first is overconfidence. A person who has reached a calm, lofty understanding may decide that intellect has outgrown passion and that the fire is no longer needed. The second is the opposite: a person who has drifted so far that he feels no connection at all may give up. The Rebbe's answer to the second is that a spark always remains in the heart, and it can be fanned into flame.

The fire must also be continual. The Chassidic masters read the words "it shall not go out" as fire that "puts out the 'no'", the inner voice of hesitation. But it only works if we feed it constantly. Enthusiasm from the past is not enough, because coldness quickly returns.

The Rebbe asks us to bring "life, involvement and fire" to three things:

- **Torah study:** not only to fulfill an obligation, but with warmth and engagement.
- **Prayer:** not as a mechanical routine but as a heartfelt request.
- **Mitzvot and tzedakah:** with inner warmth, and beautifully, not only dutifully.

## A story

A well-known story; its details cannot be confirmed. It was told by Rabbi Nechemia of Dubrovna, and it illustrates the same point.

Reb Nechemia told of a time he saw a Russian soldier standing guard on a winter night during the czar's rule. The soldier's feet froze inside his boots. For this, his commander had him whipped. The commander's reproach was that the soldier should have remembered the oath he had taken to serve the czar, and that this memory should have kept him warm.

Reb Nechemia said this incident inspired his service of G-d for the next twenty-five years. The point is clear: if the memory of a commitment to a human ruler is expected to keep a person warm on a freezing night, how much more should our commitment to G-d keep our hearts warm?

The story is told alongside a teaching of Rabbi Sholom DovBer of Lubavitch, that there is only a thin wall between coldness and heresy, and that Torah study and prayer require a flaming heart.

## Ideas for the Shabbat table

- The altar fire was lit every day and never allowed to go out. What is something in your life that needs daily attention to keep alive?
- Children: what makes you feel warm inside? How could you "add wood" to that feeling?
- The Rebbe says love for G-d should be visible, not private. What does it look like to show it?
- Adults: when you pray or study, do you feel warmth, or is it routine? What small thing might change it?
- The Rebbe teaches that a spark always remains in everyone. Who in your life needs someone to see their spark?
- Name one mitzvah you do that you could do "beautifully, not just dutifully."

## An essay

Every fireplace owner knows the secret of fire: it is not the match that matters, it is the wood. A match lights a flame in a second. Keeping it going through a long evening takes small, regular additions.

Tzav is about exactly that. The priest added wood to the altar every morning. The Torah does not ask for a single dramatic blaze, but for continuity: "it shall not go out." The Rebbe applies this to the heart. Most of us have had a moment of great inspiration, at a wedding, at a funeral, in a hospital waiting room, on a hilltop. The question is what happens the next Tuesday.

The Rebbe's honesty is striking. He names two ways the fire dies. One is complacency: "I've learned this already, I don't need the feeling." The other is despair: "I'm too far away, nothing is left." And he answers both. To the complacent: even on a Shabbat of the soul, the fire is needed. To the despairing: a spark always remains.

A practical way for a modern family to begin: pick one small daily fuel. A few words of prayer in the morning, a line of Torah at night, one kind phone call, a coin in the charity box before dinner. Nothing is too small, because the point is continuity, not size.

And fire is not only for ourselves. A fire is warm for everyone in the room. In Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda there are families who would love to feel that warmth but do not know where to start. A visible fire is how we invite them.

## Common questions

### What is Parshat Tzav about?
Parshat Tzav is about the laws of the offerings from the priests' point of view and the seven-day consecration of Aaron and his sons. It includes the command that a fire burn continually on the altar. See [Leviticus](/learn/book-of-leviticus).

### What does the Rebbe teach about the continual fire in Parshat Tzav?
The Rebbe teaches that the altar is the heart, and the continual fire is a love of G-d that is visible and always fed. It guards against both overconfidence and despair, since a spark always remains in the heart.

### What is the main lesson of Parshat Tzav?
The main lesson is to keep a daily fire of warmth in Torah, prayer and good deeds. [Prayer](/learn/prayer) and [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah) are two simple ways to feed it.
