## The Rebbe's teaching

Only someone who truly loves another person should point out that person's faults: this is the Rebbe's lesson from the laws of tzaraat.

Tazria describes tzaraat, a spiritual affliction that appeared on the skin. A qualified scholar examined it, but a person became impure only when a kohen declared him so. The Rebbe asks two questions: why is the kohen's declaration decisive, and why does this unusual rule apply to tzaraat?

His answer starts by explaining how serious the status was. A person with tzaraat was separated from the community, sent "outside the camp," even apart from others who were impure. Such a heavy judgment should be given only by someone who feels its full weight. The kohen's role is to bless the people with love, so he is a "man of kindness." Because of that nature, the Torah entrusts him with the decision. And a kohen who had to pronounce such a ruling would work hard to help the person become pure again.

The scholar's knowledge ensures the diagnosis is correct; the kohen's compassion governs the decision to separate someone.

For us, the Rebbe draws these lessons:

- Do not judge hastily. When you see a serious flaw in another person, do not rush to condemn or push them away.
- First check your own heart: do you truly love that person? Without love, judgment comes from your own flaws, not from sound reasoning.
- Declaring another person cut off, without love, is a false judgment, and it is a kind of harmful speech that can cause its own damage.
- Unconditional love heals division and brings the world closer to redemption.

## A story

The Chafetz Chaim, Rabbi Yisrael Meir Kagan (known by the title of his book on the laws of forbidden speech) lived in Radin, Poland. Rabbi Mendel Weinbach of Ohr Somayach retold this story.

A Torah scholar once visited the Chafetz Chaim at his home. The sage was in his mid-eighties and frail, yet he was in unusually good spirits. He asked his guest to come close and open his mouth so he could count his teeth. The guest hesitated, but the sage insisted. The visitor counted, and found that the elderly sage had a full set of teeth, in excellent condition.

The Chafetz Chaim explained, in a sentence the story is remembered for: "I guarded what came out of my mouth, and G-d guarded my mouth in return."

The story is a gentle illustration of a lifetime of care with words. A man who spent his life teaching the laws of speech lived what he taught, and the story remembers him for it.

## Ideas for the Shabbat table

- Why might the Torah have trusted someone known for kindness to make the hardest decision?
- Children: if a friend did something wrong, how could you tell them in a kind way?
- Adults: before you speak about someone, can you ask, "Am I saying this out of love?"
- The Chafetz Chaim guarded his speech all his life. What is one small habit that could help you guard yours?
- When was a time someone corrected you kindly, and it helped? What made it work?
- Try to say one good thing about each person at the table.

## An essay

We live in an age of instant judgment. Someone says something online, and within minutes thousands of strangers have decided who they are. Tazria, with its strange laws of skin and priests, speaks directly to this.

The Rebbe asks why a kohen was the only one who could declare a person impure. The answer is moving: because that judgment can only be made by someone who loves. A person who truly cares about others will hesitate to condemn them. He will feel the weight of every word. And if he must speak, he will do everything he can to bring the other person back.

This is a high standard, but it is not impossible. It begins with a simple question we can ask in the middle of a conversation: "Why am I about to say this?" If the honest answer is "because I care about them and want to help," we can speak, and gently. If the honest answer is "because it feels good to be right" or "because I'm annoyed," the Rebbe suggests we pause and look inward first.

The Chafetz Chaim lived this way, and his story is a quiet testimony. He did not simply avoid gossip; he gathered the laws of speech into a book for everyone. The Rebbe adds a promise for us: love heals what judgment divides.

For a family, this might mean a rule at the Shabbat table: no talking about people who are not present, unless it is to say something good. Children learn it quickly, and everyone relaxes. A table where no one is being judged is a table where everyone can be themselves.

## Common questions

### What is Parshat Tazria about?
Parshat Tazria is about the laws of purity after childbirth and the laws of tzaraat, a spiritual skin affliction examined by the kohen. See [Leviticus](/learn/book-of-leviticus) for the setting.

### What does the Rebbe teach about Parshat Tazria?
The Rebbe teaches that only a kohen, a "man of kindness," could declare a person impure with tzaraat, because such a serious judgment should come only from someone who loves. We should not judge others hastily, and should first check our own hearts.

### What is the main lesson of Parshat Tazria?
The main lesson is to judge others with love and to guard our speech. Read more about [forbidden speech](/learn/lashon-hara) and [judging others favorably](/learn/judging-others-favorably).
