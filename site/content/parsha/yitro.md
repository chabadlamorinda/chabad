## The Rebbe's teaching

The Rebbe teaches that the Giving of the Torah is not only an event in the past: we thank G-d as the Giver of the Torah in the present tense, and we bring it down into our daily life in three steps.

The Rebbe begins with a question. The Torah portion that tells of the Giving of the Torah is named after Yitro, not Moses or Sinai. The Sages say it was "shameful" that Moses and the 600,000 did not say "Blessed is G-d" until Yitro did, even though they had already sung at the sea. Why did Yitro's declaration matter so much? And why does Rashi say that what brought Yitro to the desert was the splitting of the sea and the war with Amalek?

His answer: Yitro was a leader in Midian and a priest of idols who knew every false deity, and his wisdom was huge. His mistake was giving independent power to spiritual intermediaries. When he declared that G-d is greater than all the gods, he refined that knowledge and drew a higher light into holy wisdom. The Torah could only be given once heavenly and earthly realms were joined, and this was the missing link. The splitting of the sea showed that heaven and earth can meet. The war with Amalek showed that evil's force had been restrained but not transformed, as when a person's desires go quiet in prayer and awaken afterward. Yitro completed the union. The word for "blessed" suggests drawing down. The song at the sea raised G-dliness up, but Yitro's blessing drew it down to the lowest levels.

What it means for us is a three-step pattern for each day. First, "the splitting of the sea": on waking, acknowledge G-d, and then immerse yourself in prayer and Torah study. Second, "the war with Amalek": bring holiness into ordinary life, knowing G-d in all your ways, even in areas far from holiness. Third, "Yitro's blessing": refine the material world itself, so that G-dliness fills it and the Torah is, in a way, given anew.



## A story

Rabbi Hillel Zaltzman tells this account in his memoir *Samarkand*, about growing up in Samarkand and Tashkent in the Soviet Union. In that time most parents expected their children to go to Soviet schools and university to earn a profession. His father refused. He believed Soviet public schools aimed to instill Marxist-Leninist ideology, so he kept his children out. He taught basic reading, writing and arithmetic at home, and relied on Talmud and Chassidus for deeper learning. The author's brother Berel learned the trade of making commercial signs, and later the father opened his own sign factory. The family earned its living that way until they left Russia in 1971, and many families in their community did the same.

Rabbi Zaltzman recalls the teacher, Rabbi Bentcha, and two incidents. In one, a classmate boasted that his mother had enrolled him in school and mocked the narrator's friends. Rabbi Bentcha overheard and questioned the boy about a verse from Deuteronomy. When the boy gave the plain meaning, Rabbi Bentcha asked for a deeper reading: the verse meant following only G-d, and not the Communist school. The boy understood, and later raised a devout family.

In the other, during a Hebrew writing lesson, the narrator refused to let a friend, Mordechai Goldschmidt, use his inkwell. Rabbi Bentcha noticed and later asked whether he had said Modeh Ani that morning. After the boy's attempt, the teacher explained that *modeh* means selflessness, and that sharing ink and a pen reflects that value.

Rabbi Zaltzman writes that these lessons, which shaped his teachers' method, stayed with him and his peers for life.

A teacher could turn a simple inkwell into a lesson in Torah.

## Ideas for the Shabbat table

- Ask: where did we "bring Torah down" into ordinary life this week, such as a kind word at work or a blessing before a meal?
- Ask the children: if sharing a pen can be a mitzvah, what other ordinary things become holy when we do them with care?
- Discuss: the Rebbe's three steps (prayer and study, holiness in daily life, refining the world). Which one is easiest for you? Which is hardest?
- Talk about Yitro: a man who knew everything about idols became the one whose declaration helped allow the Torah to be given. Can someone with a very different background help us grow?
- Adults: what could you bring from your work, your knowledge or your skills into a holy purpose?
- Everyone: say Modeh Ani together and discuss what "modeh," thankful and selfless, means for tomorrow.

## An essay

We often picture the Torah as something that happened once, at a mountain, to our ancestors. The Rebbe's teaching on Yitro invites us to see it as something that is given to us, in the present, every day.

The way he describes it is surprisingly practical. First comes the quiet foundation: waking up and thanking G-d, praying, learning. Second comes the part that tests us, bringing that holiness into ordinary life: how we talk to a colleague, handle money, drive, and raise children. Third, and most ambitious, is to refine the world itself, so that G-dliness fills even the places that seem far from it.

That is why mitzvot use physical things. A story from Samarkand shows it beautifully. A teacher in the Soviet Union used a shared pen and inkwell to teach what "thankful" really means. The ordinary became holy because a teacher cared enough to connect the two.

Yitro's example is encouraging. He had explored every idea, and his knowledge was not wasted. When he declared that G-d is greater than all, that knowledge was lifted into holiness. Whatever we know, whether business, science, art or cooking, is something we can bring to the service of G-d.

You do not need to change your whole life this week. Pick one step from the three: a moment of thanks when you wake, one holy intention in the middle of your workday, or one small act of sharing that turns a normal moment into a mitzvah.

## Common questions

### What is Parshat Yitro about?
Parshat Yitro (Exodus 18:1-20:23) tells how Moses' father-in-law Yitro joins the Israelites and advises him to appoint judges, and how G-d gives the Ten Commandments at Mount Sinai. Read more about [the Ten Commandments](/learn/ten-commandments) and [the book of Exodus](/learn/book-of-exodus).

### What does the Rebbe teach about Parshat Yitro?
The Rebbe teaches that the Giving of the Torah is renewed every day, and that we can bring it into our lives by praying and learning, bringing holiness into daily life, and refining the material world. See [why we do mitzvot](/learn/why-mitzvot).

### Why is the portion about the Ten Commandments named after Yitro?
The Rebbe explains that Yitro's declaration that G-d is greater than all gods drew G-dliness down into the lowest levels, which was needed for the Torah to be given. Find this week's portion on the [parsha page](/parsha).
