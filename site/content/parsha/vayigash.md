## The Rebbe's teaching

The Rebbe teaches that tears can ease our pain, but they do not build anything, so when the work is our own, we must act and not only sigh.

The Rebbe begins with the moment Joseph and Benjamin meet again and fall on each other's necks, weeping. Our Sages say that Joseph wept for the two Holy Temples that would stand in Benjamin's land and be destroyed, and Benjamin wept for the Tabernacle at Shiloh that would stand in Joseph's land and be destroyed. The Rebbe asks: why the neck, and not the head? Why did each brother weep over the other's sanctuary and not his own? And why did Jacob not weep at all, but recite the Shema?

His answer begins with the neck. The head is higher, but the neck does the connecting, carrying life and thought from the brain to the body. The Temple is like that: its purpose is to bring Heaven's light down into daily life, and each of us does that through the practical work of refining our own conduct.

Why weep for the other's sanctuary? When someone else's world is in ruins, tears, kind words and prayer are what we can give. His return depends on his own choice. But for our own sanctuary, our own home, character and Jewish life, sighing is not enough. Tears can even become a trap, because they let us feel we have already done our part. Jacob, who held both sanctuaries within his family, did not cry. He said the Shema, giving himself fully to G-d, and so was actively building.

The Rebbe adds that despair is never the answer. When King Hezekiah was told he would die, he prayed, and fifteen years were added to his life. 

What it means for us: it is natural to feel pain about the world or about another person. Let it move you to pray and to help. And when something is yours to fix, a relationship, a habit, a home with little Jewish life in it, do one concrete thing.

## A story

This true account comes from the diary of Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn, the sixth Lubavitcher Rebbe, who describes traveling by coach with his father, the fifth Rebbe, Rabbi Sholom DovBer.

On the road to an estate called Serebrinka, they passed two chassidim resting by the roadside with their eyes closed. They were Reb Peretz and Reb Menachem Mendel, schoolteachers from Beshenkovitz. Reb Peretz was reciting a chassidic discourse aloud and Reb Menachem Mendel was listening. They never noticed the coach go by. The Rebbe explained that they were reviewing a discourse he had delivered on Shavuot. The two men had walked to Lubavitch for thirty-three years, since 1871, and walked home again after the holiday.

When the coach reached the village of Rudnia, local rabbis, householders, three teachers and about fifty students came out to greet the Rebbe. He blessed them and spoke at length. He said the very land had been waiting for those two men to sit and learn Torah there, and he praised chassidic schoolteachers as the "Abrahams" of their generations, who bring G-dliness into Jewish homes. His father, he added, valued schoolteachers even above rabbis.

The diary ends quietly. At Serebrinka, the blacksmith, Reb Gershon, served the travelers soup and milk.

Two schoolteachers walked to Lubavitch year after year, to keep learning so that they could keep teaching.

## Ideas for the Shabbat table

- Ask: is there something in the news or in our community that makes us want to cry? What could we do about it, and not only feel?
- Ask the children: what is the difference between feeling sorry for a friend and helping a friend? Can you do both?
- Talk about the neck: who in our family, school or community is a "neck," connecting the big ideas to everyday life?
- Discuss: Jacob said the Shema instead of weeping. What do you say or do when you are upset?
- Ask everyone to name one teacher who made a difference for them, and what that teacher did.
- Adults: is there one small concrete step this week, such as a class, a call or a mitzvah, that would turn a worry into action?

## An essay

Every family and every community has moments when we feel the weight of what is broken. A friend is struggling. A grandchild knows nothing about Jewish life. A world that seems to be coming apart. The first response is tears, and tears are good. They show that we care.

The Rebbe teaches that tears are a beginning and not an ending. They ease our pain, but a tear has never repaired a roof. When the broken thing is in someone else's life, we can pray for him, speak kindly and gently, and be there. We cannot choose for him. But when the broken thing is ours, our time for learning, our Shabbat, our patience at home, we are asked to roll up our sleeves.

There is a gentle warning in this. It is easy to feel moved, to sigh, to share a sad post, and to feel that we have done something. The Rebbe notes that tears can trick us in just this way. A real answer is smaller and harder: one class attended, one call made, one candle lit.

There is also a hopeful side. The Rebbe teaches that we should never despair, because a person's prayer and effort can change even a decree that seems final. The two schoolteachers from Beshenkovitz did not wait for perfect conditions. They walked, they learned, they taught, and a Rebbe said the land itself had been waiting for them.

Our home, our children's education and our community are our own sanctuary to build. In Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda, a Jewish home grows one concrete step at a time, and every step counts. Start this week with one small act, and let your sadness become the fuel for it.

## Common questions

### What is Parshat Vayigash about?
Parshat Vayigash (Genesis 44:18-47:27) tells how Judah steps forward to plead for Benjamin, Joseph reveals himself to his brothers, and Jacob and his family come down to Egypt. Read more in [Joseph and his brothers](/learn/joseph-and-his-brothers).

### What does the Rebbe teach about Parshat Vayigash?
The Rebbe teaches that crying can ease our pain but does not repair anything, so where the work is our own, we must act. Jacob, who recited the Shema instead of weeping, shows us how. See also our guide to [forgiveness and making peace](/learn/forgiveness-and-making-peace).

### What is the main lesson of Parshat Vayigash?
The main lesson is to turn feeling into action, with prayer, kindness and one concrete step. You can find this week's portion on the [parsha page](/parsha), and ideas for study in [daily Torah study](/learn/daily-torah-study).
