## The Rebbe's teaching

The Rebbe teaches that the two names of our forefather, Jacob and Israel, describe two ways of serving G-d, and that every Jew needs both: the careful work of weekdays and the open joy of Shabbat.

The question is simple. G-d gave Jacob a new name, Israel, yet the Torah keeps calling him Jacob afterward. With Abraham, the Sages say, the Torah does not go back to the old name. Why is Jacob different?

The Rebbe explains, drawing on Chassidic teaching, that each name is a way of living. "Jacob" recalls how Jacob received Isaac's blessings: with cleverness and disguise. In Chassidic terms, it is the way of working inside the ordinary world while keeping a hidden, holy purpose. Outwardly we eat, work and do what everyone does, but inwardly we are drawing out the sparks of holiness hidden in physical things. "Israel" is a higher and more open way, where physical life already reveals holiness. Eating a Shabbat meal is itself a mitzvah, not a disguised means to something else.

The two names also differ in how much struggle they involve. Jacob must still contend with his evil inclination and refine his animal soul. Israel has already won, and his service is about rising higher within holiness. The Rebbe adds that for an ordinary Jew, "Jacob" corresponds to the weekday and "Israel" to Shabbat. Even the greatest people have both, and every Jew keeps the Jacob mode, which is why the Torah keeps the name.

The Rebbe draws practical points. On weekdays, approach eating, work and everyday matters with an inner purpose, doing them for the sake of Heaven. On Shabbat, enjoy physical life openly and recognize the holiness in it. And serve with joy rather than fear: like a soldier who marches toward danger in a cheerful spirit, joy lightens the task and helps us succeed. The Rebbe applies this to the moment Shabbat ends: a Jew need not dread returning to the work of the week, because strength and gladness come with it.

## A story

Rabbi Menachem Posner tells this story, which he heard from his father-in-law, Rabbi Nachman Levine, who heard it from his own father-in-law, Yitzchok Waxman, the youngest son of the man at its center. It is presented as a true account handed down in the family.

In the early twentieth century, life was hard in the village of Chernestra in Ukraine. Yisroel Dov Waxman, known as Berel, left his wife Rochel and their growing family to look for work in America, hoping to send money home and bring them over later. Before he boarded the train to the port, his father, Meshulem Zushia, asked him to swear never to work on Shabbat. Berel, a devout chassid, was startled that his father would even ask, but he gave his word.

In New York, a fellow villager helped him find work pressing shirts in a Lower East Side sweatshop, from early morning to late at night, six days a week. As autumn shortened the days, he realized he would need to leave early on Fridays. When he told his boss, the man threw his ironing board into the street and fired him.

Berel left the board at a nearby laundry and went to a synagogue, and spent a lonely Shabbat with no income, but strong faith. After Shabbat, the laundry attendant returned the board without question. Soon a stranger offered him overtime pressing work. At the end of the week his envelope held far more than he had ever earned. Only then did he realize that his former boss had been paying him a fraction of his worth.

Berel later told his children that had he worked on Shabbat, he would have lost not only his share in the World to Come but his fair portion in this world too. A few years later, he brought his wife and children to New York. A century later, his descendants still keep Shabbat. The family also tells one variant: that his father asked him instead to put on tefillin every day before work.

## Ideas for the Shabbat table

- Ask the children: Jacob had two names. Do you have a nickname or two names? Do you act differently with your friends and with your family?
- What is something you do during the week that is "ordinary" but can be done for a good purpose, such as eating healthy food to have strength to help others?
- How is Shabbat different from a weekday in our house? Is there something we can enjoy more openly on Shabbat?
- Berel kept a promise to his father even when it cost him his job. Have you ever kept a commitment when it was hard? What helped?
- For adults: the Rebbe says to serve with joy rather than fear. What is one task this week you could approach with more gladness?
- Read about [Jacob our father](/learn/jacob-our-father) and talk about the moment he faces his brother.

## An essay

There is a particular kind of tiredness that comes from being two people. At work we are one person, at home another, and at synagogue another. We change our voice, our humor, even our clothes. Many of us know this feeling, especially when our Jewish life is only a small part of the day.

The Rebbe's reading of Jacob and Israel makes it less lonely. It says that having two modes is not a failure. It is the Torah's own description of a Jewish life. The weekday way, Jacob, is about being in the world with a hidden purpose: eating to have strength, working to be able to give, doing everything for Heaven even if it looks ordinary. The Shabbat way, Israel, is about enjoying the world openly, with candles, wine, a meal, and knowing that this too is holiness.

Berel's story shows how serious that weekday purpose can be. He had made a promise to his father, and he held to it in the most uncomfortable place: a sweatshop, with a family far away. He did not know that the story would end with a better job. Nor does the Rebbe promise that. But his story, remembered a century later, reminds us that one Shabbat kept in difficult circumstances can echo for generations.

And the Rebbe's final point is warm: do it with joy. A soldier who goes to meet danger cheerfully already has an advantage. Returning to the work of the week after Shabbat does not have to feel like a drop. It can feel like a mission.

## Common questions

### What is Parshat Vayishlach about?
Parshat Vayishlach tells how Jacob prepares to meet his brother Esau, wrestles with an angel and receives the name Israel. It also includes the story of Dinah and Rachel's passing. See [Jacob our father](/learn/jacob-our-father).

### What does the Rebbe teach about Parshat Vayishlach?
The Rebbe teaches that the two names Jacob and Israel are two ways of serving G-d. Jacob is the careful work of the weekday and Israel the open joy of Shabbat, and every Jew needs both, serving with joy rather than fear.

### What is the main lesson of Parshat Vayishlach?
We can live and work in any environment while keeping our Jewish identity whole. Jacob told Esau that he had lived with Laban and still kept the commandments. To read more about peace between brothers, see [forgiveness and making peace](/learn/forgiveness-and-making-peace).
