## The Rebbe's teaching

Knowing the fine line between clean and unclean takes a connection to something higher than ourselves, and that is the Rebbe's explanation for why kosher laws come in a portion called "Eighth."

Parshat Shmini ends with the instruction "to distinguish between the unclean and the clean." The Rebbe asks why a portion whose name, "Eighth," points to the loftiest level of G-dliness finishes with laws that seem so down-to-earth. He notes how fine the difference can be: sever exactly half of an animal's windpipe and it is not kosher; sever more and it is.

His answer begins with the numbers. Seven stands for creation and the G-dliness within it. Eight stands for a level above creation, which belongs to G-d Himself. And because the distinction between clean and unclean can be so small, it is easy to mistake one for the other. Only a person connected to that transcendent level can see it clearly.

The Rebbe describes how the evil inclination argues. Someone aware only of the G-dliness inside the world can be talked into leniency: "the animal has a divine spark that should be elevated, it is wasteful to throw out food over a tiny doubt." But a person sensitive to the transcendent will not deviate from the Divine will even a little. That wish to be holy goes beyond intellectual argument and is stronger than the doubts.

The lesson is not about specific rules. It is that when a fine point of Jewish law leaves real doubt, we should not rationalize toward the easier way. Instead, the inner drive to be close to G-d guides us. For real questions in our own kitchen, we ask the Rabbi.

## A story

A well-known story; its details cannot be confirmed.

In Frankfurt, Rabbi Pinchas Horowitz (1730-1805) was approached by the neighborhood butcher. A slaughtered ox had a defect in its lung, and the case was borderline. Several earlier authorities would have forbidden the meat. The rabbi spent many hours studying their rulings, and then declared the ox kosher.

A disciple asked why he had gone to such lengths. The great Shach, one of the major halachic authorities, considered such meat forbidden. Wouldn't it be safer to discard it?

The rabbi explained how he imagined the heavenly court. If he ruled leniently, the Shach would be the star witness against him. His defense would be that lesser authorities had ruled the ox kosher, and he had followed them. That prospect troubled him.

But imagine the opposite, he said. If he had thrown out the meat, the ox itself would testify: it could have fed hungry people and supported Torah study and good deeds, and he had discarded it when grounds for kashrut existed. He said he would "rather take my chances against the Shach than confront an angry ox in court."

The rabbi had weighed both sides carefully and with a sense of responsibility. Kosher law is not about being strict or lenient for its own sake; it takes both knowledge and a serious heart.

## Ideas for the Shabbat table

- The Torah teaches us to "distinguish" between clean and unclean. Why might eating be one of the first places where the Torah asks us to pay attention?
- Children: what is a rule at home you may not understand but that your parents keep for a reason?
- What is one thing you could eat more mindfully this week, with a blessing before and after?
- The Rebbe says only a connection to something higher helps us see fine differences. When has a small difference mattered a lot?
- Adults: do you ever argue yourself into an easier choice? What would it take to pause first?
- If you want to start keeping kosher, what is one small step you could take? (See the guide below.)

## An essay

Most of what we do in a day involves eating, and most of it we do on autopilot. Parshat Shmini is a gentle interruption. It says: even this, the most ordinary act, is a place where we can choose.

The kosher laws are not only a list of what is forbidden. In the Rebbe's reading, they are also about attention. To keep kosher is to ask, every time you reach for food, "Is this something I should take into myself?" That question trains a muscle. The person who practices it at the table finds it easier to use in a business meeting, in a conversation, in an email.

The Rebbe also gives a reason why this requires something beyond logic. If we only reasoned, we could always find a clever case for the easier choice. The number eight, a level above nature, reminds us that holiness is not only about what makes sense to us. It is also about wanting to be close.

This is why Chassidic teaching says the very act of eating can be holy. We receive our energy from food. If the food is kosher, and blessed, that energy goes into good.

Does this feel like too much? It need not be done all at once. Many people begin with one meal, one blessing, one item. The story of the rabbi and the ox is a reminder that serious people weigh questions with both caution and compassion. For your own kitchen, call the Rabbi.

## Common questions

### What is Parshat Shmini about?
Parshat Shmini is about the eighth day of the Sanctuary's dedication, the tragic death of Aaron's sons Nadav and Avihu, and the laws of kosher animals. See [kosher basics](/learn/kosher) and [Aaron](/learn/aaron-the-high-priest).

### What does the Rebbe teach about the kosher laws in Parshat Shmini?
The Rebbe teaches that the difference between clean and unclean can be tiny, and that only a connection to the transcendent level of "eight" gives the clarity to distinguish them. When in real doubt, we should not rationalize toward leniency.

### What is the main lesson of Parshat Shmini?
The main lesson is that even eating can be holy, if done with attention and a desire to be close to G-d. You can start with [keeping kosher one step at a time](/learn/keeping-kosher-one-step-at-a-time).
