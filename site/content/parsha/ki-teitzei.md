## The Rebbe's teaching

The Rebbe taught that the war in this portion is the inner battle against our own negative side, and that we enter it already holding the upper hand, because the soul comes from G-d.

The portion opens, "When you go out to war against your enemies." The Rebbe noticed an unusual phrase: the Torah says "go out to war," not simply "go to war." War is not the natural home of the Jewish soul. Before it entered the body, the soul knew the infinite Divine presence, where evil has no power. So when the soul "goes out" into the world of struggle, it is leaving its natural state, and it is not meant to feel at home there.

The Rebbe also pointed to the wording "against your enemies," which can be read "above your enemies." The soul's source in G-d's essence gives it the upper hand over evil. The Rebbe added that the inner struggle is fought mainly in daily prayer, and that Torah study can remove negativity more peacefully, almost automatically. When evil can be removed through study, choosing to fight it directly in prayer is an "optional war."

What this means for us: when we struggle with anger, laziness or temptation, we are not fighting from a position of weakness. The struggle is real, but the soul within us is stronger than the impulse. We do not have to be perfect to begin; we need to decide to go out and engage, and trust that we have been given the strength.

Ki Teitzei is usually read during Elul, the month for taking stock and returning, so the message arrives at the right time. See our guide to [the evil inclination](/learn/yetzer-hara) and [the two souls](/learn/the-two-souls-explained).

## A story

The Talmud tells about Rabbi Chiya and his wife (Yevamot 63a).

Rabbi Chiya's wife mistreated him. Yet he still brought her gifts. Rav asked him: why do you put up with her?

Rabbi Chiya answered that it is enough for him that she raises their children and keeps him from improper thoughts.

The story is often quoted when the portion speaks of marriage, and it teaches gratitude. Rabbi Chiya did not pretend his marriage was perfect. But he chose to keep his eyes on what his wife did give him, and that choice turned a hard situation into a relationship in which he still gave.

A caution: this is a short Talmudic account, and it is not advice for any particular marriage. If you or someone you know is in a difficult marriage, please speak privately with the Rabbi or Rebbetzin (call 925-384-7242). The lesson of the story is gratitude for the good that we do receive.

## Ideas for the Shabbat table

- Ask everyone: what is one person in our life we have not thanked in a while, and what could we say?
- For children: Ki Teitzei tells us that if we see a friend's lost item, we must return it. Has anything been returned to you, or have you returned something? How did it feel? See [returning lost items](/learn/returning-lost-items).
- Adults: The portion has more mitzvot than any other, and many are about everyday decency (paying a worker on time, honest weights). Which one speaks to you this year?
- Discuss: the Rebbe says the soul is "above" the enemy. What does it change to know that you start from strength?
- Talk about it: the Torah says to pay a worker on the same day. What does that teach about how we treat people who depend on us? See [paying workers on time](/learn/paying-workers-on-time).
- Try this: at the end of the meal, each person names one thing they are grateful for about the person on their right.

## An essay

Ki Teitzei is the portion that holds the most commandments, seventy-four, and almost none of them is about spectacular events. They are about ordinary life: returning a lost coat, building a fence around a flat roof so nobody falls, paying wages on time, leaving a forgotten sheaf of grain in the field for the poor.

It is a reminder that holiness lives in the details. And the details are exactly where our inner battle takes place. Nobody is tempted to steal the Torah scroll. We are tempted to ignore a neighbor's lost item because we are busy, to speak sharply because we are tired, to postpone the call we promised to make.

The Rebbe's teaching gives us a hopeful way to understand these small struggles. We do not "go to war" like someone who has no home but the battlefield. We "go out" to war, from a place of peace and Divine strength. We enter the struggle already assured of the soul's upper hand. That changes how we feel about failing. A stumble is not proof that we are weak, only a reminder that the battle is ongoing and that we are able to return to it.

The story of Rabbi Chiya adds another dimension. Sometimes the hardest battle is not with a temptation but with resentment, the habit of seeing only what is missing in the people closest to us. The Talmud's answer is a quiet one: look for what they do give, and say thank you for it.

As Elul unfolds, we can ask ourselves two small questions each day. What is one thing I will do better, with the strength I already have? And for whom am I grateful today?

## Common questions

### What is Parshat Ki Teitzei about?

Parshat Ki Teitzei (Deuteronomy 21:10-25:19) contains seventy-four commandments, including returning lost property, sending away the mother bird, building a roof fence, paying workers on time, honest weights, and remembering Amalek.

### What does the Rebbe teach about Parshat Ki Teitzei?

The Rebbe teaches that "go out to war" describes the inner struggle against our negative inclinations, which we enter with the soul already holding the upper hand, since the soul is a part of G-d.

### What is the main lesson of Parshat Ki Teitzei?

Holiness is found in everyday decency, and every person has the strength to win the inner battle because G-d gives the strength. See [the weekly parsha](/learn/weekly-parsha) for how to study each portion.
