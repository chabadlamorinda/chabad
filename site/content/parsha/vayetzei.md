## The Rebbe's teaching

The Rebbe teaches that people who spend their weeks in business and everyday work are not "second class" next to scholars, because turning the physical world into a dwelling for G-d is the very purpose of creation.

The Rebbe begins with the name Zevulun, given by Leah, who hoped her husband would "dwell with me." Zevulun's tribe was known for commerce, and his brother Yissachar for Torah study. Why would Zevulun's character shape where Jacob's home would be? And how could Jacob, the man of the "tents of Torah," have built the tribes of Israel during twenty years as a shepherd in Laban's house in Charan?

The Rebbe's answer is that G-d "desired a dwelling in the lower realms." His dwelling is not only in the spiritual worlds but here, in the physical world. Zevulun's work of using worldly things for a heavenly purpose and doing mitzvot with physical objects makes the world a home for G-d. This is why, in the blessings of Jacob and of Moses, Zevulun is mentioned before Yissachar.

But the work of refinement is temporary, while a dwelling should be permanent. The Rebbe explains that permanence comes from what is eternal: the Jewish soul and the Torah. A Jew who is immersed in worldly matters and still studies Torah and keeps mitzvot reveals the soul's essential G-dly power. The Rebbe compares this to a person who has overcome a struggle: he reveals a strength that someone who never faced the test has not shown.

The practical lesson is directly for working people. Most Jews today, the Rebbe says, are in effect Zevulun, and they should not think they are secondary to scholars. They should set fixed times for Torah study and make it a permanent part of their character. On Shabbat, free from commerce, they should give themselves to unhurried prayer. Chassidic teaching explains that businesspeople can pray with greater strength than scholars, because light comes out of darkness. A person who has struggled all week to earn a living can arouse a deeper love for G-d in prayer.

For you, this means that your workweek is not a distraction from your Jewish life. It can be its foundation.

## A story

Don Wolf tells this story in "Here's My Story" on TheRebbe.org. He was born in Melbourne, Australia, to Polish Holocaust survivors who arrived in 1949.

In 1972 he came to New York hoping to continue his studies at Chabad headquarters. At his first private audience, the Rebbe surprised him by advising him to return to Melbourne and, by the summer, to talk with his teachers and father about when he would join the family business. Don was stunned.

Two years later, before his wedding, Don told the Rebbe he felt unfulfilled in his father's building business and asked about a job at the Yeshivah Centre. The Rebbe said that was not for him. He encouraged Don to set times for Torah study, but urged him to stay in business. "The main thing," the Rebbe said, "is for you to make sure that your Torah study is fixed in your soul." The Rebbe explained that a businessman doing outreach is received differently than a rabbi, and added: "In your current occupation, you can achieve in one hour what another person may not achieve in twenty-four hours."

Don says this guidance shaped his life. For more than 35 years he balanced his business with volunteer service on the boards of Chabad institutions, always asking how Torah affects his family and the people around him.

## Ideas for the Shabbat table

- Ask the children: Jacob worked for many years as a shepherd. What do you think he learned from taking care of sheep? What do the grown-ups in our family do all day?
- Let each adult share something about their work that they are proud of, and one thing that is hard about it.
- Jacob dreamed of a ladder with its feet on the ground and its top in heaven. Where in your week do you feel your feet on the ground? Where do you feel closer to heaven?
- The Rebbe suggests fixed times for Torah study, even for the busiest. What small, fixed time could we choose together, even ten minutes?
- Shabbat is a time free from commerce. What is one thing we could stop doing during Shabbat to feel more present?
- Read about [Tanya at work](/learn/tanya-at-work) and [livelihood](/learn/parnassah-work-and-livelihood), and talk about one idea you liked.

## An essay

Many people feel a quiet gap between their Jewish life and their working life. Monday to Friday we are emailing, managing and calculating, and on Shabbat we are supposed to be "Jewish." It can feel as though one life is the real one and the other is a necessary distraction.

Parshat Vayetzei looks at the same gap in the life of Jacob. He leaves home with a blessing and a dream of angels, and he lands in Laban's house, where he spends twenty years working hard for a difficult employer. Yet it is there that he builds his family, the tribes of Israel. The Rebbe's teaching sees this as no accident. G-d wants a home in the lower world, and Jacob builds it in a field, with a flock, under a hard boss.

This means that the office, the shop, the clinic or the classroom is not where holiness stops. It is where it is meant to enter. When we are honest in a contract, kind to a coworker who is struggling or careful with the way we spend our time, we are making the world a home for G-d. The Rebbe told Don Wolf that a businessman who reaches out to other Jews is received differently from a rabbi, and can do in an hour what another person may not do in a day. That is not flattery. It is a way of saying that being in the world gives a person access that others do not have.

The Rebbe also asks that this Jewish life have a fixed anchor: a regular time for Torah and a Shabbat of real prayer and rest. A week without an anchor drifts. A week with even a small fixed time for learning has a center.

## Common questions

### What is Parshat Vayetzei about?
Parshat Vayetzei tells how Jacob leaves Beer Sheba, dreams of a ladder with angels, works for Laban in Charan and marries Leah and Rachel. His children are born and he returns toward Canaan. See [Jacob our father](/learn/jacob-our-father).

### What does the Rebbe teach about Parshat Vayetzei?
The Rebbe teaches that Jacob's years in Laban's house show that the physical world can become a dwelling for G-d, and that people in business should not consider themselves secondary to scholars. He encourages fixed times for Torah study and unhurried prayer on Shabbat.

### What is the main lesson of Parshat Vayetzei?
Wherever life takes us, the office, the classroom or a new city, we can bring heaven down to earth. Jacob stayed true to himself in Haran, far from holiness. For more, read [Tanya at work](/learn/tanya-at-work).
