Yes, you can keep kosher one step at a time. Chabad.org teaches that taking small steps is a sound approach, not a compromise, and gives a simple plan in three stages: kosher meat and no mixing of meat and dairy, then kosher products and separate dishes, then a fully kosher kitchen with a rabbi's help.

## Is gradual growth a compromise?

A reader wrote to Chabad.org that the full change felt overwhelming. Rabbi Yisroel Cotlar answered that dividing a mitzvah into stages makes it more achievable and builds stability. Learning a language or math begins with the basics, and this is not a failure to reach the goal but the way to it. Every mitzvah, whether keeping kosher, putting on tefillin or keeping Shabbat, is a meaningful connection with G-d in its own right.

He also notes that some shortcuts are real compromises: avoiding pork and shellfish is not the same as keeping kosher.

## Stage one: kosher meat, and no meat with dairy

Buy only kosher meat, and stop eating meat and dairy together. If you live in Lafayette, Moraga or Orinda, [Bay Kosher](/programs/bay-kosher) is a project of Chabad of Lamorinda. See also [kosher meat](/learn/kosher-meat) and [meat and milk](/learn/meat-and-milk).

## Stage two: kosher products and separate dishes

Buy only products with reliable kosher certification, which are more common than many people expect. See [kosher symbols](/learn/kosher-symbols) and [kosher shopping at a regular supermarket](/learn/kosher-shopping-at-the-supermarket). Separate your pots, pans and cutlery into meat and dairy sets and mark them clearly. This helps prevent mix-ups later.

## Stage three: a kosher kitchen

Invite a rabbi to look at your kitchen. He will advise how to divide the meat and dairy areas, and which utensils can be koshered and which must be replaced. Then the sinks, oven and utensils are koshered, and the kitchen becomes kosher. Read the overview in [how to make your kitchen kosher](/learn/kosher-kitchen). Rabbi Labkowski is happy to help: 925-384-7242.

## When your family is not at the same pace

Chabad.org's Sarah Zadok writes that couples often grow in Jewish observance at different paces, and this is common. She says one may set the rules for what is practiced in one's own kitchen, but cannot require a spouse to feel inspired by them. Her advice includes:

1. Keep your own practices without turning your spouse's choices into a battle.
2. Thank your spouse for specific good qualities, and look for the good first.
3. Be patient and let your example speak, by warmth and joy.
4. Remember that kindness and compassion are Jewish values too.

See also [keeping kosher with family](/learn/keeping-kosher-with-family).

## Common questions

### How do I start keeping kosher?
Start with kosher meat and no mixing of meat and dairy. Then buy only certified products and separate your dishes, then ask a rabbi to help with your kitchen.

### Is it OK to keep kosher gradually?
Yes. Chabad.org says that small steps are not a compromise but the way to build something lasting.

### Is avoiding pork and shellfish enough?
It is a start, but Chabad.org notes that it is not the same as keeping kosher.

### What if my spouse does not want a kosher kitchen?
Keep your own practices peacefully, appreciate your spouse, and be patient. Call the Rabbi if you would like to talk it over.

### Do I need new dishes right away?
Not necessarily. Many things can be koshered, and some need to be replaced. Ask the Rabbi before you throw anything out.
