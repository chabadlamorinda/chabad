Counting the Omer is the mitzvah of counting each of the 49 days from the second night of Passover until Shavuot. Each night, after nightfall, we say a blessing and count the day aloud, building up to the giving of the Torah.

## What is the Omer?

The omer was a measure of barley brought as an offering in the Holy Temple on the second day of Passover. The Torah commands: "You shall count for yourselves, from the day after the day of rest, from the day you bring the omer... seven complete weeks" (Leviticus 23:15). On the 50th day, we celebrate Shavuot.

## Why we count

When the Jewish people left Egypt, they eagerly counted the days until they would receive the Torah at Mount Sinai. Counting shows anticipation, like counting down to a wedding.

Kabbalah and Chassidic teaching explain that the 49 days correspond to seven emotional traits, each made up of all seven: kindness, discipline, harmony, endurance, humility, bonding and leadership. Each day is a chance to refine one combination, such as "kindness within discipline," preparing ourselves to receive the Torah.

## When to count

- Count at night, after nightfall, starting on the second night of Passover.
- If you forget at night, count during the next day without the blessing, and then continue counting with the blessing on the following nights.
- If you miss an entire day (night and the next day), continue counting on the following nights without the blessing.

## How to count, step by step

1. **Stand**, if you can.
2. **Say the blessing** (below).
3. **Count the day**, saying both the days and the weeks. For example: "Today is fifteen days, which are two weeks and one day of the Omer."
4. **Add the short prayer** "May the Merciful One restore the Temple service to its place," and many add Psalm 67 and other verses.

Most prayer books print each day's count in full, in Hebrew and English. Chabad custom is to count after the evening service.

## The blessing

**Hebrew:** בָּרוּךְ אַתָּה ה' אֱלֹקֵינוּ מֶלֶךְ הָעוֹלָם, אֲשֶׁר קִדְּשָׁנוּ בְּמִצְוֹתָיו, וְצִוָּנוּ עַל סְפִירַת הָעוֹמֶר

**Transliteration:** Baruch atah Ado-nai, Elo-heinu melech ha'olam, asher kid'shanu b'mitzvotav, v'tzivanu al sefirat ha'omer.

**Translation:** Blessed are You, L-rd our G-d, King of the universe, who has made us holy with His commandments, and commanded us concerning the counting of the Omer.

## The Omer as a time of mourning

During the Omer, 24,000 students of the great sage Rabbi Akiva died because they did not treat each other with proper respect. In their memory, part of the Omer period is observed with some mourning customs:

- No weddings.
- No haircuts or shaving.
- Many avoid live music and dancing.

Customs differ on which days these apply. Ask the Rabbi about your community's practice.

## Lag BaOmer

The 33rd day of the Omer, Lag BaOmer, is a day of joy. It is the anniversary of the passing of Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai, author of the Zohar, who asked that the day be celebrated. Many hold parades, bonfires and outings. Mourning customs are lifted, and many three-year-old boys have their [upsherin](/learn/upsherin).

## Common questions

### Can I count before nightfall?
The ideal time is after nightfall. If you prayed the evening service early with a congregation, you may count then without a blessing and repeat it after dark. Ask the Rabbi about your situation.

### What if I am not sure whether I counted?
If you are unsure whether you counted on a previous day, you may continue counting with the blessing.

### Do women count the Omer?
Women are not obligated, since it is a time-bound mitzvah. Many women count, and customs differ on whether they say the blessing. Ask the Rabbi.

### Do I need to count the weeks too?
Yes. From the seventh day on, the count includes both the days and the weeks, because the Torah speaks of counting both. The text in the prayer book does this for you.
