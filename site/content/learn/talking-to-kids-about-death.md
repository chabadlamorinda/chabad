When someone a child loves passes away, parents often wonder what to say. You do not need perfect words. Children need honesty, warmth and the sense that they are not alone. Judaism gives us gentle, true ideas to share, and a path of mourning that includes the whole family.

## Use honest, simple words

- **Say "died," not "went to sleep" or "we lost him."** Young children take words literally and may fear sleeping or think the person can be found.
- **Answer the question asked,** briefly, and let them ask more.
- **It is fine to say "I don't know."** Some questions have no simple answer.
- **Let them see you sad.** Crying together teaches that grief is a form of love.

## What Judaism teaches about the soul

Judaism teaches that every person has a [soul](/learn/the-soul), a part of G-d, that lives forever. When a person dies, the body stops working and is buried, but the soul returns to G-d and continues to exist. See [The Afterlife](/learn/afterlife).

For a young child you might say: "Grandpa's body stopped working and he died. His body is buried, but his soul, the part of him that loved you, is with G-d. We will always remember him, and the good things we do can make his soul happy."

## By age

- **Under 6:** short, concrete answers, repeated often. They may ask the same question many times.
- **6 to 10:** they understand death is permanent and may worry about other family members dying. Reassure them about who takes care of them.
- **Preteens and teens:** they can handle more detail and deeper questions. Invite them into the conversation and the mitzvot.

## Funerals and shiva

Whether a child attends the [funeral](/learn/jewish-funeral) depends on the child's age and temperament; many families include older children. Prepare them for what they will see, and have a trusted adult beside them who can step out with them if needed.

During [shiva](/learn/shiva), the week of mourning, children can help greet visitors, light a candle or simply be with family. Shiva surrounds a family with community, and children feel that too. Customs about which children observe mourning practices vary; ask the Rabbi.

## Kaddish and Tehillim

Children can do something for the soul of the person they loved:

- **Answer "Amen"** to the [Kaddish](/learn/kaddish), or say it if the Rabbi advises.
- **Say a chapter of [Tehillim](/learn/tehillim)** (Psalms).
- **Give tzedakah** or do a mitzvah in the person's memory.

Doing something helps children feel less helpless.

## Signs to watch

Grief in children comes and goes. It is normal for a child to play happily one minute and cry the next. Talk to a doctor or counselor if you see changes that last: trouble sleeping or eating, withdrawal, new fears, falling behind in school, or talk of wanting to die.

## Common questions

### Should I tell my child that G-d took Grandma?
Be careful with that phrase; a child may become afraid of G-d or angry at Him. It is better to say her body stopped working and her soul went back to G-d.

### Should my child go to the funeral?
It depends on the child. Many older children find it helps them say goodbye. If a child goes, prepare them and keep a caring adult with them. Ask the Rabbi about your situation.

### Can a child say Kaddish?
Customs vary, especially for children under bar mitzvah age. Ask the Rabbi, who can guide you.
