Yes, it is a great mitzvah to rejoice with a bride and groom: to sing, dance and make them happy at their wedding. Chabad.org teaches that joining the celebration and cheering the couple is one of the most important things a guest can do.

## Why is rejoicing a mitzvah?

Chabad.org's article on the wedding reception says that joining the celebration to cheer the couple is considered a great mitzvah. It brings an example from the Talmud: leading sages put aside their Torah study to entertain newlyweds with song and dance.

Another Chabad.org article cites the Shulchan Aruch (Even HaEzer 65:1) for the principle that making the couple joyful is a great mitzvah. In the Talmud (Ketubot 17a), Rabbi Yehuda bar Ilai danced before the bride, and Rav Shmuel bar Rav Yitzchak juggled myrtle branches while dancing. The Talmud (Berachot 6b) says that those who bring joy to the bride and groom are considered as if they had rebuilt part of Jerusalem.

Why does it matter so much? Chabad.org suggests that the guests' joy distracts the couple from the worries of married life and frees their minds to focus on one another.

## How do Jewish weddings show this joy?

- **Dancing.** Singing and dancing are the center of the reception, with pauses for the courses of the meal. At a Chassidic wedding, men dance with the groom and women with the bride, in separate circles, ideally divided by a mechitzah.
- **Lifting the couple.** The couple may be lifted on chairs. Chabad.org says that Rabbi Dovber of Lubavitch named lifting and carrying the bride and groom as one of the main ways to honor them, and that chairs are a respectful alternative to shoulders. The chairs may recall a throne, because the couple is compared to a king and queen.
- **Entertainment.** Traditional weddings may include juggling, props and acrobatics for the couple.
- **A meal of mitzvah.** A joyous mitzvah is marked with a *seudat mitzvah*, a meal with bread, meat or poultry and wine, so attending the wedding meal is a mitzvah in itself. The groom traditionally makes the blessing over bread on a large challah, which is shared with the guests.
- **Tzedakah.** The Zohar recommends inviting the poor, and the Rebbe encouraged placing a charity box on the head tables. See [Tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah).

Chabad.org notes that some Chassidic communities have a *mitzvah tantz*, in which relatives dance "with" the bride by holding a cord. Chabad does not practice this, and the Rebbe judged it inconsistent with Jewish law.

## What can a guest do?

1. **Join the dancing.** Chabad.org says you "cannot really mess up": you can join hands or put your hands on a neighbor's shoulders in a circle.
2. **Dress modestly.** Men wear a kippah, and women cover their sleeves, hemlines and necklines.
3. **Greet the couple warmly** with *mazal tov*, and if you drink, raise a glass with *l'chaim*. You never have to drink.
4. **Respect the separate dancing.** Outside close family, men and women touch only their own gender, even when hugging.
5. **Bring children.** They are a welcome part of the celebration.

## Common questions

### Is it a mitzvah to dance at a Jewish wedding?

Yes. Chabad.org calls joining the celebration to cheer the bride and groom a great mitzvah.

### Why do guests lift the bride and groom on chairs?

It is one way to express joy and honor the couple. Chabad.org says Rabbi Dovber of Lubavitch named lifting and carrying them a main way of rejoicing, and the chairs may recall a throne.

### Why do men and women dance separately?

At a Chassidic wedding, men dance with the groom and women with the bride, ideally with a mechitzah between them.

### What is a mitzvah tantz?

A custom in some Chassidic communities in which relatives dance with the bride while holding a cord. Chabad does not practice it.

### What should I wear to an Orthodox wedding?

Dress modestly. Men wear a kippah, and women cover sleeves, hemlines and necklines.

### Do I have to drink at a Jewish wedding?

No. Nobody is expected to drink, and no one should drink to excess.
