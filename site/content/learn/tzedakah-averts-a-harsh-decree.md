In the High Holiday prayer Unetaneh Tokef, the congregation calls out that teshuvah, tefillah and tzedakah, repentance, prayer and charity, "remove the evil of the decree." Chabad.org teaches that the Sages saw tzedakah as a protection from harsh decrees, and that the Lubavitcher Rebbe explained that these three words mean much more than they seem.

## What the Sages say

Chabad.org explains that the Sages taught that tzedakah brings atonement and protects a person from harsh heavenly decrees. It also notes that giving charity in honor of a sick person is a strong merit, which tradition says can annul harsh decrees.

Chabad.org also tells a story from the Talmud (Shabbat 156b). A reed-cutter was destined to die from a snakebite, but he survived because he had secretly shared his bread with a poor coworker.

These are teachings of tradition. They are not a formula, and nothing here says that a person who is ill or grieving did not give enough. If you have a personal situation on your mind, [talk to the Rabbi](/help/talk-to-the-rabbi).

## The prayer: Unetaneh Tokef

*Unetaneh Tokef* is recited on Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur. Chabad.org says it was written about a thousand years ago by Rabbi Amnon of Mainz. In the middle of it, everyone calls out together, *Uteshuvah, utefillah, utzedakah ma'avirin et ro'a hagezeirah*, popularly translated as "repentance, prayer and charity remove the evil decree." Read more about the prayer in [Unetaneh Tokef](/learn/unetaneh-tokef).

## The Rebbe's deeper reading

Rabbi Moshe Bogomilsky, presenting a teaching of the Lubavitcher Rebbe (Likkutei Sichot, vol. 2) on Chabad.org, explains that the common translations do not capture what the three words mean.

- **Teshuvah** is more than "repentance." It means returning to your essential self. A Jew's core is good, and sin is a foreign attachment that does not change that core. Teshuvah reconnects the soul to its source. See [teshuvah](/learn/teshuvah).
- **Tefillah** is more than asking for things. It means union and attachment with G-d. Requests are part of it, but not its core, so it applies even to someone who lacks nothing.
- **Tzedakah** is more than optional generosity. It comes from the root *tzedek*, justice: giving is what is right. Money is held in trust from G-d for distribution to others, and a person who gives where there is no obligation imitates G-d, who gives without being obligated.

On this reading, the three terms mean returning to one's true self, drawing close to G-d, and acting justly. A person who lives by these meanings is protected, and goodness and kindness follow.

## What this means for us

You do not need to wait for Rosh Hashanah. Chabad.org notes that tzedakah is always appropriate, and some good times to give are before prayer and, for women and girls, before lighting Shabbat candles. ## Common questions

### What does "teshuvah, tefillah and tzedakah remove the evil decree" mean?
It is the central line of the Unetaneh Tokef prayer on Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur. Chabad.org teaches that the Sages saw these three as protection from harsh decrees, and that the Rebbe explained that they mean returning to one's true self, closeness to G-d, and acting justly.

### Does giving charity cancel a bad decree?
Chabad.org teaches that tzedakah brings atonement and protects from harsh decrees. Judaism does not give a formula, and it never suggests that someone who suffers did not give enough. For your own situation, ask the Rabbi.

### Who wrote Unetaneh Tokef?
Chabad.org says it was written about a thousand years ago by Rabbi Amnon of Mainz.

### Why does the prayer say the evil of the decree is removed?
Chabad's reading is that through teshuvah, tefillah and tzedakah a person lives in line with their true self, and so is open to G-d's goodness and kindness. This is the Rebbe's explanation as presented on Chabad.org.

### Should I give tzedakah when someone is sick?
Chabad.org says that giving charity in honor of a sick person is a strong merit. Read [Giving tzedakah for someone who is sick](/learn/tzedakah-for-someone-who-is-sick) for how this is done.
