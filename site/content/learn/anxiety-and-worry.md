Judaism's answer to worry is bitachon (בִּטָּחוֹן), a calm trust that G-d runs the world, is good and cares about you, while you still do everything you reasonably can. Chabad teaches that peace of mind can be built step by step, and that it goes together with the help of doctors and counselors, never instead of them.

If you are in crisis or thinking of hurting yourself, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, right now.

## What bitachon means

Chabad.org explains that trust in G-d is more than love or awe. It is calm reliance, based on believing that G-d is good and that He cares about you, so that the future need not frighten you. We still act and make plans. The goal is to act without worrying in the mind.

A well-known parable (from the Maggid of Dubno) tells of a traveler who keeps carrying his heavy sack even after being offered a ride in a wagon. We are like that traveler when we insist on carrying every burden ourselves, although G-d carries them with us. Letting go takes reflection and practice, which is why trust is something we cultivate over time. See our guide on [bitachon](/learn/bitachon).

## Letters from the Rebbe

The Lubavitcher Rebbe wrote to many people struggling with anxiety. Chabad.org shares his advice in several letters. Retold simply:

- Study and absorb the Jewish teachings on trust in G-d and on Divine Providence, until they become part of how you think. King David's words from Psalms are a source of courage: "G-d is with me, I shall not fear."
- The reason you think is causing the anxiety may not be the real cause.
- Sometimes an uneasy feeling comes from a gap between how we live and who we truly are as Jews. Bringing daily life closer to the soul, through Torah and mitzvahs, can bring calm.
- Practical steps he suggested to some people included having mezuzahs and tefillin checked and giving a small coin to charity each weekday before morning prayer.
- In one case he advised a person to stop dwelling on the nervousness and to focus on something positive and practical.

These letters were written to specific people. They are not medical advice for you, and you should speak to a doctor or counselor too.

## Judaism values doctors and therapists

Judaism does not ask you to choose between faith and medicine. The Torah gives a doctor permission to heal, and Jewish teaching sees good medical care as part of how G-d helps us. Chabad.org describes the Rebbe encouraging a man with a mental health condition to find a good psychiatrist and follow his or her guidance, together with spiritual practice. See [the Jewish view of mental health](/learn/mental-health-jewish-view).

Talk to a doctor or counselor too, if worry affects your sleep, work or family.

## Practical steps today

1. **One step.** Do the one practical thing you can do about the worry today. Then let the rest go.
2. **Say a few Psalms.** Short chapters such as [Psalm 121](/learn/psalm-121) and [Psalm 23](/learn/psalm-23) are a Jewish tradition in anxious times. See [Tehillim](/learn/tehillim).
3. **Say Modeh Ani** in the morning, and thank G-d for the day.
4. **Give a small coin to tzedakah** each morning: [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah).
5. **Rest on Shabbat.** One day each week to put down phones, errands and decisions, and remember that the world is not on your shoulders: [Shabbat for beginners](/learn/shabbat-for-beginners).
6. **Speak to someone.** A friend, the Rabbi, a counselor.

## Common questions

### What does Judaism say about anxiety?
Judaism teaches bitachon, calm trust in G-d, together with doing what you can in practical terms. It also values seeking medical and professional help.

### Is there a Jewish prayer for anxiety?
There is a long tradition of saying Psalms in anxious times, such as Psalm 121 and Psalm 23, and speaking to G-d in your own words. See [Psalm 121](/learn/psalm-121).

### Can trust in G-d replace a doctor or therapist?
No. Chabad.org shows the Rebbe encouraging a person to see a good psychiatrist alongside spiritual practice. Talk to a doctor or counselor too.

### How can Shabbat help with worry?
Shabbat is a weekly pause from work, screens and errands, a day to rest the mind and remember that G-d runs the world.
