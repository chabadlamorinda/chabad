Chanukah (חֲנֻכָּה) is a Hebrew word that means "dedication." It names the day, about 2,200 years ago, when the Maccabees cleaned the Holy Temple in Jerusalem and dedicated it to G-d again after the Greeks had defiled it.

That is the plain meaning, and it is the one most people learn. But Jewish teachers have always loved to look inside words, and the name Chanukah carries several more messages, each one a small lesson for today.

## The plain meaning: dedication

The Hebrew root חנך means to inaugurate, to begin something with purpose. The Torah uses the phrase *chanukat hamizbeach*, "the dedication of the altar," when the Tabernacle in the desert was first put into service. In the same way, after the Maccabees won back the Temple, they built a new altar and dedicated it. The holiday took its name from that moment.

You may see the word spelled Chanukah, Hanukkah or Hanukah. The first sound is a throaty "kh," like the "ch" in the Scottish "loch." All the spellings are the same Hebrew word.

## Rest: "they rested on the 25th"

Our Sages noticed that the word can be split into two: חנו (*chanu*, "they rested") and כ״ה (*kaf-hei*, which is the number 25). Read this way, Chanukah means "they rested on the 25th." The Maccabees had fought hard, and on the 25th of the Hebrew month of Kislev they entered Jerusalem and the Temple and finally rested from battle.

Another reading is *chein kah*, "grace on the 25th": on that day, the Jewish people found favor.

## A hidden ruling in the letters

There is also a charming explanation that turns the word into a mnemonic. Chanukah can be read as a short phrase meaning "eight candles, and the law follows Beit Hillel." This refers to a famous argument about how to light the menorah. One school of sages, Beit Shammai, said to light eight on the first night and reduce each night. The other, Beit Hillel, said to light one on the first night and add one each night. We follow Beit Hillel, and the name of the holiday remembers that. You can read the reasons behind it in [Why We Add a Candle Each Night](/learn/why-add-a-candle-each-night).

## Education: the link to chinuch

The word Chanukah shares its root with *chinuch* (חִנּוּךְ), which means education. The Greek rulers did not only want to control the land. They banned Torah study and brit milah, the mitzvah that begins a Jewish boy's life as a Jew. In other words, they attacked Jewish education at its very start.

So Chanukah is also the holiday of Jewish education: the freedom to teach our children who they are. The Lubavitcher Rebbe often spoke about this connection, and it is why children are at the center of the celebration, with the dreidel, the gelt and the lights. Read more in our guide to [chinuch](/learn/chinuch) and in [Chanukah for kids](/learn/chanukah-for-kids).

## Why so many meanings?

In Jewish tradition a name is not a label. It tells you what a thing is. Chanukah teaches that dedication is not a one-time event but a way of living: we rest when the fight is over, we thank G-d for the grace we found, we keep learning, and each night we add a little more light than the night before.

## Common questions

### What does Chanukah mean in Hebrew?
Chanukah means "dedication" or "inauguration." It remembers the rededication of the Holy Temple in Jerusalem after the Maccabees won it back from the Greeks.

### Why is Chanukah on the 25th of Kislev?
The Temple was rededicated on the 25th of Kislev, and the Sages set the holiday to begin on that day. The word Chanukah can even be read as *chanu* (they rested) plus *kaf-hei* (25), a hint to the date.

### Is it spelled Chanukah or Hanukkah?
Both are correct. The Hebrew חֲנֻכָּה has no single English spelling, and the first letter is a throaty "kh" sound that English has no letter for.

### What is the connection between Chanukah and education?
The Hebrew words Chanukah and *chinuch* (education) come from the same root. The Greeks banned Jewish learning and brit milah, and Chanukah celebrates the freedom to raise Jewish children.

### Does Chanukah mean "festival of lights"?
"Festival of Lights" is a popular nickname that comes from the menorah. The word Chanukah itself means dedication, not lights.
