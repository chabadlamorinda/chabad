Jewish meditation in Chassidic teaching is called hisbonenus, "contemplation": thinking deeply about an idea of G-dliness until it moves the heart, especially before and during prayer. Unlike techniques that aim to empty the mind, hisbonenus fills the mind with a true idea and lets it shape our feelings and actions.

## What hisbonenus is

Hisbonenus comes from the Hebrew word binah, understanding. It means taking a concept, such as G-d's oneness or His constant creation of the world, and examining it carefully and at length, until it becomes real to you.

The Rambam teaches that when a person contemplates G-d's wondrous works and wisdom, he will come to love G-d and to stand in awe of Him. Chassidus, and especially the teachings of Chabad founded on the [Tanya](/learn/tanya), turned this into a daily path of inner work.

## Roots in Jewish tradition

- **The Mishnah** tells that the early pious ones would wait an hour before praying, to direct their hearts to G-d.
- **The Kabbalists** taught kavanah, focused intention, in prayer.
- **Chabad** teaches that the mind leads the heart. When the mind truly understands, love and awe follow on their own. This is the meaning of Chabad: chochmah, binah and da'at, wisdom, understanding and knowledge.

In the 1970s the Rebbe called for developing Jewish meditation rooted in Torah, so that Jews seeking inner calm and meaning would find it within their own tradition.

## How it differs from other meditation

- **It has content.** Instead of emptying the mind or focusing on a sound, we focus on a truth about G-d and the soul.
- **It leads to connection.** The goal is not only calm, but a living relationship with G-d.
- **It leads to action.** Real contemplation shows itself in kinder words, better prayer and more mitzvot.

## How to begin, step by step

1. **Choose one idea.** For example: G-d creates the entire world anew at every moment; nothing exists without His constant energy. Or: G-d is present right here, in this room, with me.
2. **Learn it first.** Study a short passage from Chassidus on that idea, such as from the Tanya, so your contemplation is grounded and accurate.
3. **Find a quiet time.** Early morning, before praying, is traditional. Five to ten minutes is a fine start.
4. **Think it through in detail.** Picture it: the sky, the trees, your own breath, all being brought into existence now. Explore the idea from different angles.
5. **Let it reach the heart.** Notice the feeling that grows, such as wonder, love, humility or joy.
6. **Bring it into prayer.** Begin praying with that awareness. Let words like "Blessed are You" carry what you have just thought about. See our [prayer guide](/learn/prayer).
7. **Carry it into the day.** Return to the thought for a moment during work or a hard conversation.

## Meditation during prayer

In Chabad, the morning prayers themselves are built for contemplation. The blessings before the Shema describe the angels and the heavens, and the [Shema](/learn/shema) declares G-d's oneness. Chassidim traditionally pray slowly, pausing to contemplate these passages. Many begin with a slow [niggun](/learn/niggun) to quiet the mind.

## Common questions

### Is Jewish meditation the same as mindfulness?
No. Mindfulness focuses on present experience. Hisbonenus focuses on G-dly truths, aiming to awaken love and awe of G-d. Both value stillness, but their goals differ.

### Do I need to be a scholar to do this?
No. Start with a simple idea, even a single sentence. Depth grows with practice and learning.

### How long should I meditate?
Start with a few minutes a day. Chassidim in earlier generations spent long hours, but consistency matters more than length.
