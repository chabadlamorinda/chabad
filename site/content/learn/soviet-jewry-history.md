For most of the twentieth century, the Soviet government worked to uproot religion, and Jewish observance was discouraged and often punished. Even so, Jews kept Judaism alive in secret, in underground schools, hidden matzah bakeries, secret circumcisions and quiet Shabbat tables, and many families in Lafayette, Moraga, Orinda and the East Bay are the grandchildren of those people.

## The campaign against Judaism

After the 1917 revolution, the new government restricted religious schools, closed synagogues and discouraged observance. Teaching children Torah and Hebrew became dangerous. Many Jews lost not only their traditions but also their names, their language and often their knowledge that they were Jewish. This history is told here without politics, as the story of ordinary people holding on to their heritage.

## Jewish life underground

Brave Jews acted despite the risk:

- **Secret schools and teachers.** Children learned Hebrew letters and Torah in hidden classrooms and private homes.
- **Hidden mikvahs.** Communities quietly built or maintained [mikvahs](/learn/mikvah).
- **Matzah for Passover.** Families baked matzah in secret so that the Seder could take place.
- **Brit milah.** Some parents circumcised their sons in secret, a great act of faith. See [brit milah](/learn/brit-milah).
- **Shabbat in a whisper.** A grandmother lighting candles behind a curtain was often the only Judaism a child saw.

Chabad Chassidim were among those who led and organized this underground network. The Sixth Lubavitcher Rebbe, Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn, was arrested in 1927 for his work strengthening Jewish life, and was released after worldwide pressure. Learn more about the [Chabad Rebbes](/learn/chabad-rebbes). Many other Jews paid heavily for the simple act of observing Judaism.

## Refuge and the war years

During World War II, many Jews fled eastward to Central Asian cities such as Tashkent and Samarkand, where Jewish communities took in refugees. Later generations of Soviet Jews who wanted to leave sometimes waited years and lost jobs for applying. They are remembered as refuseniks. The world's Jewish communities did not forget them.

## Jewish life reborn

After 1991, Jewish life across the former Soviet Union revived, with new schools, synagogues and communities, and hundreds of thousands of Jews emigrated, including to the Bay Area. Today, many families here are rediscovering what their parents and grandparents guarded. For the wider story, see the [Jewish history overview](/learn/jewish-history-overview).

## Common questions

### How did Jews keep Judaism alive in the Soviet Union?

They taught children in secret, baked matzah at home, kept Shabbat quietly and, in some cases, performed circumcisions in hiding. Chabad Chassidim helped organize much of this underground.

### What is a refusenik?

A refusenik was a Soviet Jew who applied to emigrate and was refused, often losing their job as a result. They became a symbol of Jews' determination to live Jewishly.

### My grandparents never spoke about being Jewish. Why?

In many Soviet families, speaking about Judaism was dangerous, so silence was a way of protecting the children. It does not mean your family lost its Jewish identity.

### Can I still have a bar or bat mitzvah as an adult?

Yes. Many adults celebrate a bar or bat mitzvah later in life. Ask the Rabbi about starting.

### Is there a Russian-speaking Jewish community near Lafayette?

### How can I find out whether my family is Jewish?

Look at your mother's line and gather old papers. The Rabbi can help you privately; see [how to show you are Jewish](/learn/proving-jewish-ancestry).
