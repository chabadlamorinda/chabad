Tanya says that sadness has no value in itself, because it makes a person heavy and slow, and only a person who is alert and joyful can win the inner struggle. Chapter 26 of Tanya, the central book of Chabad Chassidic thought by Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi, teaches how to deal with sadness over worldly troubles and over spiritual failings, and how to bring back joy.

This guide follows that reasoning. For joy in general, see [simcha](/learn/simcha); for the book itself, see [Tanya](/learn/tanya).

## Why joy matters for winning the struggle

In Chapter 26, Tanya compares the inner struggle to two wrestlers. A lazy wrestler loses even to a weaker opponent. So a person cannot defeat his own negative impulses with laziness and sluggishness. What is needed is alacrity, and alacrity comes from joy and an open heart.

Sadness, in other words, is not a sign of depth. Tanya treats it as a hindrance. Even the verse "In every sadness there will be profit" is read to mean that a benefit may come after sadness, namely the true joy that follows sincere regret, and not that sadness is good in itself.

## Two kinds of sadness

The chapter separates two causes:

- **Worldly sadness**: distress over livelihood, health or children. Tanya teaches the faith that everything comes from G-d and that even what hurts has a good in it that we cannot see. This is a very high spiritual ideal to grow toward, not a demand to feel happy about pain. Judaism honors grief and mourning, and nobody is asked to pretend a loss does not hurt. If you are going through a loss or a crisis, please talk to someone; see [hope in hard times](/learn/hope-in-hard-times).
- **Spiritual sadness**: feeling distressed over your own failings. When this kind of gloom over your spiritual shortcomings comes at the wrong time, for example in the middle of a workday, Tanya calls it a trick of the evil inclination, because a discouraged person is more likely to give up or give in. This is about guilt over one's service of G-d, not about depression or a low mood that will not lift, which is never anyone's fault (see the word of care below).

## Tanya's practical remedy

The chapter offers a simple order of steps:

1. **Notice the timing.** Real regret is not for any hour. When it arrives in the middle of business, it is not the right time.
2. **Set a fixed time** to look honestly at where you fell short and at G-d's greatness.
3. **Feel it fully, then let it go.** After that time, put the sadness away completely.
4. **Trust in forgiveness.** Tanya says G-d pardons abundantly, even for repeated mistakes. Believing this is the very joy that follows regret.

For the difference between heavy sadness and the alive regret that moves us to change, see [sadness or bitterness](/learn/sadness-versus-bitterness).

## Chapter 27: not letting stray thoughts sadden you

The next chapter applies the same idea to thoughts we do not want. Tanya says a person who pushes away such a thought should be glad, not sad, because he did the right thing. Read more in [how to handle unwanted thoughts](/learn/tanya-unwanted-thoughts).

## A word of care

Tanya's guidance is spiritual. It is not a treatment for depression or anxiety, which are real conditions that deserve professional care, and seeking that help is also right when needed. Feeling low is not a failure and not a lack of faith. Talking to a doctor or therapist and learning with a rabbi can go together. For personal situations, ask the Rabbi. See also the Jewish view on [mental health](/learn/mental-health-jewish-view).

## Common questions

### What does Tanya say about sadness?
Tanya says sadness is a hindrance to serving G-d and to winning the inner struggle, because it brings heaviness instead of alacrity. Genuine regret has its place, but at set times, and it should lead to joy.

### Is sadness a sin in Judaism?
Tanya calls sadness a hindrance, not a virtue, and teaches us to work against it; it does not ask anyone to blame himself for feeling low. Grief and mourning are honored in Judaism; the concern is staying stuck in heaviness.

### What is the difference between worldly and spiritual sadness in Tanya?
Worldly sadness comes from troubles such as livelihood, health or children. Spiritual sadness comes from feeling you have failed in your service. Tanya gives a different remedy for each.

### Does Tanya replace therapy or medication?
No. Tanya's guidance is spiritual, and professional help is also right when needed. Ask the Rabbi if you are unsure.

### How can I start bringing more joy into my day?
Tanya's approach is to choose actions and a set time for reflection, then let the heaviness go. Our guide to [simcha](/learn/simcha) has practical ideas.
