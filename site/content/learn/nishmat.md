Nishmat Kol Chai (נשמת כל חי), "The soul [or breath] of every living thing," is a beautiful and ancient hymn of thanks said during the Shabbat morning prayers and at the end of the Passover Haggadah. Chabad.org describes it as a song of G-d's greatness and kindness, and of how impossible it is for us to thank Him enough.

## What Nishmat means

The first words say that the soul of every living being will bless G-d's name. The Hebrew word for soul, neshamah, is related to the word for breath, and our tradition teaches that every breath is a gift. The hymn begins with that idea: because life itself is given to us, everything alive praises its Source.

Nishmat is one of the most beloved parts of the prayer service, but nobody knows for sure who wrote it. Ask the Rabbi if you would like to learn about the theories.

## When Nishmat is said

According to Chabad.org, it is recited:

- During the Shabbat morning prayers
- At the end of the Passover Haggadah, at the Seder
- When visiting a cemetery

On Shabbat and holidays, it comes in the section of morning services called Pesukei D'Zimra, the verses of praise, after the Song of the Sea and before the closing blessing of that section. On other days of the week, it is not said. Come to [Shabbat morning services](/learn/shabbat-day) and you will hear it.

## What the hymn says

You do not need to read the whole hymn at once. A taste of its themes, in plain English, from the Chabad.org translation:

- Every living being blesses G-d, and there is no other true King, redeemer or savior.
- G-d never sleeps. He wakes the sleepers, gives speech to the mute, frees the bound, supports the falling and lifts the bowed.
- If our mouths were full of song, and our bodies could express all praise, we still could not thank Him enough for His countless favors.
- It recalls rescues, including the Exodus from Egypt, sustenance in famine, and healing from illness.
- It calls on every part of us, our limbs, spirit and tongue, to give thanks and praise.

That last idea is the most moving: gratitude is not only a thought, it is something we say and do with our whole selves.

## Nishmat and gratitude in everyday life

This hymn is a school for thankfulness. Many families in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda make a point in November of naming what they are thankful for. Judaism asks us to do it daily. [Modeh Ani](/learn/modeh-ani), said on waking, is the first thanks of the day, and Nishmat is the grand thanks on Shabbat.

Some ways to bring it home:

1. Around the Shabbat table, have each person name one thing they are thankful for.
2. Thank people out loud. See [hakarat hatov, recognizing the good](/learn/hakarat-hatov).
3. After a danger or recovery, say the blessing of thanks. Read [Birkat Hagomel](/learn/birkat-hagomel). Ask the Rabbi whether there is a custom of adding Nishmat; this guide does not state one.
4. Learn a few lines in English and say them quietly on Shabbat.

## Common questions

### What does Nishmat Kol Chai mean?
It means "The soul of every living thing," and it opens a hymn in which all living beings bless and thank G-d.

### When is Nishmat Kol Chai said?
Chabad.org says it is said in the Shabbat morning prayers, at the end of the Passover Haggadah, and when visiting a cemetery. On Shabbat and holidays, it is part of the morning service.

### Who wrote Nishmat?
The author is not known for certain. If you are curious about the traditions, ask the Rabbi.

### Is Nishmat said every day?
No. It is part of Shabbat and holiday mornings. On weekdays it is not part of the regular service.

### Is there a custom to say Nishmat after being rescued or recovering from illness?
For that case, say Birkat Hagomel as the Rabbi explains. Ask the Rabbi about any custom to add Nishmat.
