Everyone has been the new person: the first day at a job, the first week in a town where you know no one, the first time walking into a synagogue where everyone else seems to know the songs. The Torah remembers that feeling and builds a commandment around it.

## "You shall love the stranger"

"You shall love the stranger, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt" (Deuteronomy 10:19). The sages noticed that the Torah warns us about the stranger more times than about almost anything else, dozens of times by their count. Why so often? Because the newcomer is the easiest person to overlook.

The reason the Torah gives is striking. It does not say "because it is kind" but "because you were strangers." Our whole people began as outsiders, and we are asked to carry that memory with us.
## Abraham's tent

The model is Abraham. The Torah describes him sitting at the door of his tent in the heat of the day, looking for travelers to welcome (Genesis 18). When he sees three strangers, he runs to them, bows, brings water and prepares a feast. Tradition says his tent was open on all four sides so that no guest would have to search for the entrance.

Chassidic teaching points out the order of events: Abraham was in the middle of a conversation with G-d, and he interrupted it to greet strangers. From here the sages learned that welcoming guests is greater than receiving the Divine presence, because a person is G-d's image walking toward you. See [hachnasat orchim](/learn/hachnasat-orchim) for the mitzvah of hosting.

## Welcoming new families in a community

A community is not a building; it is the moment someone new walks in and is seen. A few things make the difference:

- **Say hello first.** Do not wait for the newcomer to find you. Introduce yourself, ask their name, remember it.
- **Explain without being asked.** Which page we are on, when to stand, where the kids' room is. What is obvious to you is a mystery to them.- **Follow up.** A text during the week ("It was lovely meeting you, will you be there on Shabbat?") turns a visit into a return.

The Rebbe's approach was that every Jew belongs, before they have done anything to "earn" it. Nobody at Chabad of Lamorinda is asked for a membership card or a level of observance; see [not religious? you are welcome](/learn/not-religious-welcome).

## Inviting someone for Shabbat

The simplest and most powerful act of welcome is an invitation to a Shabbat meal. You do not need a big house or a gourmet dinner; you need a chair and a warm word. Think of who is new at work, on your street or at your children's school, or alone this week. See [hosting your first Shabbat dinner](/learn/hosting-your-first-shabbat-dinner).

## Helping new immigrants

For a family that has moved from another country, the "stranger" of the Torah is literal. Beyond friendship, they often need practical help: understanding school enrollment, finding a doctor, a driver's license, how to open a bank account, where to buy kosher food. Offer to be their first call for a month. If you are the one who has just arrived, see [moving to Lamorinda](/learn/moving-to-lamorinda) and call the Rabbi; that is exactly what he is here for.

## Simple steps anyone can take

1. Greet one person you do not recognize at every service or event.
2. Invite one new family for a Shabbat meal this month.
3. Bring a challah or a plate of cookies to a new neighbor, with your phone number.
4. Tell the Rabbi about a newcomer you met, so that no one slips through.

## Common questions

### Who counts as a "stranger" in the Torah's sense?
The Torah's *ger* refers to the outsider who lives among us: in its narrow sense a convert, and in its wider sense anyone new, alone or far from home.

### What if a newcomer is not Jewish?
Kindness and welcome are owed to every person, and non-Jewish spouses and friends are part of many of our families. Welcome warmly; for questions about participation in a ceremony, ask the Rabbi.
