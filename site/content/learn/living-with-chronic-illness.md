Judaism teaches that when illness lasts a long time, you hold two things together: you do everything the doctors advise, and you trust that G-d is with you and that your days still have purpose. The Rebbe's letters to people who were ill return again and again to these two ideas, with a third: keep going with joy, prayer and good deeds as best you can.

This guide is for people living with a long illness, and for the families who love them. It is not medical advice. Please talk to your doctor about anything medical, and ask the Rabbi about anything personal.

## Do what the doctors say

In his letters to people who were ill, the Rebbe, Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson, was clear that medical care comes first. He told people to follow the specialist's instructions, to get another opinion if doctors disagree, and not to treat themselves on the advice of friends. When a doctor told a patient to stop smoking, he said the patient should obey.

Jewish tradition sees a doctor as G-d's messenger for healing. Seeking treatment is not a lack of faith. It is part of faith. The Talmud teaches that the Torah itself gives the doctor permission to heal.

## Trust in G-d: bitachon

[Bitachon](/learn/bitachon) (בִּטָּחוֹן) means trust in G-d: a calm confidence that G-d is good and is running the world with care, even when we cannot see how. In the Rebbe's letters, trust is not a mood you must force. It is a way of acting "simply, without entering into calculations."

He also advised against dwelling on the illness itself. Chabad teaching often repeats a short saying: "Think good, and it will be good." This is not denial of pain. It means that a hopeful mind helps the person and the people around them, and that no one has to face the road with despair.

## Joy, purpose and what you can still do

A long illness can steal energy and plans. The Rebbe encouraged people to serve G-d with joy and to add to their Torah and mitzvot in whatever ways are possible. This might mean:

- A little [Tehillim](/learn/tehillim) (Psalms) each day, even one chapter.
- Giving [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah) each weekday morning, even a small coin.
- Lighting Shabbat candles or saying the Shema with attention.
- Having a mezuzah or tefillin checked by a scribe. Ask the Rabbi if you would like this done.
- One kind word or phone call to someone else.

Everyone is different, and some days you can do very little. That is also fine. Jewish thought teaches that a person's purpose does not depend on how much they can do. A soul is precious simply because it is here.

## Prayer and the Mi Sheberach

Many families add the name of the ill person to the [Mi Sheberach](/learn/mi-sheberach) (מִי שֶׁבֵּרַךְ), a prayer for healing said in the synagogue. The name used is the person's Hebrew name and their mother's Hebrew name. If you do not know it, the Rabbi can help. Names can stay on the list for as long as needed; you can also renew them.

Praying does not replace treatment and does not mean you did something wrong. Jewish tradition sees prayer as a conversation with G-d from the heart, in any language.

## Shabbat, fasting and difficult questions

Illness raises real halachic questions: Can I take medicine on Shabbat? Do I have to fast on Yom Kippur? The principle of [pikuach nefesh](/learn/pikuach-nefesh), saving a life, can override many laws, and Jewish law gives special leniency to the ill. But the details depend on the person and the condition. Please do not decide alone, and do not feel guilty about asking. Call the Rabbi, who will help you with your case and your doctor's advice.

## Care for the caregiver and the community

Illness affects the whole family. Spouses, children and friends need support too. See [caregiver support](/learn/caregiver-support) for more on this. The mitzvah of [visiting the sick](/learn/bikur-cholim) (bikur cholim) is a community responsibility: a visit, a call, a meal, a ride to an appointment. Our tradition teaches that the one who visits takes away a part of the sick person's burden.

If you are in Lafayette, Moraga or Orinda and need a visit, a call, or just someone to listen, reach out. If you are a neighbor who wants to help, tell us.

## Common questions

### What does Judaism say about chronic illness?
Judaism teaches that illness is not a sign that G-d has abandoned you, and that you should pursue medical care while trusting in G-d. A long illness does not reduce a person's worth or purpose. Ask the Rabbi if you wish to talk about your own situation.

### Should I rely on prayer or on doctors?
Both. Jewish tradition says to follow the doctor's advice and to pray, because the doctor is G-d's messenger for healing. The Rebbe's letters told people to follow their specialist and to strengthen their trust in G-d.

### How do I add someone to the Mi Sheberach list?
Give the Rabbi the person's Hebrew name and their mother's Hebrew name, or ask for help finding them. Call Rabbi Labkowski at Chabad of Lamorinda in Lafayette at 925-384-7242.

### Do I have to keep Shabbat and fast if I am sick?
Not always. Jewish law gives great weight to health, and many rules are relaxed when health is at stake. Because the answer depends on the person, ask the Rabbi and your doctor together.

### What can I do when I feel I have no strength left?
Do what you can, however small: one psalm, one coin of tzedakah, one blessing. Jewish tradition values sincerity over quantity. Do not hesitate to ask for support from the Rabbi or from friends.

### How can I help a friend with a long illness?
Visit or call regularly, offer specific help such as a meal or a ride, and listen without trying to fix. Visiting the sick is a mitzvah, and the presence of a friend is itself a kind of healing.
