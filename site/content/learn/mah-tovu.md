Mah Tovu (מַה טֹבוּ), "How goodly," is the verse that begins "How goodly are your tents, O Jacob, your dwelling places, O Israel" (Numbers 24:5), and many Jews say it when they enter a synagogue and at the very start of the morning prayers. It is a blessing that was spoken by someone who came to curse the Jewish people, and it has become a way to begin prayer with warmth toward other people.

## Where Mah Tovu comes from

The words come from the Torah portion Balak. A prophet named Balaam was hired to curse the Jewish people as they camped in the desert. Instead, when he looked at their camp, he blessed them with these words. Chabad.org explains, following Rashi, that what impressed him was modesty: the entrances of the tents were arranged so that no tent faced another, and nobody could look into a neighbor's home.

So Mah Tovu is praise for a people who respect each other's privacy. Our tradition also reads "tents" as the Jewish home and "dwelling places" as the houses of study and prayer, so the verse sits naturally at the door of a synagogue.

## Why we say it when we pray

The Sages teach that the verse refers to synagogues and study halls, which is why it is said on entering one. Many siddurim, including the Chabad siddur, place it near the start of the morning service, after [Modeh Ani](/learn/modeh-ani). Ask the Rabbi if you want to know exactly where it falls in the siddur you are holding.

There is a second reason that Chabad teaching stresses. Before praying, many siddurim have the person say that he or she accepts the commandment to love a fellow Jew as oneself. The idea is that prayer is communal, and we come before G-d as part of a people. Chabad.org points out that the feeling stirred by Mah Tovu is itself that acceptance: we tend to notice faults in others, but here we choose to see the good, picturing a fellow Jew as he looks in synagogue and imagining him at home in the same light. Read more in [Ahavat Yisrael](/learn/ahavat-yisrael).

## A minute-long way to say it

1. Pause at the door of the sanctuary, or at the start of your prayers.
2. Say the line slowly, in Hebrew if you can, or in English. The transliteration begins: "Mah tovu ohalecha Ya'akov, mishkenotecha Yisrael."
3. Think of one person in the room, or at home, and what is good about them.
4. Think of your own home as a small sanctuary: a place where privacy, kindness and G-d's presence are welcome.
5. Continue with the rest of the prayers. The siddur adds a few more verses of welcome, in the same spirit.

You do not need to know Hebrew. The meaning matters more than the speed.

## How we can live Mah Tovu

- **Respect privacy.** Do not pry or listen in on others. This is the heart of the tent story.
- **Speak well of people.** Compliment a neighbor in Lafayette, Moraga or Orinda in the same way you would on a good day.
- **Make your home a place others feel good in.** A warm welcome, a Shabbat table, a [mezuzah](/learn/mezuzah) on the doorpost.
- **Treat the synagogue as a home.** Greet people there kindly, especially newcomers. See [your first time at a synagogue](/learn/first-time-at-synagogue).

## Common questions

### What does Mah Tovu mean?
Mah Tovu means "How goodly" (or "how beautiful"), and the full verse says "How goodly are your tents, O Jacob, your dwelling places, O Israel." It is a blessing from the Torah (Numbers 24:5).

### Who said Mah Tovu?
Balaam, a non-Jewish prophet, said it. He was hired to curse the Jewish people but blessed them instead after seeing how their tents were arranged.

### Why were the tents arranged that way?
Chabad.org explains, following Rashi, that the tent entrances did not face each other, so people could not look into one another's homes. It is praise for modesty and privacy.

### When do Jews say Mah Tovu?
Many say it when entering a synagogue and near the start of the morning prayers. Ask the Rabbi about the custom of your own family or community.

### Do I need Hebrew to say Mah Tovu?
No. You can say it in English or read the transliteration. What counts is that you understand and mean it.

### What does Mah Tovu have to do with loving other Jews?
Chabad teaching connects it with the declaration to love your fellow as yourself, said before prayer. Saying Mah Tovu is a chance to see the good in others before you pray.
