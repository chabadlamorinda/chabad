The sandek (סנדק) is the person who holds the baby during the circumcision at a bris, and it is traditionally considered the greatest honor at the ceremony. The kvater (קוואטער) is a couple who bring the baby from the mother to the sandek, and the chair of Elijah is a seat set aside for Elijah the Prophet, the "guardian of the covenant."

If you are expecting a baby boy in Lafayette, Moraga or Orinda, here is who does what and how to choose.

## The sandek

The sandek holds the baby on his lap during the circumcision. Chabad.org explains that the place where the baby lies, the sandek's lap, is likened to an altar, and calls this the greatest honor at the ceremony. It is therefore traditionally given to someone of exceptionally good character, often a grandfather or other respected relative.

Traditionally, the sandek immerses in a mikvah beforehand, receives an aliyah to the Torah on the Shabbat before or the day of the brit, and often gives the parents or baby a meaningful gift. A separate "standing sandek" holds the baby for the blessings and the naming. Since one man does the first job and another the second, this gives you two honors to give.

Practical tip: the sandek will sit for a while, so provide a steady chair, and the mohel may want a pillow on his lap or a footstool.

## The kvater

The kvater honor goes to a couple. The wife takes the baby from the mother (or from women who pass him along) and hands him to her husband, who brings him to the mohel. The words "kvater" and "kvaterin" are the man and woman of the pair. Chabad.org says that, traditionally, a pregnant woman does not serve as kvaterin. If this applies to someone you are thinking of, ask the Rabbi.

Kvater is sometimes translated "godparent," but the role is an honor at the ceremony, not a promise to raise the child.

## The chair of Elijah

If it is your family's custom, a special chair is prepared for Elijah the Prophet, called Kisei shel Eliyahu (כסא של אליהו). One person places the baby on that chair, and another lifts him afterward and gives him to his father. Chabad.org says this is a custom and not required everywhere.

## Other honors

Chabad.org's circumcision checklist lists additional honors, such as:

- Reciting the blessings
- Reading the blessing for the naming of the child
- Leading the Grace after Meals at the celebratory meal
- Reading the six stanzas that begin "May the Merciful One..." (Harachaman)

Many of these are not mandatory. Review the list with your mohel or rabbi. See [Preparing for a bris](/learn/preparing-for-a-bris) and [Brit Milah](/learn/brit-milah).

## How to choose with family harmony

1. **Start with the Rabbi.** Ask which honors your family wants, and whether any person you are thinking of has an issue.
2. **Think of what each person can do.** The sandek must be able to sit and hold the baby steadily.
3. **Spread the honors.** With a sandek, standing sandek, kvater couple, chair of Elijah, and others, most close relatives can have one.
4. **Call before sending invitations.** Ask personally and warmly, with a backup person in case someone cannot attend.
5. **Ask someone to coordinate** on the day, to find the honorees and keep things moving.

If family dynamics are sensitive, the Rabbi can help you find a kind way forward.

## Common questions

### What is a sandek?
A sandek is the person who holds the baby during the circumcision. It is traditionally the greatest honor at a bris, usually given to a person of great character.

### What is a kvater?
A kvater is a couple who bring the baby from his mother to the sandek or mohel. It is an honor, sometimes compared to godparents.

### Are kvater the same as godparents?
Not exactly. The kvater honor is part of the ceremony, not a lifelong religious duty for the child. The parents raise the child.

### What is the chair of Elijah?
It is a chair set aside for Elijah the Prophet at the bris, where the baby is placed for a moment. It is a custom, and not every family uses it.

### Who should be the sandek?
Someone of exceptional character, often a grandfather or respected relative who can sit steadily. Ask the Rabbi if you have questions.
