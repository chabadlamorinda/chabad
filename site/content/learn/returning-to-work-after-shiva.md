In Jewish law, a mourner does not work or conduct business during the seven days of shiva, and may return to work and business once shiva ends. Mourning does not stop at that point, though: some restrictions continue through the thirty days of sheloshim, and for a parent, through the year.

This guide explains what changes on the eighth day, what stays the same, and where to ask the Rabbi about your own situation.

## No work during shiva

Shiva is the seven days that begin after the burial, and the day of burial counts as the first day. The mourners step away from daily routines, according to Chabad.org, in order to focus on the memory of the person who died, think about how to honor them in their own lives, and receive comfort.

That is why the mourner does not work or do business during these days. See our guide to [shiva](/learn/shiva) for the full picture.

If staying away from work would cause you real hardship, do not decide this alone. Call the Rabbi right away, ideally before shiva begins, and he will advise you about your case.

## What changes after shiva

Chabad.org lists what is lifted once shiva ends. The mourner may:

- Leave the shiva house and change out of the shiva clothing
- Sit on regular chairs and wear leather shoes
- Return to work and conduct business
- Use cosmetics, lotions and jewelry
- Study Torah and resume marital relations
- Greet others in the usual way, although others should not greet the mourner that way first

This is a gradual return. After shiva, the mourner is encouraged to leave the house and slowly rejoin society.

## What continues through sheloshim

Sheloshim is the thirtieth day from burial. Until then, even a mourner who is back at work keeps some restraint:

- No haircuts, shaving or nail cutting
- No new, freshly laundered or ironed clothing (shirts, underwear and socks may be changed as needed)
- No music, concerts, pleasure trips, or social dinners and parties
- No buying a home, redecorating or buying new furniture, unless delay would cause great financial loss

For relatives other than a parent, mourning ends after the morning service on the thirtieth day. See [sheloshim](/learn/sheloshim).

Work events can be tricky. Chabad.org notes that those whose profession requires them to attend festive events, such as musicians, photographers or caterers, may attend. If a work dinner or a party is on your calendar, ask the Rabbi what to do. Our guide to [mourners and celebrations](/learn/mourners-and-celebrations) explains more.

## After sheloshim, if you lost a parent

For a mother or father, mourning continues until the first yahrzeit, twelve months after death. Kaddish is said at the three daily prayers for eleven months. The restrictions on music and social events continue the full year. A mourner may get a haircut after thirty days once friends or neighbors mildly criticize an unkempt appearance. Read the guide to [the year of mourning](/learn/year-of-mourning).

## Common questions

### When can I go back to work after shiva?
You may return to work and business right after shiva ends, which is the morning of the seventh day. If your work requires you to start earlier, ask the Rabbi before shiva begins.

### Can I work during shiva?
No, work and business are not done during shiva. If this would cause real hardship, ask the Rabbi about your own case.

### Can I go to a work party during sheloshim?
Social dinners and parties are not attended during sheloshim, and for a parent, during the whole year. If your profession requires attending such events, ask the Rabbi what is appropriate.

### Do I still say Kaddish when I go back to work?
Yes. For a parent, Kaddish is said at the three daily prayers for eleven months, and it requires a minyan. Ask the Rabbi if you need help finding one.

### Can I get a haircut after I return to work?
Not during sheloshim. For a parent, a haircut may be taken after thirty days once friends or neighbors mildly criticize one's unkempt appearance.

### What if I do not feel ready to return?
The mourning framework is designed to move step by step, but Chabad.org notes that mourners may not feel ready to move on to the next, lighter stage. Speak with the Rabbi, and see [the mourning journey](/learn/mourning-journey).
