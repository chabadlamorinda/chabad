Kosher salt is coarse-grained salt, named for its traditional use in "koshering" meat: salting it to draw out the blood, which the Torah forbids us to eat. It is not "more kosher" than other salt, because pure salt is a mineral and is kosher by nature.

## Why is it called kosher salt?

The Torah forbids eating blood (Leviticus 7:26), so after slaughter meat must be soaked, salted and rinsed to remove it. This process is called *koshering*. The salt used for it has a particular size of grain, and cooks began calling it "kosher salt." "Koshering salt" would be the more accurate name.

You can read more about the full process in our guide to [kosher meat](/learn/kosher-meat).

## Why the grain size matters

According to Jewish law (Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh Deah 69), the salt for koshering meat should be neither too fine nor too coarse:

- **Fine table salt** dissolves into the meat and does not draw the blood out.
- **Very coarse salt** is too thick and simply rolls off.
- **Kosher salt** sits between the two. Its crystals cling to the meat and then fall away.

## Kosher salt versus table salt

Cooks like kosher salt because the larger crystals are easy to pinch and spread evenly. Table salt is usually iodized, which some people say changes the taste. If you swap one for the other in a recipe, remember that a spoonful of kosher salt is less salty than a spoonful of fine table salt, so use roughly twice as much by volume.

## Is all salt kosher?

Plain salt is a mineral, so it is kosher. A kosher symbol on a salt container simply means an agency confirmed that nothing else was added. For salt with anti-caking agents, flavors, or blends, look for a [kosher symbol](/learn/kosher-symbols) or ask the Rabbi. We do not recommend particular brands.

## Salt in Jewish life beyond the kitchen

Salt appears often in Jewish tradition. In Temple times, offerings were salted. Today our table is likened to an altar, so many dip the bread in salt after the blessing of [Hamotzi](/learn/hamotzi) and often keep salt on the table. Some bring bread and salt into a new home as a blessing for steady provision. Salt also reminds us of the "covenant of salt," a lasting bond.

In [challah](/learn/challah) traditions, salt on Shabbat bread is a lovely way to remember this. For the Seder, salt water recalls the tears of slavery.

## Common questions

### What is kosher salt?
Kosher salt is coarse salt whose grain size is ideal for drawing blood out of meat in the koshering process. It gets its name from that use, not from special certification.

### Is regular table salt kosher?
Yes. Pure salt is a mineral and is kosher by nature. Check for a kosher symbol if it has additives or flavors.

### Why do chefs use kosher salt?
The larger flakes are easy to pinch and spread evenly, and many cooks find it tastes purer than iodized table salt.

### Can I use kosher salt instead of table salt?
Yes, but use about twice as much kosher salt by volume, since the crystals are larger.

### Do Jews salt their food for religious reasons?
Many dip bread in salt after Hamotzi, since the table is likened to an altar where offerings were salted. Salt is also used to kosher meat.
