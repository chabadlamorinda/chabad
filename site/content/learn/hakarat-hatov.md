Hakarat hatov means "recognizing the good," and it is the Jewish value of gratitude toward G-d and toward people. Judaism treats gratitude as a foundation of character: the very word Jew, Yehudi, comes from a Hebrew root that means to thank.

## The name of a grateful people

When Leah gave birth to her fourth son, she said, "This time I will thank G-d," and named him Yehudah (Genesis 29:35). The Jewish people are called Yehudim, after him. Being Jewish means, in a sense, being someone who says thank you.

## Recognizing the good

Hakarat hatov has two parts:

1. **Noticing.** Seeing the good that has been done for us, instead of taking it for granted.
2. **Expressing it.** Saying thank you in words and showing it in actions.

The Torah teaches that gratitude reaches even things that cannot feel it. Moses did not strike the Nile or the dust of Egypt to start the plagues; Aaron did, because the river had protected baby Moses and the sand had hidden the Egyptian he struck. If we owe thanks to water and dust, how much more to people.

## Gratitude to G-d every day

Jewish life is full of thank yous:

- **Modeh Ani,** the first words on waking, thanks G-d for returning our soul. See [Modeh Ani](/learn/modeh-ani).
- **Blessings before and after food** acknowledge that everything comes from G-d. See [blessings](/learn/blessings) and [Birkat Hamazon](/learn/birkat-hamazon).
- **Modim,** in the Amidah, is a daily prayer of thanks "for Your miracles that are with us every day."
- **Shehecheyanu** celebrates new things and special times.
- **The Asher Yatzar blessing,** said after using the bathroom, thanks G-d for a healthy body.

## Gratitude to people

- **Parents.** The mitzvah of [honoring parents](/learn/honoring-parents) is rooted in gratitude for life and care.
- **Teachers, friends, helpers.** A thank you note, a call or a small gift.
- **Those who serve the community,** like doctors, firefighters and volunteers.
- **Spouses.** Thanking your spouse for the small things builds shalom bayit.

## Practical steps for a grateful life

1. **Say Modeh Ani** every morning, slowly, and mean it.
2. **Name three good things** at dinner each night. Kids love this.
3. **Write one thank you** each week.
4. **Thank G-d out loud** for small things: a parking spot, a good test, a safe drive.
5. **Give tzedakah** when something good happens, as a way of saying thanks. You can also [sponsor](/sponsor) a kiddush or Tehillim in honor of a blessing.

## Common questions

### Why is gratitude so important in Judaism?
Gratitude keeps us humble and aware that everything we have comes from G-d. It also makes us kinder people, since someone who notices the good in their life is more likely to share it.

### How do I thank G-d for something good that happened?
You can thank G-d in your own words at any time, in any language. Many also give tzedakah, sponsor a kiddush, or say a chapter of Tehillim in thanks. After surviving a danger, there is also a special blessing called Birkat HaGomel; ask the Rabbi.

### Is it ungrateful to complain?
Judaism allows us to cry out to G-d and ask for what we need. Gratitude does not mean pretending everything is perfect. It means noticing the good even when we are asking for more.

### What is Birkat HaGomel?
It is a blessing said in synagogue, usually during the Torah reading, after recovering from a serious illness, giving birth, a dangerous journey or another danger. Ask the Rabbi if it applies to you.
