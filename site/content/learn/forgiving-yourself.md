You said the words you cannot take back, or made the choice that hurt someone, and you have apologized, repaired what you could, and resolved never again. And still, at night, it comes back. Judaism has a great deal to say about forgiving others, and just as much about the harder task: forgiving yourself.

## G-d has already forgiven you

The Torah's teaching on [teshuvah](/learn/teshuvah), return, is startlingly generous. When a person regrets what they did, stops, makes right what can be made right, and commits to a different future, the wrong is forgiven. The sages teach that teshuvah out of love turns even past wrongs into merits: they became the fuel for a better life. Maimonides writes that one who has done teshuvah should not be reminded of their past; it is no longer who they are.

So if you have done teshuvah and still feel unforgiven, the one holding out is not G-d. Are you holding a higher standard than He does?

## Two kinds of guilt

The [Tanya](/learn/tanya) draws a sharp line between two feelings that look alike.

**Guilt that helps** is honest regret: "That was wrong, it does not fit who I want to be, and I will fix it." It is specific, it points toward action, and once the action is taken, it lets go. This is the regret at the heart of teshuvah.

**Guilt that paralyzes** says, "I am a bad person. I always ruin things. What is the point." It is vague, points nowhere and never lets go. The Tanya calls this *atzvut*, a heavy sadness, and teaches that it is not holiness but a trick of the inner adversary: a discouraged person does not serve G-d with energy or do much good for anyone.

## Bitterness that is brief and purposeful

The Tanya does not say "never feel bad." There is a place for *merirut*, bitterness over our failings, but it should have a chosen time, be brief, and have a purpose: to shake us awake and move us to change. Then it gives way to joy, the joy of a soul that is a part of G-d and can always return. Serve G-d with joy, and do not let the past steal the present. See [joy](/learn/simcha).

## Practical steps

1. **Name it exactly.** Not "I am terrible," but "I did this, on this day, and it hurt this person in this way." Vague guilt cannot be repaired; a specific wrong can.
2. **Repair what you can.** Apologize, return, replace, make it right. If the person is gone or unreachable, do a kindness in their name, give [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah), and treat others differently.
3. **Say it to G-d.** Out loud, in your own words. Then say, "I have done what I can. I accept Your forgiveness." See [talking to G-d in your own words](/learn/talking-to-g-d-in-your-own-words).
4. **Set a time limit on the bitterness.** When the old guilt returns, give it a moment, then deliberately turn to something good: a mitzvah, a kind word, a task.
5. **Judge yourself favorably.** You would give a friend the benefit of the doubt: they were young, frightened, did not know. Give it to yourself. See [judging others favorably](/learn/judging-others-favorably).

## When to talk to someone

Guilt that will not lift, that keeps you from sleep, work or the people you love, or that comes with thoughts of hopelessness, needs more than a guide. Talk to the Rabbi or Rebbetzin; they have heard everything and will not judge you. And please talk to a doctor or therapist too: caring for your mind is a mitzvah, and getting help is strength, not weakness. See [mental health in Jewish thought](/learn/mental-health-jewish-view) and [grief and hard times](/help/grief-and-hard-times).

## Common questions

### How do I know if my teshuvah was real?
The sages give a simple test: the same situation comes around and you act differently. If you have regretted, repaired and changed, it is real; the lingering feeling is not evidence against you.

### What if the person I hurt will not forgive me?
Judaism asks you to ask sincerely, up to three times, and to make right what you can. After that, the sages say, you have done your part and G-d accepts your teshuvah.

### Is there a special time for this?
Every night before sleep is a moment to look back, let go and begin again; see [the bedtime Shema](/learn/bedtime-shema). Yom Kippur and the month of Elul are the year's great season of return; see [Yom Kippur](/holidays/yom-kippur).
