The Book of Job (Iyov, איוב) is a book of the Writings in the Tanach about a righteous man who loses everything, and about what we can and cannot understand about suffering. Its answer is not a neat explanation. It teaches that we cannot always know why a person suffers, that we must never blame the sufferer, and that faith can continue even without answers.

## Who was Job?

According to Chabad.org, the Talmud offers several opinions about who Job was and when he lived, with no clear agreement. The most widely held view places him from the time Jacob's family went down to Egypt until the Exodus. Other opinions place him in the time of Abraham, the Judges, or even the return from Babylon. A minority view in the Midrash holds that the events of the book never actually happened, and that the book shows how strong his faith would have been even under such trials. Maimonides held that the opening scene, in which Satan challenges Job, is a parable that carries core principles of Jewish faith. Because the Sages themselves differed, you can read the book with an open mind. Ask the Rabbi if you wish to explore this.

The Midrash calls Job the most righteous of the non-Jewish nations of his time. He was upright and feared G-d. He was generous, built his home with four doors so that guests could enter from any side, supported the poor, the sick, widows and orphans, and served as a just judge.

## The story

Job was wealthy and righteous. In one day he lost his children, his servants and his livestock, as a test of his faith. He accepted this, saying that G-d gives and G-d takes away, and he blessed G-d's name. Then he was struck with painful boils. His wife told him to curse G-d, and he refused. The Talmud notes that he did not sin with his lips, though his heart was troubled.

## His friends

Three friends came to console him. Instead, they debated why he was suffering. They argued that affliction must be a rebuke for something he had done. Job rejected their explanations. He knew he was not the person they described, and he questioned G-d's justice and why Divine Providence seemed to overlook his righteousness.

Many people today meet the same "friends": the person who says, "everything happens for a reason, so what did you do?" Jewish tradition learns from the book the opposite lesson. Chabad.org notes the halachic lesson the Sages draw: in the presence of a mourner, stay silent until the mourner speaks. For more, see [comforting mourners](/learn/comforting-mourners).

## G-d's answer from the whirlwind

G-d answers Job from a whirlwind. He does not give a list of reasons. He speaks about human limitation, and about the vast gap between our understanding and His. Job admits his error and repents. G-d restores his fortune twofold, and Job lives a long and successful life. According to the Midrash, he was reconciled with his friends when he prayed for them, and this prayer brought him even greater blessing.

## What the book teaches

- **We cannot judge a person who suffers.** Never assume that a person's pain is a punishment. Investigate thoroughly before judging, and be patient.
- **Humility before G-d's wisdom.** Some things we may not understand, and our not understanding does not mean there is no meaning. See [why bad things happen](/learn/why-bad-things-happen) for the larger Jewish discussion.
- **Faith without easy answers.** Job's faith survives because it continues to speak to G-d, even in protest. See also [doubts in faith](/learn/doubts-in-faith).
- **Stay with the sufferer.** The right response to pain is presence, kindness and prayer for the other person.

The book is part of the [Tanach](/learn/tanach), and many verses from it appear in Jewish funeral and mourning customs. If you want hope in a dark time, see [hope in hard times](/learn/hope-in-hard-times).

## Common questions

### What is the Book of Job about?
It is about a righteous man who loses his wealth, children and health, and about the question of Divine justice and human suffering. It teaches faith and humility when there are no easy answers.

### Who was Job in Jewish tradition?
Chabad.org says the Talmud has several opinions on his time and identity. The Midrash calls him the most righteous of the non-Jewish nations of his time.

### Why did Job suffer?
The book does not give a simple reason. G-d answers from the whirlwind, pointing to the limits of human understanding, rather than explaining Job's suffering.

### Was Job a real person?
Chabad.org reports that the Sages differed. Most place him in a particular period of history, while a minority view in the Midrash holds that the events never happened and show how strong his faith would have been. Ask the Rabbi if you want to study this question.

### What should I say to someone who is suffering?
Job's story teaches us not to blame, and to sit in silence with the person until they speak. See [comforting mourners](/learn/comforting-mourners).
