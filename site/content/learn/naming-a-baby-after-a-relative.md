Ashkenazi Jews, whose families come from Eastern and Central Europe, customarily name a baby after a relative who has passed away and not after someone living. Sephardi Jews, whose families come from Spain, North Africa and the Middle East, often name after living grandparents as an honor. Both are authentic Jewish customs.

If you are expecting in Lafayette, Moraga or Orinda, you may be hearing different advice from each side of the family. This guide explains the customs so you can decide with confidence, and for your own family, the Rabbi is the one to ask.

## The Ashkenazi custom: honor those who have passed

Ashkenazi Jews generally do not name a child after someone who is alive. Two reasons are given. Naming after a living person could appear to be waiting for that person to pass away. And out of respect for parents, children do not use their parents' given names. There is also a positive side: a child who carries a name of a loved one who has passed keeps their memory alive, and many families feel the baby brings a bit of that person's spirit and good qualities into the world.

Ashkenazim also tend to avoid naming after a deceased person if a close living relative already has that name.

## The Sephardi custom: honor the living

Sephardi families do name babies after living relatives, often the grandparents, and the grandparent regards it as a great honor. If your family has both traditions, for example, one parent Ashkenazi and one Sephardi, ask the Rabbi which custom to follow for your family.

## Exceptions that Ashkenazi sources mention

There are several allowances. Because these depend on the case, they are for the Rabbi to decide with you:

- **A relative with two names.** Some authorities allow a baby to be named after a deceased person who had only one of the names of a living relative.
- **A living righteous person or Torah scholar.** A child may be named after such a person.
- **A parent or grandparent who agrees.** Rarely, a living parent or grandparent allows the child to take the name.

If a baby was already named after a living relative, ask the Rabbi what to do.

## What if there are several loved ones, or a name is hard to use?

Many families have more than one person they would love to honor. Some choose a Hebrew name for one and an English name for another, and some give two names. Ask the Rabbi what fits. There are different opinions about names of people who passed away young or in tragic circumstances. This is a personal question, and we encourage you to ask the Rabbi privately.

## Common questions

### Can you name a baby after a living relative in Judaism?
Ashkenazi custom is not to, while Sephardi custom is to do so as an honor. There are exceptions, so ask the Rabbi about your family.

### Why don't Ashkenazi Jews name after living relatives?
There are two reasons: it could seem like waiting for the person to die, and children show respect by not using their parents' given names.

### Can a baby be named after a grandparent who passed away?
Yes. This is the most common Ashkenazi custom, and it keeps the grandparent's memory alive.

### What if my Ashkenazi and Sephardi families disagree?
Both customs are valid. Ask the Rabbi which to follow in your family. Call 925-384-7242.

### Do we have to use the Hebrew name of a relative in English too?
No. The Hebrew name is the soul's name, and the English name is a separate choice. Many families choose an English name that echoes the Hebrew one. Ask the Rabbi if you would like ideas.

### What if a grandparent shares the name we love?
Ask the Rabbi. Depending on the situation, there may be a solution, such as a different name or the exceptions above.
