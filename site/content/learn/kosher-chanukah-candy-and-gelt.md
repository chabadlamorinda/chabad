Chanukah gelt (chocolate coins) is kosher only if the package carries a reliable kosher symbol, and the symbol tells you whether it is dairy or pareve. Look at the wrapper, not the shiny foil: foil-wrapped chocolate is not kosher just because it is gelt.

## What gelt is, and why it matters

Gelt is Yiddish for "money." Chabad.org explains that the custom of giving children money on Chanukah is tied to Torah education and to charity, and in Chabad custom gelt is given every night, with a larger gift on the fourth or fifth night. Chocolate coins are a popular modern version. See [Chanukah gelt](/learn/chanukah-gelt) for the full story. Since children eat the chocolate, it is worth making sure it is kosher.

## Step by step: how to check a treat

1. **Find the kosher symbol** on the package. A real symbol is an agency's mark, such as a circled letter or a name, not just the word "kosher" on its own. See [kosher symbols](/learn/kosher-symbols).
2. **Look for the dairy marking.** A "D" or "DE" next to the symbol usually means dairy, or made on dairy equipment. The word "pareve" means neither meat nor dairy. If there is no marking, ask the Rabbi.
3. **Check ingredients that hide.** Gelatin, glazes, colorings and some flavorings can be animal-based. See [hidden kosher ingredients](/learn/hidden-kosher-ingredients).
4. **Shop from a source you trust.** [Bay Kosher](/programs/bay-kosher), a project of Chabad of Lamorinda, is always kosher and is a local source for Chanukah and kosher items.

## Dairy or pareve gelt?

Most chocolate is dairy. Chabad.org suggests choosing pareve gelt if you serve it after a meat meal, because dairy and meat are not eaten together. If you will hand out gelt right after a meat dinner, buy pareve gelt, or hold the chocolate for later. See [meat and milk](/learn/meat-and-milk) and [pareve](/learn/pareve). Ask the Rabbi how long to wait after meat before eating dairy, since customs differ.

## Candy, sprinkles and baking treats

- **Gummy candy and marshmallows** often have gelatin. Gelatin is kosher only if it comes from a kosher source and has a symbol.
- **Sprinkles and icing** can contain animal-based glazes or colorings. Look for a symbol.
- **Chocolate chips and baking chocolate** need a symbol and may be dairy.
- **Candy from another country** can be fine, but check the symbol, and ask the Rabbi if it is unfamiliar.
- **Bulk bins and unwrapped candy** are hard to check, so choose sealed packages.

## What about chalav Yisrael?

Some families prefer dairy products made under Jewish supervision, called [chalav Yisrael](/learn/chalav-yisrael). Chabad.org notes that kosher milk should be produced under Jewish supervision, and where it is not available, some rabbinic authorities accept government inspection. Ask the Rabbi what you should do.

## Gelt for charity

Chabad.org also teaches that gelt reminds us to use money for good, and suggests giving part of the gelt, about a tenth, to charity. A simple idea is to let each child set aside a coin for [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah).

## Common questions

### Is Chanukah gelt kosher?
Only if the package has a reliable kosher symbol. Foil-wrapped chocolate coins without one are not assumed kosher.

### How can I tell if gelt is dairy or pareve?
Look for "D" or "DE" next to the symbol for dairy, or the word "pareve." If you cannot tell, ask the Rabbi.

### Can kids eat gelt after a meat meal?
Only if it is pareve. Dairy chocolate waits until after the proper time, so ask the Rabbi what your family's custom is.

### Are gummy candies kosher?
Only with a symbol, because gelatin is often animal-based. See [hidden kosher ingredients](/learn/hidden-kosher-ingredients).

### Where can I buy kosher Chanukah treats near Lafayette?
Bay Kosher, a project of Chabad of Lamorinda, is a local source and is always kosher. Call 925-384-7242 for help.

### Does a "kosher style" or "kosher" word on the wrapper count?
No. A real kosher agency symbol is what to look for. The word alone is not enough.
