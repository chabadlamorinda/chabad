The Aleph-Bet (אָלֶף־בֵּית) is the Hebrew alphabet of 22 letters, and the best way to teach it to a child is a little at a time, with the vowels, using sounds, songs and play. Chabad teaching says to teach the letters together with their vowels, because the child then learns to read real syllables from the very beginning.

## What the Aleph-Bet is

The Hebrew alphabet has 22 letters, written from right to left. Five of them have a different shape at the end of a word. The vowels are small dots and dashes called *nekudot* (נְקֻדּוֹת), placed under, over or beside the letters. Hebrew in a prayer book has vowels, so a child who learns them can read the prayers.

If you want a full overview of the letters first, see our guide to the [Hebrew alphabet](/learn/hebrew-alphabet).

## How Chabad teaches it: letters with vowels

The traditional way is to teach the Aleph-Bet together with the vowels. The child first names the vowel and then the letter, and then puts them together into a sound. For example, a *patach* under the letter *gimmel* is spoken "patach, gimmel: ga." This turns a letter from a picture into a sound the child can use.

Teachers and families differ in details such as which letters to begin with and how fast to go. If you want a plan for your child's age, ask the Rabbi.

## Ten minutes a day: a simple plan

1. **Start with the child's name.** The first letter of their Hebrew name is a very good place to begin. Show it on a card and let them trace it with a finger.
2. **Introduce two or three letters a week.** Say the name of the letter and its sound. Do not rush; repetition is the teacher.
3. **Add one vowel at a time.** Once the child knows a few letters, show one vowel, such as the *patach*, and later the others. Put it under each known letter and read aloud together.
4. **Read tiny words.** Begin with short syllables, and then a few easy words they will recognize, like *Abba* and *Ima*.
5. **Review a little every day.** Five minutes at breakfast or at bedtime works better than a long session once a week.

## Fun ways to practice

- **Sing the Aleph-Bet.** Songs help young children remember the order.
- **Letter hunt.** Find a letter on a Shabbat candle box, a prayer book, a street sign in Hebrew or a mezuzah case.
- **Build letters.** Use playdough, sand, shaving cream, pipe cleaners or chocolate chips.
- **Flash cards and memory games.** Match each letter with its sound.
- **Use a story.** Children remember a letter much better when it is part of a picture or a tiny story.
- **Celebrate small wins.** A sticker, a hug or a sweet. Chabad teaches that Torah should be experienced as sweet, and there is a custom, when a child begins learning letters, of giving them something sweet so that learning feels good. Ask the Rabbi how your family can mark the day.

## Where to go next

When your child knows the letters and vowels, begin reading short Hebrew words and then the first lines of a blessing or the Shema in a children's prayer book. Hebrew school and classes build on this, and many families find that it helps to learn together. Adults who want to start too will find [Hebrew reading for adults](/learn/hebrew-reading-for-adults) useful.

There is also a bigger idea in the title "Aleph-Bet before alphabet": give children a foundation in Torah and values first, and everything else they learn will rest on it.

## Common questions

### At what age should a child learn the Aleph-Bet?
Many children begin recognizing letters around ages four to six, and Chabad education begins very early with songs, stories and blessings. Follow your child's readiness, and keep it joyful.

### Should I teach the letters first or the vowels first?
The traditional way is to teach the letters together with their vowels, naming the vowel and then the letter and combining them. Ask the Rabbi or your child's teacher if you want to choose a method.

### How long does it take a child to learn to read Hebrew?
It varies a lot. Some children read basic words in a few months, others take longer. Steady short practice matters more than speed.

### Do I need to know Hebrew to teach my child?
No. Learn the letters one step ahead of your child, or learn together. A children's primer and a few minutes a day are enough to start.

### What if my child finds Hebrew hard or boring?
Switch to games, songs and short sessions, and pause when they are tired. Praise effort. If the struggle continues, talk to a teacher about a different approach.

### Is there a custom of honey when a child starts learning letters?
Yes. There is a long-standing Jewish custom, kept in many Chabad homes and schools, of giving a child something sweet, such as honey, when they first begin to learn the Aleph-Bet, to show that Torah is sweet. Customs of the day vary, so ask the Rabbi if you would like to arrange it.
