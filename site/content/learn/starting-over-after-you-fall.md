To start over after you fall, the Tanya says to set aside a calm time to feel real regret, then put the sadness down, trust that G-d has forgiven you, and return to your life with energy and joy. The fresh start is not a feeling to wait for; it is a sequence you can follow today.

This guide takes the practical side of the Tanya. For the nature of guilt, see [forgiving yourself](/learn/forgiving-yourself). For the book's overview of repentance, see [Iggeret HaTeshuvah](/learn/iggeret-hateshuvah) and [teshuvah](/learn/teshuvah).

## Why falling is not the end

The [Tanya](/learn/tanya) is written for the person in the middle, who struggles and sometimes loses. See [the beinoni](/learn/the-beinoni). Its Chapter 26, as Chabad.org presents it, compares serving G-d to wrestling. A sluggish wrestler loses even to a weaker opponent, because heaviness hides his strength. What helps is alacrity, which comes from joy and an open heart.

That is important after a fall. The chapter does not say "feel bad for as long as you can." It says that heaviness and sadness will not defeat the inner struggle, but energy will.

## Five steps, from Chapter 26

The chapter describes a sequence for regret and its end:

1. **Choose a time.** Sadness over our failings has a set, suitable time, when the mind is calm. It is not for all day, and not for prayer, Torah study or work. The chapter points the reader to Iggeret HaTeshuvah, Chapters 7 and 11, for how to set the time.
2. **Let the heart break.** In that time, think honestly about the wrong and about the greatness of G-d, so that the regret is real.
3. **Then put the sorrow down.** The chapter says to remove it from the heart completely. Lingering grief is not the goal.
4. **Believe you are forgiven.** The chapter says to trust with full faith that G-d has erased the wrong, since He pardons abundantly. Unlike people, G-d forgives even after repeated mistakes.
5. **Return to joy.** The certainty of forgiveness is the true joy that follows sadness. According to the chapter, this joy is greater than joy that was never preceded by sadness, as light is more striking after darkness.

## Confidence is part of teshuvah

Iggeret HaTeshuvah, Chapter 11, says that when we ask forgiveness we should ask with complete certainty that G-d forgives, since a blessing said in doubt would make little sense. It also says that "whoever comes to purify himself is given assistance." Please read that as encouragement: sincere effort gets help.

The chapter reads the verse "my sin is always before me" as a reminder in the background, held at a distance, so that it gives humility without despair.

## Sadness at the wrong time

Chapter 26 observes that sadness which arises in the middle of work, with no link to prayer or Torah, may be a trick of the evil inclination meant to lower a person's spirits. Genuine sadness over sin comes when love or fear of G-d is awake, as in prayer or study.

That is the Tanya's spiritual guidance. If the heaviness stays with you, if it affects sleep, work or family, or you are in despair, please also get professional help, and ask the Rabbi. Both are right.

## Begin again, today

For many families in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda, starting over is as simple as the next action. After a fall, the Tanya's invitation is to choose the next good deed, a mitzvah, a kind word or a phone call, and do it with energy. See [success and failure](/learn/success-and-failure).

## Common questions

### How do I start over after a mistake in Judaism?
Set a time to feel regret honestly, then let the sadness go, trust that G-d forgives, and return to your life with joy.

### What does Tanya say about falling again and again?
Chapter 26 and Iggeret HaTeshuvah say that G-d forgives readily, even after repeated mistakes, unlike people. This is not an invitation to sin, but a reason not to give up.

### Should I feel bad for a long time?
No. Chapter 26 says regret has set times, and that lingering sorrow is not the goal.

### Is joy really possible after I did something wrong?
Yes. The Tanya calls the joy that follows regret true joy, and says it is greater than joy not preceded by sadness.
