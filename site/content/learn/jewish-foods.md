Traditional Jewish foods are tied to Shabbat, holidays and Jewish law, so most of them carry a story or a meaning beyond their taste. Challah, cholent, latkes and hamantashen are not just recipes: each one teaches something about Jewish life and history.

## What makes a food "Jewish"

Jewish food developed wherever Jews lived, from Eastern Europe to North Africa and the Middle East, so there is no single Jewish cuisine. What unites it is that it is [kosher](/learn/kosher) and shaped by the rhythm of the Jewish calendar. Many classic dishes exist because of Shabbat laws, such as not cooking on Shabbat, or because of a holiday custom.

## Classic foods and their meanings

**Challah:** Braided bread eaten at every Shabbat and holiday meal. Two loaves recall the double portion of manna that fell before Shabbat in the desert. The name comes from the mitzvah of separating a piece of dough, called "taking challah." See our [challah guide](/learn/challah).

**Cholent:** A slow-cooked stew of meat, beans, potatoes and barley. Since we do not cook on Shabbat, cholent is put on the fire before Shabbat begins and simmers overnight, giving a hot meal on Shabbat day. Eating hot food on Shabbat is itself a traditional way of honoring the day.

**Kugel:** A baked pudding of noodles or potatoes, often served on Shabbat. Some say its crust above and below recalls the manna in the desert, which lay between two layers of dew.

**Gefilte fish:** Ground fish formed into patties or loaves. Fish is a traditional Shabbat food, a sign of blessing and fertility. Grinding the fish removes the bones before Shabbat, avoiding the question of separating bones from fish at the table.

**Latkes and sufganiyot:** Potato pancakes and jelly doughnuts fried in oil for Chanukah, recalling the miracle of the oil in the Temple. Many also eat dairy foods on Chanukah in memory of the heroine Yehudit. See the [Chanukah story](/learn/chanukah-story).

**Hamantashen:** Three-cornered filled pastries for Purim. The filling hidden inside recalls the hidden miracle of Purim, where G-d's hand worked behind the scenes. The name is often linked to Haman, the villain of the story.

**Honey cake:** A sweet cake for Rosh Hashanah, part of the custom of eating sweet foods to ask for a sweet new year. See our guide to the [Rosh Hashanah simanim](/learn/rosh-hashanah-simanim).

**Blintzes:** Thin pancakes filled with sweet cheese, a favorite on Shavuot, when it is customary to eat dairy foods. One reason: when the Jews received the Torah and its kosher laws, they could not yet prepare meat properly, so they ate dairy.

## Other favorites

- **Matzah ball soup:** a Passover classic, made with matzah meal.
- **Tzimmes:** sweet carrots for Rosh Hashanah and Shabbat.
- **Kreplach:** dumplings eaten on the eve of Yom Kippur, Hoshana Rabbah and Purim.
- **Charoset:** the sweet mixture on the [seder plate](/learn/seder-plate).

## Keeping traditional food kosher

Traditional recipes are easy to make kosher with kosher ingredients and the right utensils. Remember that meat and dairy are never mixed: a cheese blintz and a meat cholent belong at different meals. See our guides to [meat and milk](/learn/meat-and-milk) and the [kosher kitchen](/learn/kosher-kitchen).

## Common questions

### Is all Jewish-style food kosher?
No. A "Jewish-style" deli or bakery is not necessarily kosher. Look for reliable certification. See our [kosher symbols](/learn/kosher-symbols) guide.

### Why do we eat fish and meat at Shabbat meals?
Our Sages teach that we honor Shabbat with the best food we have. Fish, meat and wine have long been signs of a festive meal.

### Do Sephardic Jews eat the same foods?
Many dishes differ. Sephardic and Mizrachi Jews have their own beautiful traditions, such as hamin, their version of cholent. The meanings behind Shabbat and holiday foods are shared.

### Can I make cholent in a slow cooker?
Yes. Many people use a slow cooker set up before Shabbat. Ask the Rabbi how to set it up properly.
