**Pikuach nefesh** means "saving a soul." It is one of the most important principles in Jewish law: when a human life may be in danger, almost every other mitzvah steps aside.

## "And live by them"

The Torah says of its commandments, "a person shall do them and live by them" (Leviticus 18:5). The Sages explain: live by them, and not die by them (Talmud, Yoma 85b). The mitzvot were given to bring life, not to cost it.

The Mishnah teaches that whoever saves one life is as if they saved an entire world (Sanhedrin 4:5). Saving a life is not a reluctant exception to Judaism. It is Judaism.

## What it overrides

When there is a real possibility of danger to life, these give way:

- **Shabbat and holidays.** Call 911, drive to the hospital, turn on lights, use the phone, do whatever is needed.
- **Yom Kippur and other fasts.** A person whose health would be endangered by fasting must eat and drink. See [The Yom Kippur Fast](/learn/yom-kippur-fast).
- **Kosher.** If a person's life depends on a food or medicine that is not kosher, they take it.

Even a **doubt** is enough. We do not wait to be sure the danger is real. The Code of Jewish Law says that one who acts quickly to save a life on Shabbat is praiseworthy, and one who stops to ask a question while a life hangs in the balance is doing wrong.

## Real examples

- A child swallows something dangerous on Shabbat: call 911 right away.
- A woman goes into labor on Shabbat: she goes to the hospital. Jewish law treats a woman in labor as a case where Shabbat gives way.
- Chest pain, a bad fall, trouble breathing, a high fever in a baby: get help now.
- A person with diabetes who needs insulin or food on Yom Kippur: they take it.
- Someone who takes daily medicine for a serious condition keeps taking it on Shabbat and fast days.

Ideally, the person who acts is a knowledgeable adult, without delay. Do not waste time looking for someone else to do it.

## Gray areas: ask the Rabbi

Not every illness is life-threatening, and Jewish law has many in-between categories: a bad cold, a headache, a chronic condition, how much to eat on a fast day, how to get home from the hospital after Shabbat ends. These depend on the details. Ask the Rabbi before Shabbat or the fast if you can, and talk to your doctor. See [Shabbat Practical Questions](/learn/shabbat-practical-questions).

The rule of thumb: **when in doubt, save the life first, then ask.**

## The three exceptions

There are only three things a Jew may not do even to save their own life (Talmud, Sanhedrin 74a):

1. Worshipping idols.
2. Forbidden relations, such as adultery or incest.
3. Murder: one may not kill an innocent person to save oneself.

Everything else gives way to life.

## Common questions

### Should I call 911 on Shabbat if I am not sure it is serious?
Yes. If there is a reasonable chance of danger, call. A doubt about life is treated as danger. You can ask the Rabbi afterwards.

### Can I take my regular medication on Shabbat?
If it is for a serious condition, yes, always. For minor aches and pains the rules are more detailed; ask the Rabbi about your situation.

### I was told by my doctor not to fast on Yom Kippur. Am I sinning?
No. Following your doctor's instructions to protect your health is a mitzvah. Speak with the Rabbi before the fast about how to eat in the best way.

### Does pikuach nefesh apply to saving a non-Jew?
Yes. Jewish law requires saving a human life, and helping any person in danger is a Kiddush Hashem. See [Kiddush Hashem](/learn/kiddush-hashem).
