To teach your children the weekly Torah portion (parsha), take five minutes at the Shabbat table for one story, one question and one lesson, and let the children do much of the talking. Every week of the year has a portion, so you always have something new to share.

## What the parsha is

The parsha (פָּרָשָׁה, "portion") is the section of the Torah read in synagogue each Shabbat. The Torah is divided into 54 portions so that we read the whole Torah every year. This week's portion is on [the parsha page](/parsha), and a longer introduction is in [the weekly Torah portion](/learn/weekly-parsha).

## Where to find kid-friendly material

Chabad.org has weekly material made for families: a Family Parshah summary you can print for the Shabbat table, question-and-answer sheets with the verses behind each question, parsha videos and songs, and games and coloring pages. You do not need to be an expert. Read the family page on Friday afternoon and pick one moment from the story.

## A five-minute Friday night routine

1. **Tell one story (1 to 2 minutes).** Choose a single scene from the portion, in your own words. Short and vivid works better than complete.
2. **Ask one question (1 to 2 minutes).** Let each child answer. There are no wrong answers, only ideas. Try "What would you have done?" or "Why do you think she did that?"
3. **Share one lesson (1 minute).** Offer one simple takeaway, such as "Kindness to guests matters," and ask the children to name one way to do it this week.
4. **Make it a game.** Give each person a turn to add a detail, or act out a scene.
5. **End with a blessing** for each child (see [Shabbat with kids](/learn/shabbat-with-kids) and [Friday night dinner step by step](/learn/friday-night-dinner-step-by-step)).

## By age

- **Ages 3 to 6.** Use a picture, a puppet or acting. One idea: Noah building the ark and caring for the animals. Ask "What did Noah need to do first?"
- **Ages 7 to 10.** Ask "why" questions, and let them be the detective. "What does this verse tell us about how to treat people?" Let them draw the scene.
- **Preteens and teens.** Ask for opinions on a moral choice in the story, and welcome disagreement. Invite them to look up a question and report back next week.

## Let the child teach

Children remember what they teach. Ask one child to tell the story next week, even if they only learned it from Hebrew School or a printout. Praise the effort. A child who "teaches" the table feels that Torah belongs to them. If they have a question you cannot answer, write it down and ask the Rabbi.

## For busy families

If Friday night is rushed, try the car, Saturday lunch or Sunday morning. Families in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda often use [Hebrew School](/programs/hebrew-school) as a springboard: ask your child what the class learned and build on it.

## Common questions

### How do I teach my kids the weekly parsha?
Share one story, ask one question and offer one lesson at the Shabbat table for about five minutes, and let the children answer.

### What is a parsha?
A parsha is a weekly portion of the Torah, read in synagogue every Shabbat so that the whole Torah is read each year.

### Where can I find the parsha for kids?
Chabad.org has family and kids' pages for each portion, and this site has [this week's parsha](/parsha). Ask the Rabbi for a kid-friendly version.

### What if I do not know the story well?
Read a short summary on Friday afternoon and learn together. Admitting "I do not know, let's find out" teaches children that learning never ends.

### What are good Shabbat table questions for children?
Ask "What happened?", "What would you have done?" and "What can we learn?" Open-ended questions invite every child to join.

### How long should the conversation be?
Five minutes is plenty. Short and joyful beats long and tiring.
