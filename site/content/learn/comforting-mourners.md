Comforting mourners, called *nichum aveilim* in Hebrew, is one of the greatest acts of kindness in Judaism. The most important thing is simply to be present, listen, and let the mourner know they are not alone.

## Why it matters

The Torah tells us that after Abraham passed away, "G-d blessed Isaac his son." The Sages explain that G-d Himself was comforting the mourner. When we comfort someone in grief, we are following in G-d's ways.

Comforting mourners is also an act of kindness for the person who passed away, honoring their memory and the life they lived.

## When to visit

The main time for comforting is during [shiva](/learn/shiva), the seven days after the burial. Before the funeral, during [aninut](/learn/aninut), the family is busy with arrangements, so practical help is better than a formal visit.

Mourners do not observe public mourning on Shabbat, so visits are usually made on weekdays. Check the hours the family is receiving visitors.

## What to say

- **Let the mourner speak first.** Traditionally visitors wait for the mourner to begin. This lets them set the tone.
- **Ask about the person.** "Tell me about your mother" opens the door to memories.
- **Share your memories.** A story they have never heard can be a treasured gift.
- **Keep it simple.** "I'm so sorry" or "I'm thinking of you" is enough. Silence is fine too.

When leaving, say the traditional words of comfort:

**HaMakom yenachem etchem betoch she'ar aveilei Tzion v'Yerushalayim.**
"May G-d comfort you among the other mourners of Zion and Jerusalem."

## What to avoid

- "I know how you feel." Every loss is different.
- "At least they lived a long life" or "It was for the best." These can feel dismissive, even when well meant.
- Changing the subject to your own experiences.
- Staying too long. Twenty to thirty minutes is usually right.

## Practical ways to help

1. **Bring food.** Prepared meals are a huge help. If the family keeps kosher, make sure the food is kosher.
2. **Help make a minyan.** Coming to the prayer services in the shiva house lets mourners say [Kaddish](/learn/kaddish).
3. **Offer specific help.** Instead of "Let me know if you need anything," try "Can I pick up your kids on Tuesday?"
4. **Give tzedakah** in the person's memory and let the family know.

## After shiva

Grief does not end after seven days. Some of the most meaningful comfort comes later:

- Call or visit during [sheloshim](/learn/sheloshim) and the months after.
- Remember the yahrzeit and send a note.
- Invite the mourner for a Shabbat meal.
- Keep mentioning the person by name. Mourners often fear their loved one will be forgotten.

## Common questions

### Can I comfort a mourner by phone or message?
Yes. If you cannot visit, a call, card or message is still a real comfort. A visit in person, when possible, is best.

### What if I did not know the person who passed away?
You can still comfort the mourner. Ask about their loved one and listen. Your care for the mourner is what matters.

### Should I bring flowers?
Flowers are not traditional at Jewish funerals or shiva homes. Food or a donation in the person's memory is more customary.

### What do I say to a non-Jewish friend who is grieving?
Kindness has no boundaries. Be present, listen and offer help. The same wisdom about what to say and avoid applies.
