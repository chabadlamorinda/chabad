The Jewish calendar has six public fast days: two major fasts, Yom Kippur and Tisha B'Av, which last about 25 hours, and four minor fasts that last from dawn until nightfall. Fasting is a time for reflection, prayer and teshuvah, returning to G-d.

## Why do Jews fast?

Fasting is not about suffering for its own sake. Setting aside food helps us focus on the soul. Jewish fasts serve two purposes:

- **Teshuvah:** a fast awakens us to examine our actions and improve. See our guide on [teshuvah](/learn/teshuvah).
- **Mourning and remembrance:** many fasts recall tragedies in Jewish history, especially the destruction of the Holy Temples in Jerusalem.

The prophets promise that in the era of Moshiach, these days of sadness will become days of joy.

## The major fasts

**Yom Kippur (10 Tishrei).** The Day of Atonement, the holiest day of the year. The fast begins before sunset and ends after nightfall the next day. Besides food and drink, we refrain from washing, applying lotions, wearing leather shoes and marital relations. It is a day of forgiveness and closeness to G-d, not of sadness.

**Tisha B'Av (9 Av).** A day of mourning for the destruction of both Temples and many other tragedies. It also lasts from sunset to nightfall, with the same restrictions as Yom Kippur. We sit on low seats and read the Book of Lamentations (Eicha).

## The minor fasts

These last from dawn (alot hashachar) until nightfall. Washing and wearing leather shoes are permitted.

1. **Fast of Gedaliah (3 Tishrei):** the day after Rosh Hashanah, mourning the assassination of Gedaliah, the Jewish governor after the First Temple's destruction.
2. **10 Tevet:** the start of the Babylonian siege of Jerusalem.
3. **Fast of Esther (13 Adar):** the day before Purim, recalling Queen Esther's fast before she approached the king.
4. **17 Tammuz:** the walls of Jerusalem were breached. It begins the Three Weeks of mourning leading to Tisha B'Av.

If a fast falls on Shabbat, it is usually moved, except Yom Kippur, which is observed even on Shabbat. Check our [holidays](/holidays) page for exact dates and times.

## Other fasts

- **Fast of the Firstborn:** on the day before Passover, firstborn males traditionally fast, or attend a siyum (completion of Torah study) that exempts them.
- **Personal fasts:** some people fast on a parent's [yahrzeit](/learn/yahrzeit), and a bride and groom traditionally fast on their wedding day until the ceremony.

## Who should not fast?

Health always comes first in Jewish law. The following are generally exempt or have special rules:

- Children before bar or bat mitzvah age.
- People who are ill, or who need to take medication.
- Pregnant and nursing women, especially on the minor fasts.
- Anyone for whom a doctor says fasting is dangerous.

Rules differ between the major and minor fasts. If you have a health concern, ask the Rabbi and consult your doctor. Never risk your health to fast.

## Common questions

### Can I drink water on a fast day?

No, on all public fast days both eating and drinking are not allowed. If you need water for medical reasons, ask the Rabbi.

### Can I brush my teeth on a fast day?

On Yom Kippur and Tisha B'Av, brushing is generally avoided. On minor fasts, there is more leniency, but care must be taken not to swallow. Ask the Rabbi for guidance.

### When does a minor fast end?

At nightfall, when three stars appear. Check local times on our [holidays](/holidays) page.

### Can I eat before dawn on a minor fast?

Yes, if you decided before going to sleep to wake up early and eat. Ask the Rabbi about the details.
