Mazal tov! A baby boy is on the way, or has just arrived, and in about a week there will be a bris. This guide walks you through it step by step. The ceremony itself is explained in [brit milah](/learn/brit-milah); here we focus on getting ready.

## Step 1: Count to the eighth day

The Torah commands that a Jewish boy is circumcised on the eighth day of his life (Genesis 17:12). The day of birth counts as day one, so a boy born on a Tuesday has his bris the following Tuesday. A baby born after sunset counts as born the next day, so ask the Rabbi to confirm the date. A bris on the eighth day takes place even on Shabbat or a holiday.

The baby's health always comes first. If a doctor has any concern, such as jaundice, low weight or a stay in the NICU, the bris is postponed until the baby is well, and a delayed bris is never held on Shabbat or a holiday.
## Step 2: Choose a mohel

A mohel is trained specifically in brit milah, both in the medical skill and in the Jewish law and meaning of the ceremony. The Rabbi can help you find a mohel. Call as soon as the baby is born, or even before, because the mohel needs to see the baby, usually the day before, to check that he is ready.

## Step 3: Decide where and when

A bris is held in the morning if possible, since we hurry to do a mitzvah. It can take place at home, at Chabad or in a hall. The ceremony itself takes only a few minutes.

## Step 4: Give out the honors

- **Sandak:** holds the baby on his lap during the circumcision. This is the greatest honor of the day, often given to a grandfather, or to a rabbi or a man known for his good deeds.
- **Kvatter and kvatterin:** the couple who carry the baby from the mother into the room and hand him to the sandak, often a couple hoping for a child of their own.

## Step 5: Choose the name

The baby's Hebrew name is announced at the bris, and by custom it is kept quiet until then. Ashkenazi families usually name after a loved one who has passed away; Sephardi families often honor living grandparents. See [Jewish baby names](/learn/jewish-baby-names) for ideas and [baby naming](/learn/baby-naming) for how a name is given.

## Step 6: Plan the meal

The meal after a bris, the *seudat mitzvah*, is a real celebration and an important part of the day. It does not need to be fancy: bagels, salads and cake make a perfectly good bris breakfast. Keep the food kosher; the Rabbi can suggest how.
## Step 7: Tell the grandparents and guests

Grandparents often want to be involved, and a bris is a wonderful way to include them. Explain early that the date is set by the Torah, so it may fall on a workday, that the ceremony is short, and that there are honors that can be theirs. For guests who have never been to a bris, a line on the invitation ("a short ceremony followed by a festive breakfast") puts them at ease.
## Step 8: Keep the baby comfortable

The mohel will give instructions about feeding before the ceremony and care in the first days afterward; follow them. Have a clean outfit, diapers and a blanket ready, and a quiet room where the mother can rest with the baby afterward. Most babies settle and sleep soon after.
## Common questions

### What if the baby is born premature or needs to stay in the hospital?
The bris waits until the doctors and the mohel agree the baby is healthy, however long that takes. Ask the Rabbi to help set the new date; it will not be on Shabbat or a holiday.

### What do the parents do during the ceremony?
Very little. The father says one blessing (the mohel shows him the words), and everyone answers "Amen." The mother may be in the room or in a quieter room nearby; both are fine.
