Divorce is painful for everyone, and most of all for children, who did not choose it. Judaism does not judge a family that has come apart; the Torah itself provides for divorce. What it offers is steadiness: rhythms, mitzvot and people that stay the same when so much else is changing.

## Keep Shabbat and the holidays steady

Children in two homes live with two sets of rules, two bedrooms and two calendars. Shabbat can be the one thing that is the same in both. Even if the parents observe differently, agree on a few shared anchors: candles on Friday night, a blessing over the children, Kiddush and challah, a phone-free hour together. A child who lights candles with Mom one week and with Dad the next still has one Shabbat. See [Shabbat with kids](/learn/shabbat-with-kids) and [blessing your children](/learn/blessing-your-children).

Plan the holidays early and in writing, so the children are not caught in a last-minute tug of war; many families alternate holidays or divide Chanukah's eight nights. The goal is that the holidays remain joyful, not something the children dread.

## Honor both parents

The commandment to honor father and mother does not end with a divorce, and children learn how to keep it mainly from us. The hardest and most important rule: do not speak badly of the other parent in front of the children. A child hearing one parent demeaned is wounded on both sides, because half of them comes from that parent. Speak well of the other parent, and never make a child carry messages or secrets. See [honoring parents](/learn/honoring-parents).

## Their Jewish education goes on

A child's Hebrew school, Bar or Bat Mitzvah preparation and Jewish friendships are among the most stable things in their life; keep them going even if logistics are harder now, and make sure both parents are welcome at every ceremony. A [Bar or Bat Mitzvah](/learn/bar-mitzvah) after a divorce takes extra planning, but it can be a day when a child sees the whole family standing together for them. Talk early with the Rabbi, who has helped many families make it so.

## The Jewish divorce (get)

When a Jewish marriage ends, Jewish law asks for a get, a Jewish divorce document given through a Beth Din, in addition to the civil divorce. Completing it promptly is an act of kindness to each other and matters for the children's future, since it allows each parent to remarry under Jewish law. The process is simpler and gentler than many people fear, and the Rabbi can guide you through it discreetly. See [Jewish divorce and the get](/learn/jewish-divorce).

## What children need to hear

Children often believe that the divorce is their fault, or that they can fix it. Tell them clearly, more than once: this is not because of you, both parents still love you, and that will not change. Let them be sad or angry without rushing to cheer them up. Chassidic teaching sees every child as a whole soul with their own path; your job is not to spare them all pain, which no parent can, but to make sure they are never alone in it.

## Take care of yourself, and ask for help

Children do best when their parents are doing all right. Judaism asks us to care for our own health and spirit too; it is not selfish. Rabbi Yisroel and Rebbetzin Tzipora Labkowski are here for a private conversation at any stage. For the children's emotional needs, and your own, a family therapist or child psychologist is a real help. ## Common questions

### How should we tell the children?
Together if you can, calmly, with simple words that suit their age, and with the same message from both of you: this is not their fault, both parents love them, and here is what will stay the same. Expect to repeat it many times.

### Who decides the children's Jewish upbringing after a divorce?
Ideally both parents agree on the main things, such as school and holidays, and write them down. Where you cannot agree, a mediator or the courts decide; Jewish law also has views on it. Ask the Rabbi, and keep the children's stability as the goal.

### Our levels of observance are different. What do the children do in each home?
Children can manage two homes with different customs if neither parent mocks the other. Agree on what travels with the child: perhaps kosher food, Shabbat candles and the bedtime Shema. Ask the Rabbi about cases where Jewish law is involved.
