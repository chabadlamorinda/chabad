Aninut is the period of mourning from the moment of a close relative's death until the burial. A mourner during this time is called an *onen*, and Jewish law frees them from many obligations so they can focus fully on caring for the person who passed away.

## What is aninut?

The word *aninut* comes from a Hebrew root connected to deep grief. It describes the first, raw stage of loss, when the mourner is in shock and the main task is arranging a proper Jewish funeral and burial.

Aninut applies to the same seven close relatives who later sit [shiva](/learn/shiva): father, mother, son, daughter, brother, sister and spouse.

## Why the special rules?

Jewish tradition is deeply sensitive to the state of a new mourner. Before the burial, the dignity of the person who passed away comes first. A mourner cannot be expected to pray calmly or celebrate anything while their loved one is not yet laid to rest. So the Torah lifts the usual obligations and lets them devote themselves to the funeral.

## What an onen does and does not do

During aninut, the mourner traditionally:

- **Is exempt from positive mitzvot,** such as daily prayers, putting on [tefillin](/learn/tefillin) and saying blessings, because their focus belongs on the burial.
- **Does not eat meat or drink wine,** and avoids celebrations and entertainment.
- **Focuses on arrangements:** contacting the Chevra Kadisha (Jewish burial society), the funeral home, the cemetery and the Rabbi.
- **Does not yet observe shiva customs.** Sitting low, receiving visitors and other shiva practices begin after the burial.

Kriah, the tearing of a garment, is usually done at the funeral, guided by the Rabbi.

## Shabbat and holidays

On Shabbat and major holidays, funerals do not take place, and many of the rules of aninut are set aside. The mourner may join in Shabbat prayers and meals. Because the details depend on timing, ask the Rabbi.

## When does aninut end?

Aninut ends when the burial is complete. At that point the mourner begins shiva, and the obligations to pray and say blessings resume. The mourner begins saying [Kaddish](/learn/kaddish) at the graveside.

## How you can help an onen

- **Handle practical tasks.** Make phone calls, pick up relatives, arrange food for after the funeral.
- **Do not make a formal visit yet.** Traditionally, condolence visits begin during shiva, after the burial. A quick, helpful presence is appropriate if the family welcomes it.
- **Call the Rabbi.** He can help coordinate the Chevra Kadisha, the funeral and the shiva.

## Common questions

### How quickly should the funeral happen?
Jewish tradition values burial as soon as possible, ideally within a day, out of respect for the person who passed away. Some delays are allowed, for example to let close family arrive. Ask the Rabbi.

### Can an onen go to work?
Generally the onen's time is devoted to funeral arrangements. If there is a pressing work matter, ask the Rabbi.

### What if the burial is far away or delayed?
Distance, delays and who is handling the arrangements can change how aninut works. Call the Rabbi as soon as possible so he can guide you.

### Should an onen say Kaddish before the funeral?
No. Kaddish begins at the burial. Until then, the mourner's role is to care for the arrangements.
