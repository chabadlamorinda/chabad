If someone has no son to say Kaddish, or the sons cannot realistically say it every day with a minyan, Chabad.org says another person should say Kaddish in the deceased's merit: a close male relative, or someone hired to do so. It is a last resort, not a first choice, and the mourner continues to do good in the person's memory.

Chabad of Lamorinda can arrange Kaddish for your loved one: please use the form on our [Kaddish page](/kaddish). A memorial plaque can also be added in the synagogue; please consult the Rabbi (925-384-7242).

If you are in that situation, you are not alone, and there are good options.

## Who normally says Kaddish

According to Chabad.org, Kaddish is traditionally said by sons for a parent for eleven months after death, and on each yahrzeit. It is said at the three daily prayers and requires a minyan, which is ten adult Jewish men. See the full guide to [Kaddish](/learn/kaddish).

## When there is no son, or he cannot say it

Chabad.org gives two options:

1. **Another male relative, preferably a close one.** Some authorities prefer a son-in-law first. Ideally he should not be someone whose parents are both still living; if his parents are alive, he should ask their permission. He should consult his rabbi.
2. **Someone outside the family.** The person saying Kaddish must be told the deceased's Hebrew name and the Hebrew name of the deceased's father. The relatives should pay him a stipend. Some rabbinical colleges offer this service for a donation.

Chabad.org says hiring a substitute is "not an ideal first choice," and is for real need, not convenience. On each day when someone else says Kaddish, the mourner should still study Torah and give charity, except on Shabbat and Jewish holidays.

## What about daughters?

The Chabad.org pages on Kaddish speak of sons and do not settle whether daughters say Kaddish or hire a substitute. Customs differ among communities, so please ask the Rabbi about your own situation.

What Chabad.org does say is that women may take on a specific mitzvah in honor and memory of the deceased, and that this brings the same merit to the soul as saying Kaddish. Good examples are lighting Shabbat candles, giving tzedakah or studying Torah in their memory. See [learning in memory of a loved one](/learn/learning-in-memory-of-a-loved-one).

## Other ways to honor the soul

Chabad.org calls this "nothing greater" one can do for a departed soul: accept additional good deeds "in merit of" the person.

- Study Torah, especially Mishnah
- Give tzedakah
- Take on an improvement in a mitzvah
- Establish a fund or institution in their memory

Children, relatives and friends can all take part. On the [yahrzeit](/learn/yahrzeit), anyone who is observing the day may say Kaddish. Read about the [first yahrzeit](/learn/first-yahrzeit).

## Common questions

### Who says Kaddish if there is no son?
Another male relative, preferably a close one, or someone outside the family who is hired to say it. Chabad.org says this is for real need, and the Rabbi can guide you.

### Can you hire someone to say Kaddish?
Yes, when there is no son or the sons cannot say it daily with a minyan. The relatives pay the person a stipend, and meanwhile the mourner studies Torah and gives charity.

### Can a daughter say Kaddish?
Chabad.org's pages on this do not settle it, and communities differ. Ask the Rabbi. A daughter can also take on a mitzvah in her parent's memory, which brings the same merit to the soul.

### Can a friend say Kaddish for someone who died?
Chabad.org says children, relatives and friends are all encouraged to do good deeds in the person's merit. For Kaddish itself, the options are a relative or a hired substitute. Ask the Rabbi in your case.

### What if I cannot get to a minyan every day?
Then Kaddish can be arranged through a substitute. Call Rabbi Labkowski at 925-384-7242 to hear what can be arranged, or see our [Kaddish page](/kaddish).

### What does the person who says Kaddish for someone else need to know?
He needs the deceased's Hebrew name and the Hebrew name of the deceased's father. See [Hebrew names](/learn/hebrew-names) if you are not sure of them.
