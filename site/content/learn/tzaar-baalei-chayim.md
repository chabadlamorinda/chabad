Tzaar baalei chayim means "the suffering of living creatures," and Jewish law forbids causing animals unnecessary pain. Judaism teaches that animals are G-d's creations, and treating them with compassion is both a mitzvah and a mark of good character.

## Where it comes from

The Torah gives several laws about animals:

- **Help an overburdened animal.** If you see a donkey collapsing under its load, you must help unload it, even if it belongs to someone you dislike (Exodus 23:5).
- **Do not muzzle an ox** while it threshes grain, so it can eat while it works (Deuteronomy 25:4).
- **Let animals rest on Shabbat.** The Ten Commandments include rest for "your ox and your donkey" (Deuteronomy 5:14).
- **Send away the mother bird** before taking the eggs (Deuteronomy 22:6 to 7).
- **Do not slaughter** a mother animal and its young on the same day (Leviticus 22:28).

The Talmud concludes that preventing animal suffering is a Torah principle.

## Feed your animals first

The Talmud teaches that a person may not eat before feeding their animals, based on the verse "I will give grass in your field for your cattle, and you shall eat and be satisfied" (Deuteronomy 11:15). The animals come first in the verse, so they come first at mealtime. (You may drink before giving them water.) Pet owners can make this a daily habit.

## Character, not only rules

Jewish tradition sees kindness to animals as a training ground for kindness to people. Moses and King David were chosen to lead the Jewish people after they showed care as shepherds. The Midrash tells that when a young goat ran away from Moses' flock to drink, he carried the tired animal back on his shoulders, and G-d said: one who shows such compassion to sheep will shepherd My people.

## Kosher slaughter and compassion

Judaism permits eating meat, but within limits that respect the animal. Shechitah, kosher slaughter, uses a perfectly sharp, smooth knife and a single swift cut by a trained shochet, designed to minimize pain. Read more about [kosher meat](/learn/kosher-meat).

## Hunting and harming animals

Jewish tradition strongly discourages hunting for sport, since it causes suffering for no real need. Using animals for genuine human needs, like food, work or medical research, is permitted, but even then, unnecessary pain must be avoided. Specific questions depend on the details, so ask the Rabbi.

## Pets in a Jewish home

Having a pet is permitted, and caring for it well is a mitzvah. A few practical points:

1. **Feed them before you eat.**
2. **On Shabbat,** you may feed and water your pets, though there are limits on handling some animals; ask the Rabbi about yours.
3. **On Passover,** pet food may not contain chametz; plan ahead.
4. **When a pet dies,** the grief is real. You are welcome to call the Rabbi to talk.

## Common questions

### Does Judaism say animals have souls?
Chassidic teaching explains that every creature has a G-dly spark that gives it life, and animals have an animal soul. This is why we treat them with care and respect, even though only humans are given the mitzvahs.

### Can Jews have a dog or cat?
Yes. Owning pets is permitted, and caring for them properly is part of tzaar baalei chayim. Just keep their food free of chametz on Passover and ask about Shabbat care.

### Is it okay to kill insects or pests?
When pests cause harm or a health risk, it is permitted to remove or kill them. Avoid needless cruelty, and when you can, remove them without harm.

### Is hunting allowed in Judaism?
Hunting purely for sport is strongly discouraged in Jewish tradition because it causes suffering without a real need. For specific situations, ask the Rabbi.
