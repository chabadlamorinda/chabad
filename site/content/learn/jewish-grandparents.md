Jewish grandparents hold a special place in Jewish life as the living link between generations, passing on stories, values and traditions with a warmth only they can give. Jewish law also asks grandchildren to honor their grandparents, much as they honor their parents.

## A link in the chain

Judaism has survived for more than three thousand years because each generation passed it to the next. The Torah says, "Make them known to your children and your children's children" (Deuteronomy 4:9). The Talmud teaches that someone who teaches Torah to a grandchild is considered as if they received it at Mount Sinai.

Proverbs puts it beautifully: "Grandchildren are the crown of the elders" (17:6).

## Honoring grandparents

Jewish law teaches that grandchildren should show respect to their grandparents, though the primary duty is to parents. Parents should also model this, since children learn how to treat their own parents by watching how parents treat theirs. See [honoring parents](/learn/honoring-parents).

Ways to honor grandparents:

- Call and visit often.
- Ask for their stories and advice.
- Stand when they enter, and give them the best seat.
- Include them in celebrations and holidays.
- Help them as they age. See [caring for aging parents](/learn/caring-for-aging-parents).

## What grandparents can give

**Stories.** Tell your grandchildren where the family came from, how you celebrated holidays as a child, and the people they were named after. Family stories give children roots and pride.

**Traditions.** Share a recipe for challah or latkes, a holiday melody, or a family custom. These small things become treasured memories.

**Blessings.** On Friday night, many parents and grandparents bless the children, placing hands on their heads. Boys are blessed to be like Ephraim and Menashe, girls like Sarah, Rebecca, Rachel and Leah, and all with the Priestly Blessing.

**Jewish gifts.** A child's siddur, a Jewish storybook, a tzedakah box, a pair of Shabbat candlesticks for a daughter or granddaughter, or tzitzit for a boy are gifts with lasting meaning.

**A living example.** Children notice what grandparents do. Lighting Shabbat candles, saying a blessing before eating, or giving tzedakah together teaches more than any lecture.

## Long-distance grandparents

Even from far away, you can light Shabbat candles at the same time, read the weekly [parsha](/learn/weekly-parsha) together by video, or send holiday packages. Some grandparents write an [ethical will](/learn/ethical-will), a letter sharing their values for the family to keep.

## When families are diverse

Many grandparents today have grandchildren growing up with different levels of observance. Warmth and respect go a long way. Inviting grandchildren to a seder, a Chanukah lighting or a Shabbat meal can open a door with love, never pressure.

## Common questions

### Am I obligated to honor my grandparents?
Yes. Jewish law teaches that one should honor grandparents, though the obligation toward parents comes first. In practice, warmth, respect and regular contact are what matter most.

### How can I give my grandchildren a Jewish identity if their parents are not observant?
Lead with love and respect for the parents' choices. Share holidays, stories and small mitzvahs when invited. If you have questions, the Rabbi is happy to talk with you.

### Can grandparents bless their grandchildren?
Yes. A grandparent's blessing is precious. You can use the traditional Friday night blessing or simply bless them in your own words.

### What is a good Jewish gift for a grandchild's bar or bat mitzvah?
Something lasting: tefillin for a boy, Shabbat candlesticks for a girl, a set of Jewish books, or a gift in multiples of 18. See our guides to [bar mitzvah](/learn/bar-mitzvah) and [bat mitzvah](/learn/bat-mitzvah).
