The Lubavitcher Rebbe, Rabbi Menachem M. Schneerson, taught that Chanukah is the holiday of bringing light outside: from the menorah in the doorway to the street, and from each home to the world around it. His Chanukah teachings return again and again to three ideas: add light every day, never be afraid to shine in the dark, and make every home a place of Torah, prayer and kindness.

## 1. Light that goes outside

In a well-known discourse in *Likkutei Sichot*, the Rebbe contrasts the Menorah of the Temple with the Chanukah lights. The Temple Menorah was lit inside the Sanctuary, in the daytime, where holiness was open and visible. The Chanukah lights are lit at the doorway, outside, after sunset, to light up the darkness of exile.

The Rebbe explained that this is why the Chanukah menorah has eight lights and not seven. Seven is the perfection of the natural world, and eight is a light that goes beyond it, the kind of light needed to brighten the darkest places. You can read the comparison in [the Temple menorah and the Chanukah menorah](/learn/temple-menorah-and-chanukah-menorah).

## 2. A light no decree can stop

In the same discourse, the Rebbe points out something striking. Some mitzvot can be prevented by a harsh government, because a Jew who refuses to give them up may be in danger. But the mitzvah of Chanukah can always be done: even in the hardest circumstances, one candle at the dinner table fulfills it. In this way, no decree can stop Chanukah. The Rebbe explained that the mitzvah is rooted in the soul's bond with G-d's essence, which no sin or exile can break.

This is a teaching of courage. A very small flame can be kept anywhere. See also [menorahs in dark times](/learn/menorahs-in-dark-times).

## 3. One more light each night

In a talk on the eighth night, the Rebbe emphasized that the Chanukah menorah belongs at the doorway, outside, in every place and in every time, and that it lights both the home and its surroundings. He also taught that we add a candle each night to show steady growth "from strength to strength," in Torah and in mitzvot.

For the rules and customs of lighting, see [the Chanukah menorah](/learn/chanukah-menorah) and [lessons of Chanukah](/learn/lessons-of-chanukah).

## 4. Every home a Chabad House

On the fifth, sixth and seventh nights of Chanukah in 1987, the Rebbe spoke to children and adults about a very practical mission. He asked children to request from their parents a symbolic "spiritual Chanukah gelt": to make their own room, and ideally the whole house, a place of prayer, learning and charity. Children can begin by saying *Modeh Ani* each morning, keeping a *pushkah* (charity box) in their room, and having a siddur or Chumash. Married adults, he said, should work together to make their home a Chabad House, a place of Torah, prayer and acts of kindness.

The Rebbe also called for public menorahs in visible places in every city and town, to publicize the miracle, and to use those lightings to invite people to light at home. You can read about this in [public menorah lightings](/learn/public-menorah-lighting) and [the history of the menorah campaign](/learn/menorah-campaign-history).

## Common questions

### What was the Rebbe's main message about Chanukah?
That the light of Chanukah must go outside: from the doorway to the street, from the home to the world, and that we should add light each day.

### Why did the Rebbe encourage public menorah lightings?
To publicize the miracle in public places in every city and town, and to invite people to kindle the lights at home as well.

### What is "every home a Chabad House"?
It is the Rebbe's call from 1987 to make every home, and every child's room, a place of Torah, prayer and charity.

### What is spiritual Chanukah gelt?
It is the symbolic gift the Rebbe asked children to request from their parents: a home that is a place of prayer, learning and tzedakah.
