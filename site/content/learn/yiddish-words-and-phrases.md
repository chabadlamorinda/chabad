Yiddish is the everyday language Ashkenazi Jews developed over a thousand years, a mix of Germanic words with Hebrew, Aramaic and Slavic words, written in Hebrew letters. Words like mensch, kvetch and chutzpah are Yiddish, and so are the warm greetings "gut Shabbos" and "mazel tov."

## Where Yiddish came from

Yiddish (יידיש) means "Jewish." It began in the 9th and 10th centuries among Jews who settled along the Rhine in what is now Germany, and it spread east to Poland, Russia and beyond. Hebrew, the holy tongue, stayed the language of prayer and Torah, while Yiddish became the language of home, market and street.

Before World War II about 13 million people spoke Yiddish. The Holocaust and assimilation cut that number sharply, but Yiddish is alive today, especially in Chassidic communities. Many bubbes and zaydes in the Bay Area, and many Russian-speaking families in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda, remember a grandparent who spoke it.

## Family and people

- **Bubbe** (באבע): grandmother. **Zayde** (זיידע): grandfather.
- **Mishpacha** (משפּחה): family, from the Hebrew.
- **Kinder**: children.
- **Naches** (נחת): the deep pride and joy parents and grandparents feel from their children. We say "may you have naches."
- **Mensch** (מענטש): literally "person." A real mensch is a decent, caring, honorable human being, the highest compliment.
- **Haimish**: homey, warm and unpretentious.

## Food

- **Challah**: the braided Shabbat bread. **Kugel**: a baked noodle or potato pudding.
- **Latkes**: potato pancakes for Chanukah. **Kreplach**: dumplings. **Knish**: a filled pastry.
- **Cholent**: the slow-cooked Shabbat stew. **Bagel** and **lox** are Yiddish words too.

## Feelings and everyday life

- **Kvell**: to glow with pride. **Kvetch**: to complain. 
- **Schlep**: to drag or haul something a long way. **Schmooze**: to chat warmly.
- **Chutzpah**: nerve, boldness. **Klutz**: a clumsy person. **Nudnik**: a bore who keeps pestering.
- **Meshuga**: crazy. **Oy vey**: a sigh that fits good news and bad.

## Shabbat, shul and blessings

- **Gut Shabbos** (גוט שבת): "have a good Shabbat." **Gut yom tov**: have a good holiday.
- **Mazel tov** (מזל טוב): congratulations, literally "good fortune."
- **A gezunt** (אַ געזונט): "to your health," said when someone sneezes or recovers.
- **L'chaim** (לחיים): "to life," the toast.
- **Shul**: synagogue. **Davening**: praying. **Bentching**: saying the blessing after a meal.
- **Kiddush**: the blessing over wine, and the small meal after Shabbat services. 

## Does Yiddish matter today?

Yes. Jewish teaching sees Yiddish as having a measure of holiness, because Jews used it for centuries to study Torah and live the mitzvahs. It also keeps a way of speaking, humor and warmth alive. Learning a few words is a lovely way to connect with a grandparent, and to teach children where they come from. ## Common questions

### What does mensch mean in Yiddish?
A mensch is a decent, kind and honorable person. It literally means "person," and it is one of the highest compliments in Yiddish.

### What does kvetch mean?
To kvetch is to complain, often repeatedly. A "kvetcher" is someone who does it a lot.

### What does "gut Shabbos" mean?
"Gut Shabbos" means "have a good Shabbat." Jews say it to each other from Friday afternoon through Shabbat.

### What does naches mean?
Naches is the joy and pride a parent or grandparent feels from a child's good deeds and happiness.

### Is Yiddish the same as Hebrew?
No. Hebrew is the ancient holy tongue, while Yiddish grew up in Europe from German-based speech mixed with Hebrew, Aramaic and Slavic words. Both are written in Hebrew letters.
