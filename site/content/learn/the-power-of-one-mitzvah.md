According to the Tanya, a mitzvah is not only a good deed: it is the one way a person can truly be joined to G-d, because a mitzvah expresses G-d's own inner will. Even a single mitzvah, done with a physical object or a simple act, draws G-d's light into the world.

That makes the smallest act, a coin in a pushka, a blessing before a meal or a Shabbat candle, matter more than it looks. Here is what the Tanya teaches, in plain words, for families in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda.

## Why only a mitzvah can connect us

Rabbi Naftali Silberberg's "Let's Talk Tanya" class on Chapter 35 opens with the verse the Tanya is built around: "For the matter is very near to you, in your mouth and in your heart, that you may do it." Thought, speech and action are the three "garments" of the soul, and action is the climax of the verse.

The class explains that creation expresses G-d's will through many layers of concealment, while mitzvot express His inner will with no concealment. That is why only mitzvot can truly be one with Him. The Alter Rebbe, following the Zohar, compares the body to a wick and good deeds to oil: the light of the Shechinah rests on a person through good deeds. The soul alone cannot merge fully with G-d, since even a tzaddik's love implies a lover and a beloved.

## A mitzvah creates its own reward

Chapter 37 of the Tanya says: unlike a field owner who pays a worker with money the worker did not make, "a mitzvah actually creates its own reward." Through a mitzvah, a person draws G-d's infinite light downward into physical matter.

Chapter 37 also says that the object of a mitzvah, such as the parchment of tefillin or a kosher etrog, is lifted into holiness. And even the energy a person gets from food, if it gave strength for a mitzvah, is lifted too. See [Serving G-d in Everyday Life](/learn/serving-g-d-in-everyday-life).

## Why every mitzvah counts

Chapter 4 describes the soul as having 613 "organs," matching the 613 mitzvot. When a person does a mitzvah, the corresponding soul-organ is "clothed." Chapter 4 says that omitting even one mitzvah leaves its corresponding organ without its garment, which is why the Alter Rebbe stresses the word "all."

Do not hear this as pressure. The chapter's point is that each mitzvah has its own place and its own worth, and that no mitzvah is minor. Where should you begin? Ask the Rabbi. See [what is a mitzvah](/learn/what-is-a-mitzvah) and [why mitzvot](/learn/why-mitzvot).

## It is not about how you feel

The class on Chapter 35 notes that the Tanya's key verse ends with "that you may do it." Practical observance matters most, even though love and awe are also "very near." So you do not need a special feeling first. Chapter 4 says love and awe are the roots of the mitzvot, and the class shows how the mitzvah itself pulls the whole person, even the physical body, into holiness: it describes the physical strength used in putting on tefillin being absorbed into holiness.

This connects to the [beinoni](/learn/the-beinoni): a person who still struggles inside yet keeps choosing the right action.

## Common questions

### Why does one mitzvah matter?
The Tanya teaches that a mitzvah expresses G-d's inner will, so doing one joins a person to G-d and draws His light into the physical world. Chapter 37 says a mitzvah actually creates its own reward.

### What does the Tanya say about mitzvot?
Chapter 4 calls mitzvot the "organs of the King" and the means to cling to G-d. Chapter 37 says every mitzvah lifts the physical world toward holiness. The Chapter 35 page says only mitzvot can truly connect us to G-d.

### Do I have to feel something to do a mitzvah?
No. The verse the Tanya is built on says "that you may do it," and practical action is its climax. Feelings of love and awe are also "very near," but the deed itself matters most.

### Is a small mitzvah worth doing if I cannot do them all?
Chapter 4 says each mitzvah has its own place, and Chapter 37 says even doing a mitzvah with a physical object lifts that object into holiness. Start with one and ask the Rabbi what to add.

### What happens when you do a mitzvah?
According to the Tanya, you draw G-d's light into the physical world, the object and your own energy are lifted into holiness, and you are joined to G-d's will. This does not mean that mitzvot must feel mystical.
