The Baal Shem Tov, the founder of the Chassidic movement, taught that giving should be done with joy, and Chassidic stories about him show tzedakah changing the lives of giver and receiver. Chabad.org shares his teaching about generous giving and two classic tales, both retold below in our own words.

## A teaching: giving with joy

Chabad.org's article "The Mystical Act of Charity" (Rabbi Dr. Eliezer Shore, from the teachings of the Baal Shem Tov) notes that the Baal Shem Tov gave more than a fifth of his income to charity. The Talmud (Ketubot 50a) sets a fifth as the limit for one who wants to be generous. When asked, he explained that the Hebrew word for "to be generous," *bizbez*, comes from the word for "booty," *biza*. A person who gives without joy is like someone robbing himself, so the Sages set a limit. But one who gives joyfully, and takes pleasure in giving, has no set amount.

The lesson: it is the spirit of giving that matters. See [Maaser](/learn/maaser) for the usual standard.

## A story: a tale of two cows

Chabad.org's "A Tale of Two Cows" by Rabbi Yerachmiel Tilles tells of Reb Zev Wolf Kitzes, the Baal Shem Tov's constant companion. On a visit to a poor villager, the Baal Shem Tov asked for a donation of eighteen rubles for an important cause. The man did not have it, so he sold his furniture and his cow. Reb Zev Wolf watched in silence.

Soon the villager could not pay his rent and was evicted. He moved to another village and bought a new cow, scraping by on her milk. When the local squire's cow fell ill, the squire's servant bought the villager's milk, and the squire became a steady customer. The squire came to trust his honesty, and being childless, he eventually gave him his entire estate before going abroad.

Years later Reb Zev Wolf was collecting for Jewish prisoners and still needed three hundred rubles. The wealthy landlord, the same villager, recognized him and gave it all. Reb Zev Wolf then understood. The Baal Shem Tov explained that the villager's good fortune lay elsewhere, and the large gift had pushed him to start again.

## A story: Avigdor of Brody

Chabad.org's "Lost in the Mail for Sixteen Years," by Shaul Wertheimer, tells of a wealthy man, Avigdor, who brought a large sum to the Baal Shem Tov to distribute to the poor. The Baal Shem Tov offered a blessing, and Avigdor proudly refused, saying he had everything. The Baal Shem Tov asked him only to deliver a sealed letter to the head of Brody's charity committee. Avigdor put it in his jacket and forgot it.

Sixteen years later, Avigdor's fortune collapsed. Selling his old clothes, he found the letter. He hurried to Mr. Tzaddok, the charity chairman, a former tailor who had become wealthy and generous. The letter said Avigdor had fallen because of his pride, asked Tzaddok to help him, and promised he would prosper again, this time with humility. As a sign, it said Tzaddok's wife would give birth that day. While they read it, someone ran in with the news of a baby boy. Avigdor regained his wealth and remained humble.

A well-known story; its details cannot be confirmed. Both tales are classic Chassidic stories, told on Chabad.org for what they teach: give with joy, and give with humility.

## What these stories share

- Giving is about the heart, not only the amount.
- Giving that costs something is never forgotten.
- Humility matters: the giver is a messenger, not the owner.

## Common questions

### Did the Baal Shem Tov give a lot of charity?
Chabad.org's article on charity notes that he gave more than a fifth of his income, and that he said joyful giving has no set limit.

### Why is there a limit of a fifth in Jewish law?
The Talmud sets it so that a person does not impoverish himself. The Baal Shem Tov taught that one who gives joyfully is not limited the same way. Ask the Rabbi about your own situation.

### Are these Baal Shem Tov stories true?
They are well-known Chassidic stories told on Chabad.org; their details cannot be confirmed historically. The teaching about giving more than a fifth is cited from the Baal Shem Tov's teachings.

### What is the message of the tale of two cows?
That a gift given with sacrifice is not forgotten, and that the effect of giving can come back in unexpected ways.

### What is the message of the Avigdor story?
That wealth is a trust and humility is its guardian.

### How can I give with joy?
Start with something small and regular, like a coin in a pushka each day. See [The Pushka](/learn/pushka).
