Chapter 1 of the Tanya begins with a Talmud teaching: before a Jew is born, a heavenly oath is administered, "Be righteous and be not wicked." The chapter then asks what this means, describes the three types of people, and introduces the two souls.

This guide walks through the chapter in short steps, so you know what to expect when you read it yourself.

## Step 1: An oath before birth

The chapter opens with a teaching from the Talmud (Niddah 30b). Before a person is born, an oath is administered: "Be righteous and be not wicked." The same oath tells the soul to regard itself as wicked, even if the whole world calls it righteous. Chassidic teaching adds that the soul descends to fulfil a specific mission.

## Step 2: The question

The author immediately asks a question, which is how the Tanya often works. The Mishnah (Avot 2:13) says "Be not wicked in your own estimation," which seems to contradict the oath. And if you see yourself as wicked, you may become sad and discouraged, which makes it hard to serve G-d with joy. But if you are not troubled by it, you may become careless about wrongdoing.

When you read this for the first time, stop here and notice the question. Learning Tanya is about asking.

## Step 3: Five kinds of people

The chapter brings in a Talmud passage (Berachot 7a) that lists five categories: the complete tzaddik, the incomplete tzaddik, the rasha who prospers, the rasha who suffers, and the beinoni, the intermediate. It then explains that a beinoni is not simply "half good, half bad." The Talmud's "half and half" is a legal way of describing how a person is judged. See [the beinoni](/learn/the-beinoni) and the [glossary](/learn/tanya-glossary-for-beginners).

## Step 4: Two souls

Next, the chapter says that every Jew has two souls. The first soul gives life to the body, and the chapter describes the pulls it can bring: anger and pride, the love of pleasure, frivolity and idle talk, laziness and gloom. But the chapter also says the Jewish animal soul has good natural traits, such as compassion and kindness. See [is the animal soul bad?](/learn/animal-soul-not-evil).

The second soul is explained in Chapter 2. Read [a part of G-d above](/learn/part-of-g-d-above) and [the two souls explained](/learn/the-two-souls-explained).

## How to read it

Our suggestion, not a rule:

- Read a few lines, then stop and ask: what is the question here?
- Do not worry if the Hebrew terms are new; use the glossary.
- Read the chapter twice, once to get the shape, once for the details.
- Take your questions to a class or a partner. See [how to start learning Tanya](/learn/how-to-start-learning-tanya).

## Common questions

### What does Tanya Chapter 1 say?
It begins with the oath before birth, "Be righteous and be not wicked," asks how that fits with being "not wicked in your own estimation," discusses tzaddik, rasha and beinoni, and introduces the two souls.

### What is the oath before birth in Tanya?
It is a teaching from the Talmud (Niddah 30b) that before birth a soul is made to swear: "Be righteous and be not wicked."

### Who is the beinoni in Chapter 1?
The beinoni is the "intermediate" person. Chapter 1 explains that the title does not mean half good, half bad.

### Does Chapter 1 say people have good in them?
Yes. It says the Jewish animal soul also has good natural traits such as compassion and kindness.

### Is Chapter 1 hard to understand?
Some of the terms are new, but the questions it asks are ones everyone has. Read it slowly with the glossary, and with a teacher if you can.
