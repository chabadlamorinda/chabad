According to the Tanya, Torah study is special because the Torah is G-d's own wisdom and will, so when you understand a teaching of Torah your mind literally takes in the wisdom of G-d. The Tanya calls this "a most wonderful unity," something that has no parallel anywhere else in the physical world.

This is the answer to a question many people in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda ask: "Why should I spend time on ancient texts?" The Tanya, written by Rabbi Schneur Zalman of Liadi, gives a reason that has nothing to do with being an expert.

## Torah is more than information

Chapter 4 of the Tanya quotes the Zohar: "Torah and the Holy One, blessed be He, are entirely one." The Torah is not only a book about G-d. It is G-d's wisdom and will, which are infinite and beyond human grasp. The chapter teaches that G-d "condensed" this wisdom into the mitzvot, their laws, the letters of Scripture and the teachings of the Sages, so that people can reach it and connect with Him.

The chapter likes the picture of water. Water descends from a high place to a low place and remains the same water. In the same way, Torah came down through the spiritual worlds until it was clothed in letters and in physical objects. The chapter also quotes the Sages: "Where you find the greatness of the Holy One, blessed be He, there you find His humility."

## The mind and the subject hold each other

Chapter 5 explains how a human mind can "grasp" something so great. When your mind understands an idea, it encompasses that idea, and the idea is also clothed within the mind. A Halachah (a Jewish law) has two sides: its reasoning is G-d's wisdom and its ruling is G-d's will. When a student reaches a conclusion from the Torah's own method, his mind grasps G-d's will and wisdom, which are otherwise beyond any thought.

That is why the Tanya calls it a wonderful unity. A verdict reached by any other legal system, says the chapter, is only human knowledge.

## Food for the soul

Chapter 5 says that all mitzvot surround the soul with Divine light, from head to foot, like a garment. Torah study does that too, and it also fills the soul from within, because the student's mind takes in the wisdom. This is why Torah is called the soul's "bread" and "food." The chapter quotes the verse "Your Torah is in my inner parts." It adds that each person does this according to his own capacity, using the four levels of understanding (peshat, remez, derush and sod).

The Sages said that Torah study equals all the other mitzvot, and Chapter 5 explains why: it has both qualities, the food that nourishes from inside and the garment that surrounds from outside. Speaking the words aloud adds more.

## What "for its own sake" means

Chapter 5 defines studying "for its own sake" as studying to bind your soul to G-d through understanding Torah, each person according to his own mind. You do not have to be a scholar. You have to study with the intention to connect.

## Study and action

Chapter 4 teaches that the soul expresses itself in thought, speech and action. Studying Torah is the speech and thought side, and doing the mitzvot is the action side. See [the power of one mitzvah](/learn/the-power-of-one-mitzvah) for more on action.

## Common questions

### Why is Torah study so important in Judaism?
The Tanya teaches that the Torah is G-d's own wisdom and will, so understanding it joins the human mind to G-d in a way nothing else does. It also says Torah study is equal to all the other mitzvot combined.

### What does the Tanya say about studying Torah?
Chapters 4 and 5 say that Torah and G-d are "entirely one," and that when the mind grasps Torah it forms a "most wonderful unity" with G-d's wisdom. Torah is also the "food" of the soul.

### Do I need to be a scholar to study Torah?
No. Chapter 5 says each person studies according to his own capacity, and that studying "for its own sake" means studying to bind your soul to G-d through whatever you understand.

### Is Torah study more important than doing mitzvot?
Chapter 37 of the Tanya says Torah study unites the soul with G-d more deeply, but that "the essential thing is not study but deed." If you are the only one who can perform a mitzvah, you interrupt your study to do it. Ask the Rabbi about specific cases.
