The Four Questions, known by their opening words Mah Nishtanah ("What is different?"), are asked near the start of the Passover seder, usually by the youngest child. They point out what makes this night unusual and open the telling of the story of the Exodus.

## Why we ask questions

The Torah commands parents to tell their children the story of leaving Egypt, and it describes the child asking, "What is this?" The whole seder is built to spark questions: the plate is moved, the matzah is broken, we dip foods. When a child asks, the answer becomes a real conversation, not a lecture.

The Four Questions come right after the second cup of wine is poured.

## The Four Questions in Chabad order

Chabad follows the order of the Rambam, which begins with dipping. Other Haggadahs list them in a different order and some include a question about roasted meat.

**Introduction:** Mah nishtanah halailah hazeh mikol haleilot? "Why is this night different from all other nights?"

1. **Dipping:** Shebechol haleilot ein anu matbilin afilu pa'am echat, halailah hazeh shtei pe'amim. "On all nights we do not dip even once; tonight we dip twice." (Karpas in salt water, and maror in charoset.)
2. **Matzah:** Shebechol haleilot anu ochlin chametz o matzah, halailah hazeh kulo matzah. "On all nights we eat chametz or matzah; tonight only matzah."
3. **Bitter herbs:** Shebechol haleilot anu ochlin she'ar yerakot, halailah hazeh maror. "On all nights we eat other vegetables; tonight, maror."
4. **Reclining:** Shebechol haleilot anu ochlin bein yoshvin uvein mesubin, halailah hazeh kulanu mesubin. "On all nights we eat sitting upright or reclining; tonight we all recline."

## Who asks

- **The youngest child** who is able traditionally asks first.
- **Every child** can then ask, and in many Chabad homes each child takes a turn, often adding a translation in their own language.
- **Adults ask too.** If there are no children, a spouse asks. Even a person alone at a seder asks the questions aloud to themselves.

A Chabad custom is for children to begin in Yiddish: "Tatte, ich vel bai dir fregen fir kashes," "Father, I would like to ask you four questions."

## The answer

The Haggadah answers right away with Avadim hayinu: "We were slaves to Pharaoh in Egypt, and the L-rd our G-d took us out from there with a strong hand and an outstretched arm." The rest of the Haggadah explains each practice: matzah for the haste of leaving, maror for the bitterness of slavery, dipping and reclining for freedom.

## Tips for parents

1. **Practice before Passover** so the child feels confident.
2. **Praise every question**, not only the four. Some families give a small treat for each new question at the table.
3. **Let kids act it out** with props, such as a bag on their back like leaving Egypt.
4. **Share the honor.** If there are several children, let each one ask in turn.

## Common questions

### Is it really four questions?
Technically there is one question, "Why is this night different?", followed by four observations. The tradition calls them the four questions, or "fir kashes" in Yiddish.

### Do I have to say them in Hebrew?
No. Saying them in any language you understand fulfills the purpose, which is to ask and to understand. Many say both Hebrew and English.

### What if I am the only one at the seder?
Ask yourself the questions aloud and answer them. Even great scholars do this when alone. Better still, contact us and we will try to help you find a seder.

### Why do we not ask about the shank bone or the egg?
The questions focus on the actions that make the night unusual. Earlier versions included a question about eating roasted meat from the Passover offering, which was dropped after the Temple was destroyed.
