Kosher certification is a service in which a rabbinic agency, such as the OU, OK or Star-K, checks every ingredient, machine and process in a food plant or kitchen and then allows its symbol on the product. The symbol tells you that people trained in Jewish law are standing behind what is inside.

## Why certification is needed

A generation ago, a careful shopper could read a label and know if a food was kosher. Today, even simple foods pass through complex factories. A cracker may contain flavorings, emulsifiers and oils from dozens of suppliers, and it may be made on equipment also used for non-kosher or dairy products. Only someone who sees the whole process can know. Read the basics in our guide to [kosher food](/learn/kosher).

## How an agency certifies a product

1. **Application.** A company asks an agency to certify its plant or kitchen.
2. **Ingredient review.** Every ingredient and its source is checked. Each must itself be kosher, often certified by an approved agency.
3. **Equipment review.** The agency checks what else runs on the same lines. If equipment was used for non-kosher food, it must be made kosher (*kashered*), often with high heat, under supervision.
4. **Classification.** Each product is marked as meat, dairy (D) or pareve, and possibly kosher for Passover (P).
5. **Supervision.** A *mashgiach*, a trained kosher supervisor, visits the plant. Some plants are checked on surprise visits; others, such as meat plants and restaurants, need a supervisor present at all times.
6. **The contract.** The company may print the agency's trademarked symbol only on approved products, and the agency can withdraw it.

## What a mashgiach does

A mashgiach checks deliveries and ingredients, watches over kashering of equipment, makes sure no unapproved products enter the kitchen, and in restaurants often checks vegetables for insects and lights the ovens and stoves. In meat production, trained *shochtim* (ritual slaughterers) and inspectors work under the agency's rabbis. See our guide to [kosher meat](/learn/kosher-meat).

## The big national agencies

Some of the most widely seen agencies in American stores are the **OU** (Orthodox Union), **OK**, **Star-K**, **Kof-K** and the **cRc** (Chicago Rabbinical Council). They certify many thousands of companies around the world. There are also local agencies, often run by a city's rabbinical council, which supervise restaurants, bakeries and caterers in their area. Our guide to [kosher symbols](/learn/kosher-symbols) shows what these marks look like and how to read the letters beside them.

## Why standards differ

All reliable agencies follow Jewish law, but on some questions there are several accepted opinions. Agencies differ on matters such as dairy ingredients, certain additives, *glatt* meat, and Jewish involvement in cooking (*bishul Yisrael*). Some communities follow stricter standards for certain products. If you see an unfamiliar symbol, ask the Rabbi whether it is reliable.

## Common questions

### Does kosher certification mean a rabbi blessed the food?
No. Certification is about supervision, not blessing. The food is kosher because of its ingredients and how it was made, which the agency has checked.

### Who pays for kosher certification?
The company pays the agency a fee for the service, much like any other inspection or quality program. For most products, the cost per item is tiny.

### Is a product without a symbol not kosher?
Not necessarily. Some simple products, like many fresh fruits and vegetables, do not need a symbol. Processed foods without certification cannot be assumed kosher. Ask the Rabbi about a specific product.

### How can a restaurant or caterer become kosher?
It needs an agency, usually a local one, to review the kitchen, kasher the equipment and provide ongoing supervision. If you are planning a kosher event, call the Rabbi for guidance.
