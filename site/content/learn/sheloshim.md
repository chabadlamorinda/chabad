Sheloshim is the 30-day period of mourning that begins on the day of the burial and includes the week of shiva. After shiva ends, mourners return to work and daily life, but keep certain mourning customs until the 30th day.

## What is sheloshim?

*Sheloshim* (also spelled shloshim) means "thirty" in Hebrew. The Torah tells us that the Jewish people mourned Moses and Aaron for thirty days, and Jewish law set this as the second stage of mourning.

Jewish mourning moves in gentle stages, each a little lighter than the one before:

1. **[Aninut](/learn/aninut):** from death until burial.
2. **[Shiva](/learn/shiva):** the first seven days after burial.
3. **Sheloshim:** through the 30th day after burial.
4. **The [year of mourning](/learn/year-of-mourning):** for a parent, mourning continues for twelve months.

This structure lets mourners grieve fully at first and then slowly return to life.

## How sheloshim is counted

Sheloshim begins on the day of the burial, which counts as day one. It ends on the morning of the 30th day, shortly after sunrise. Shabbat days are counted.

A major Jewish holiday that falls during this time can shorten or end the sheloshim. The rules depend on exact timing, so ask the Rabbi about your dates.

## Customs during sheloshim

After shiva, mourners go back to work and leave the house, but during sheloshim they traditionally:

- **Do not cut their hair or shave.**
- **Do not buy or wear new clothing.**
- **Avoid parties, celebrations and live music.**
- **Do not attend weddings or similar festive meals.** For a family wedding or another important occasion, ask the Rabbi.
- **Continue saying [Kaddish](/learn/kaddish)** at prayer services.

## Kaddish during and after sheloshim

For a spouse, sibling or child, the main period of mourning, including Kaddish, ends with sheloshim. For a parent, Kaddish continues for eleven months, and some mourning customs continue for a full year.

## Marking the end of sheloshim

Many families gather near the end of sheloshim to honor their loved one. Common ways to mark it:

- **A siyum.** Friends and family divide up the Mishnah or other Torah texts to study in the person's memory and complete them by the 30th day. The Hebrew letters of the word *Mishnah* are the same as the word *neshamah*, soul, so studying Mishnah is a traditional gift to the soul.
- **A memorial gathering.** People share memories, words of Torah and a meal.
- **Tzedakah.** Giving charity in the person's memory brings merit to their soul.
- **A grave visit.** Some families visit the grave at the end of sheloshim. See our guide to [visiting a grave](/learn/visiting-a-grave).

## Common questions

### Can a mourner go back to work during sheloshim?
Yes. Once shiva ends, mourners return to work and normal responsibilities. Only certain celebrations and personal grooming are limited.

### When can a mourner for a parent get a haircut?
For a parent, the custom is to wait longer than thirty days, until friends comment that the mourner looks unkempt. Ask the Rabbi for guidance in your situation.

### Is there a special gathering required at the end of sheloshim?
It is not required, but it is a meaningful custom. Even a small gathering with some learning and shared memories is a beautiful tribute.

### What if I only learned of the death after some time?
If you heard the news within thirty days, mourning customs apply for the rest of the period. The rules are detailed, so ask the Rabbi.
