"Where is G-d?" "Can G-d see me when I hide?" "Why can't I see Him?" Children ask the biggest questions in the smallest moments: in the car, at bath time, right before lights out. Many parents freeze, worried they do not know enough to answer. You need honesty, warmth and a few simple ideas that Jewish tradition has handed down for thousands of years.

## You are already the right teacher

The Torah says, "You shall teach them to your children, and speak of them when you sit at home, when you walk on the way, when you lie down and when you rise." The best conversations about G-d happen in ordinary life, and the person a child wants to hear from is you. A parent who says "I wonder about that too; let's think about it together" teaches more than one who pretends to know everything.

## Simple answers that are also true

Chassidic teaching has a gift for saying deep things simply, and children understand them beautifully.

- **Where is G-d?** Everywhere. There is no place without Him: in our house, in the park, in Israel, in the sky. Chassidic teaching reminds children that G-d is with them wherever they go, so they are never alone.
- **Can G-d see me?** Yes, always, and with love, like a parent watching a child sleep. Not to catch us, but because He cares about every single thing we do.
- **Why can't I see G-d?** Can you see love? Can you see the wind? We cannot see them, but we feel them and we see what they do. G-d is like that, only much more. We see Him in the world He made, in the people who are kind to us, and in the good feeling inside when we do a mitzvah.
- **Does G-d hear me?** Yes. You can talk to Him any time, in any words, out loud or in your heart. See [talking to G-d in your own words](/learn/talking-to-g-d-in-your-own-words).

Keep your answers short. A child who wants more will ask more.

## Use everyday moments

A toddler who sees a rainbow, a sunset or a newborn puppy is already full of wonder; add one sentence: "Look what Hashem made." When a child is scared at night, remind them that G-d is right there with them. When something good happens, say "Thank You, Hashem" out loud.

## Blessings: a small habit with a big message

Every blessing says, in a child's language, "this came from G-d." [Modeh Ani](/learn/modeh-ani) in the morning teaches that G-d gave us a new day. A blessing before an apple teaches that He made the apple. The [Shema at bedtime](/learn/bedtime-shema) teaches that He is one and He is ours. See [teaching children to pray](/learn/teaching-children-to-pray).

## Tell stories

Children think in stories. Abraham discovering one G-d as a boy, Moses at the burning bush, Daniel in the lions' den, Chanah praying quietly for a child: these are our family stories, and they say more about G-d than any definition. The weekly [parsha](/learn/weekly-parsha) gives you a new one every Shabbat.

## When you have doubts yourself

Many parents say, "How can I teach faith when I am not sure what I believe?" Judaism welcomes questions. You can tell your child honestly, "Some things about G-d are hard to understand, even for grown-ups, and we keep learning." Then keep learning: [belief in G-d](/learn/belief-in-g-d) and [why bad things happen](/learn/why-bad-things-happen) are good places to start, and the Rabbi is always glad to talk.

## Common questions

### My child asked why G-d let someone get sick. What do I say?
Be honest that we do not always understand G-d's ways, and that it is all right to be sad and even to ask G-d "why." Then turn to what we can do: pray, help, and be kind. See [talking to children about death](/learn/talking-to-kids-about-death) when the question is about a loss.

### Should I say "G-d" or "Hashem"?
Either is fine. Many Jewish families say "Hashem" ("the Name") in everyday talk, out of respect, and children pick it up easily. What matters is speaking about G-d naturally and warmly.

### My child says G-d is not real. Should I worry?
No. Children test ideas by saying them out loud. Stay calm, ask what made them think that, and share what you believe without pressure. A home where G-d is talked about kindly and lived through mitzvot speaks louder than any argument.
