A mitzvah is a commandment from G-d in the Torah. Jews do mitzvot because they are G-d's will, and because each mitzvah creates a real connection between the person, G-d and the world.

## What is a mitzvah?

The Torah contains [613 mitzvot](/learn/613-mitzvot). Some are things to do, like lighting Shabbat candles or giving tzedakah. Others are things to avoid, like stealing or eating non-kosher food.

The word *mitzvah* means "commandment." Chassidic teaching points out that it is related to the Aramaic word *tzavta*, meaning "connection." Every mitzvah links the person who does it with G-d, who commanded it.

## Different kinds of mitzvot

Jewish tradition groups the mitzvot into three kinds:

- **Mishpatim (laws):** mitzvot that make clear sense, like honesty and not hurting others. Society would arrive at them on its own.
- **Edot (testimonies):** mitzvot that remind us of events and ideas, like Passover, Shabbat and tefillin. We understand them once they are explained.
- **Chukim (decrees):** mitzvot whose reasons are beyond human understanding, like the laws of kosher or the red heifer.

## Why do mitzvot we do not understand?

We can and should look for meaning in every mitzvah. Learning the reasons adds depth and feeling. But the main reason we do a mitzvah is that G-d asked us to.

Think of a loving relationship. When someone you love asks for something, you do it because it matters to them, not only because you agree with the logic. Doing what G-d asks, even beyond our understanding, expresses the deepest bond.

## A home for G-d in this world

The Midrash teaches that G-d desired a dwelling place in the lower worlds. The [Tanya](/learn/tanya) explains that this is the purpose of all creation.

Mitzvot are done with physical things: leather for tefillin, wool for tzitzit, flour for challah, money for tzedakah. When we use a physical object for a mitzvah, it becomes a vessel for G-dliness. Bit by bit, the world becomes a home for G-d. That is what each mitzvah accomplishes.

## Mitzvot make us better people

Many mitzvot also shape our character: Shabbat teaches rest and gratitude, tzedakah teaches generosity, kosher teaches self-discipline. Over time, mitzvot help us become kinder, more thoughtful and more connected.

## How to begin

You do not need to do everything at once. Judaism is not all or nothing.

1. **Pick one mitzvah** that speaks to you.
2. **Learn about it** so it has meaning.
3. **Do it consistently,** even in a small way.
4. **Add another** when you are ready.

The Mishnah teaches, "One mitzvah brings another." Each step makes the next one easier.

## Common questions

### If I cannot keep all the mitzvot, is there a point in keeping some?
Absolutely. Every single mitzvah has value on its own. G-d treasures each one.

### Do mitzvot need to be done with feeling?
Feeling and intention make a mitzvah richer, and we should try to have them. But the action itself counts, even on days the feeling is not there.

### Do non-Jews have mitzvot?
Yes. Non-Jews are commanded in the seven [Noahide laws](/learn/noahide-laws), which form a foundation for a moral, G-dly society.

### Is it hypocritical to do a mitzvah if I am not sure I believe?
No. Many people find that faith grows through action. Doing a mitzvah is a sincere step, wherever you are.
