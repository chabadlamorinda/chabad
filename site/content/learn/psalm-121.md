Psalm 121, known as Shir Lamaalot ("A Song for Ascents"), is the psalm that begins "I shall raise my eyes to the mountains, from where will my help come?" Jews say it for comfort and protection, and a Chabad custom is to hang a card with this psalm in the room where a baby is born and near the newborn's crib.

## What Psalm 121 says

The psalm is eight short verses. In Hebrew it opens:

שִׁ֗יר לַֽמַּֽ֫עֲל֥וֹת אֶשָּׂ֣א עֵ֖ינַי אֶל־הֶֽהָרִ֑ים מֵ֜אַ֗יִן יָבֹ֥א עֶזְרִֽי:

עֶזְרִי מֵעִ֣ם יְהֹוָ֑ה עֹ֜שֵׂ֗ה שָׁמַ֥יִם וָאָֽרֶץ:

In English (Judaica Press translation on Chabad.org):

1. A song for ascents. I shall raise my eyes to the mountains, from where will my help come?
2. My help is from the Lord, the Maker of heaven and earth.
3. He will not allow your foot to falter; Your Guardian will not slumber.
4. Behold the Guardian of Israel will neither slumber nor sleep.
5. The Lord is your Guardian; the Lord is your shadow; He is by your right hand.
6. By day, the sun will not smite you, nor will the moon at night.
7. The Lord will guard you from all evil; He will guard your soul.
8. The Lord will guard your going out and your coming in from now and to eternity.

The word "guard" appears again and again. The message is simple: we look up to the mountains, to the heights and the things that seem bigger than us, and then we remember that our real help comes from the Creator, who never sleeps.

## The Shir Lamaalot card for a newborn

According to Chabad.org, it is customary to hang a Shir Lamaalot (Psalm 121) in the room of the new mother and the newborn, from the time the expectant mother arrives at the hospital. The card decorates the birthing room and the cradle. It is understood as a source of protection and blessing for both mother and child.

The Rebbe, Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson, taught that this custom was once natural, when babies were born at home. When births moved to hospitals, many people stopped, so the Rebbe strongly encouraged reviving it and said it is "fitting and proper to publicize everywhere that Jewish people live."

He gave a second reason, about education. The first thing a baby sees should be holy, so the letters of the aleph-bet should be before a newborn's eyes, since all of creation came from their combinations. See our guide to the [Hebrew alphabet](/learn/hebrew-alphabet).

If the hospital staff wonder about the card, you can explain that the psalm helps calm the mother, which is also the goal of the medical team.

## Treating the card with respect

The card contains Divine Names, so it is handled with care. Chabad.org explains that in the birthing room and any room where babies are changed, the card should be covered, ideally with a double covering, one of which is opaque. Some are lenient and accept a single transparent covering. A card in a room used for marital intimacy has stricter rules. Because details depend on the card and the room, ask the Rabbi or the Rebbetzin if you are unsure.

## Saying the psalm yourself

Psalm 121 is also part of the book of [Tehillim](/learn/tehillim), and many people read it simply when they need comfort or protection on a hard day. ## Common questions

### What is Shir Lamaalot?
Shir Lamaalot, "A Song for Ascents," is Psalm 121, a short psalm about G-d as our Guardian. It is the psalm printed on the card that many Jewish families hang for a newborn.

### Why do Jews hang Psalm 121 in the birthing room?
It is a long-held custom to hang it as a safeguard for mother and baby, starting when the mother arrives at the hospital. The Rebbe also taught that a baby's first sight should be holy.

### Where do I put a Shir Lamaalot card?
In the delivery room and near the baby's crib. Because it contains Divine Names, it is covered in certain rooms; ask the Rabbi or the Rebbetzin for the details.

### Can I take the card into a hospital?
Yes, this is the custom. If staff hesitate, explain that the psalm calms the mother. Chabad.org recommends that gentle explanation.
