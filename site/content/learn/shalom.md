Shalom (שָׁלוֹם) is a Hebrew word that means "peace," and it is also used for "hello" and "goodbye." Its root is shaleim (שלם), "whole" or "complete," so shalom is not just quiet but wholeness, and it is also one of the names of G-d.

## What shalom really means

Chabad.org explains that shalom is rooted in shaleim, meaning completion, and ties the two together: without peace there can be no completion. A person, a family or a world is whole only when its parts are in harmony. That is why Jewish tradition never treats peace as merely the absence of fighting.

A teaching from the Mishnah quoted on Chabad.org says that G-d "did not find a vessel that could hold blessing other than shalom," peace. Peace is the container for every other good thing.

## Hello and goodbye

Shalom is used both at arriving and at leaving. When we greet someone with shalom, we are giving them a blessing, not just a polite word. Learn other everyday words in [Hebrew phrases](/learn/hebrew-phrases) and [holiday greetings](/learn/holiday-greetings).

## Shalom Aleichem: the greeting and its answer

"Shalom aleichem" means "peace upon you." The reply reverses the words: "Aleichem shalom." Chabad.org gives several reasons:

- The Talmud encourages greeting others with G-d's name, and peace is one of those names.
- Rabbi Hunah teaches that someone who does not return a greeting of peace is like a robber, since he withholds something valuable.
- Reversing the words shows that the answer is a reply, and that the person truly wants peace for you, not just agrees with your wish.
- The plural "you" in the answer is read mystically: the responder helps create unity between the two people.
- The Lubavitcher Rebbe taught that when two people who are connected to G-d through their souls greet each other with peace, they bring G-dliness into the physical world.

## Shabbat Shalom

"Shabbat Shalom," "a Shabbat of peace," is the most common Shabbat greeting. Chabad.org's Menachem Posner adds: you can also say "Gut Shabbos" in Yiddish, which works as hello and goodbye. If you are unsure what to say, wait for the other person's greeting and repeat it. Jews do not usually say "happy Shabbat." The greeting is used from midweek through Saturday afternoon; after Shabbat, we say "Shavua tov," a good week. Read about [Shabbat day](/learn/shabbat-day).

## A name of G-d

Chabad.org notes that Shalom is one of the names of G-d, so it should not be said in places like a restroom, where G-d's name may not be spoken. Most halachic authorities allow calling or speaking of a person named Shalom in such places. If you have a specific question, ask the Rabbi.

Shalom is also a common name for a boy, and the Talmud mentions it as a woman's name too.

## Shalom in the home

The Jewish phrase shalom bayit, "peace in the home," shows how much we value it. The Sages call peace the great blessing, and our homes are where we practice it. See [shalom bayit](/learn/shalom-bayit). Neighbors in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda who greet one another warmly are making shalom, one hello at a time. And loving fellow Jews is the foundation of peace: read [Ahavat Yisrael](/learn/ahavat-yisrael).

## Common questions

### What does shalom mean in Hebrew?
Shalom means "peace," and it is also used for "hello" and "goodbye." Its root is shaleim, "whole" or "complete."

### Why is shalom used for both hello and goodbye?
It is a blessing of peace, and peace is wished at meeting and at parting. Chabad.org notes that it can mean either hello or goodbye.

### What does Shabbat Shalom mean?
It means "a Shabbat of peace," the most common greeting on Shabbat.

### What does Shalom Aleichem mean?
It means "peace upon you." The answer, "Aleichem shalom," reverses the words to show it is a reply and a personal wish for peace.

### Is Shalom a name of G-d?
Yes. Chabad.org notes that shalom is one of G-d's names, so it is not said in places where G-d's name may not be pronounced, such as a restroom.

### Can I say Shalom to someone named Shalom?
Chabad.org says most halachic authorities permit it even in such places. Ask the Rabbi if you have a specific question.
