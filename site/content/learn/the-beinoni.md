The beinoni (בֵּינוֹנִי) is the "intermediate person" in the Tanya, the central book of Chabad Chassidic thought. He is not a perfect tzaddik, and not a rasha who gives in to wrongdoing. The beinoni still feels the pull of the wrong desire, but never lets it into his thought, speech or action.

The Tanya's surprising message is that this level is within reach of every person, at every moment. The struggle itself is part of the goal.

## Three kinds of people

The [Tanya](/learn/tanya), written by Rabbi Schneur Zalman of Liadi, describes the inner struggle between the divine soul and the animal soul within each person. Based on how that struggle ends, it names three types:

- **Tzaddik** (צַדִּיק): a completely righteous person, whose inner desires have been transformed.
- **Rasha** (רָשָׁע): one who lets the wrong desire take over.
- **Beinoni**: the one in the middle, who feels the desire but chooses what is right.

Most of us assume that the beinoni is "average" or second-rate. But the Tanya says he is the book's real subject, and its title, "Book of the Beinonim," points to this.

## What makes a beinoni

According to Chapter 12 on Chabad.org, a beinoni's thoughts, speech and actions are guided by the divine soul. The animal soul's desires still arise in his heart, so he is not a tzaddik, but he does not let them rule him, not even for a moment. The chapter says the title "rasha" has never been applied to him, even temporarily.

That does not mean he never did anything wrong in his life. It describes who he is now, in his choices. The desire still comes, but he does not act on it, he rejects unwanted thoughts as soon as they come, and he answers anger or jealousy with kindness.

## The key idea: it is about acting, not feeling

Chapter 14 teaches that "the rank of beinoni is one that is attainable by every man." It does not depend on feeling. A person may not hate evil with deep emotion, because feelings are not entirely in our control and change from day to day. What is in our control is action: to "turn away from evil and do good" in deed, word and thought.

Many people think, "I don't feel spiritual, so religion is not for me." The Tanya answers: you do not need to feel it. You need to choose it.

## Tools for the struggle

Chabad.org summarizes some of the tools the Tanya gives:

- Set aside regular time for reflection and to contemplate G-d's greatness.
- Remember that the mind can guide the heart, as light chases away darkness.
- Build habits: repeating a good choice makes it feel more natural over time.
- Rejoice in G-d. The Tanya values joy over despair.

## A lesson for anyone who feels "not good enough"

If you feel you are not religious enough, or that your mistakes disqualify you, the Tanya says otherwise. The person who struggles and keeps choosing to do right is exactly the person the book is written for. Feeling the pull does not mean you failed. It means you are in the game. And if you fall, [teshuvah](/learn/teshuvah) is always available. See also [Jewish views on self-esteem](/learn/self-esteem-jewish-view).

## Common questions

### What is a beinoni?
A beinoni is the "intermediate person" described in the Tanya: someone who still feels temptation but never lets it influence his thoughts, speech or actions. It is a level the Tanya says is attainable by every person.

### What is the difference between a tzaddik, a beinoni and a rasha?
A tzaddik has fully transformed his inner desires. A beinoni feels them but never acts on them. A rasha lets them control him.

### Do I have to feel close to G-d to be a beinoni?
No. According to Chapter 14, the beinoni's level does not depend on feelings, which change, but on choices of action, speech and thought.

### Is the beinoni a lower level?
It may sound that way, but the Tanya presents it as the main subject of the book, because it is the level everyone can reach. Ask the Rabbi if you want to study it.

### What if I fail?
The Tanya speaks of rising again. Teshuvah, return, is always open, and joy rather than despair is recommended.
