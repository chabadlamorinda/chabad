A boy's Bar Mitzvah falls on his 13th Hebrew birthday and a girl's Bat Mitzvah on her 12th, counted by the Jewish calendar and not the secular one. Because the two calendars drift apart, the date is usually a few weeks before or after the English birthday, and it can even land in a different month.

## Why the Hebrew date, not the English one

The Jewish calendar follows the moon and adds a leap month in some years, so a Hebrew birthday moves around the secular calendar. A child becomes responsible for the mitzvot on the Hebrew date, whether or not there is a party. The celebration, the Torah reading and the meal simply mark what has already happened. See [Bar Mitzvah](/learn/bar-mitzvah) and [Bat Mitzvah](/learn/bat-mitzvah) for what the day means.

## How to find the date, step by step

1. **Write down the exact birth date** in the secular calendar, and the place.
2. **Note the time of birth.** A Jewish day begins at nightfall. A child born in the evening, after dark, takes the next Hebrew date.
3. **Convert it to the Hebrew date.** Use a Jewish calendar converter, or ask the Rabbi, who will do it with you.
4. **Count forward 13 Hebrew years** for a boy, or 12 for a girl, to the same Hebrew day and month.
5. **Look up the Shabbat on or after that date** to see the Torah portion; see [your child's Torah portion](/learn/bar-mitzvah-parsha).

## The tricky cases

Most children have a simple date. A few do not, and these are the ones to ask about:

- **Born near sunset.** If you do not know whether the birth was before or after nightfall, or it was during twilight, the Rabbi will advise which date to use.
- **Born in Adar, or the Bar Mitzvah year is a leap year.** A Jewish leap year has two months of Adar. Where a birthday in Adar meets a leap year, practice depends on the details; Jewish law explains the rules at length. Please ask the Rabbi. See [the Jewish leap year](/learn/jewish-leap-year-and-calendar).
- **Born on the 30th of Cheshvan or Kislev.** Some years those months have only 29 days, so the date has to move. Ask the Rabbi which day applies.
- **Born abroad or on a flight.** The place of birth can matter. Ask.

## The date and the celebration are two things

The child becomes Bar or Bat Mitzvah on the Hebrew birthday. The family then chooses a day for the service and celebration: a Monday or Thursday when the Torah is read, a Shabbat, or another day close by. Many choose the Shabbat on or after the birthday, and some travel to Israel. The halacha notes that the ideal first aliyah is a Monday or Thursday morning, or a Shabbat afternoon, and the Rabbi can help you choose. Read the [full timeline](/learn/bar-bat-mitzvah-timeline) to plan backward from the date.

## Common questions

### When is my child's Bar Mitzvah?
It is the boy's 13th birthday on the Hebrew calendar, counted from his Hebrew birth date. Convert his birth date with a Jewish calendar tool or ask the Rabbi.

### When is a Bat Mitzvah?
A girl becomes Bat Mitzvah on her 12th Hebrew birthday. Jewish tradition teaches that girls mature earlier, so the age of mitzvot comes one year sooner.

### What if my child was born after sunset?
A Jewish day begins at nightfall, so a child born after dark takes the following Hebrew date. If the birth was near sunset, ask the Rabbi to decide.

### Is the Hebrew date different every year?
Yes. A Hebrew birthday falls on a different secular date each year, because the calendar is lunar with an occasional leap month.

### What if the date is in a leap year or in Adar?
These cases have special rules and depend on the exact birth date. Ask Rabbi Labkowski at 925-384-7242 before you set the date.

### Do we have to hold the celebration on the exact day?
No. The child is Bar or Bat Mitzvah on the date itself, and the aliyah and party are held on a day that suits the family. Ask the Rabbi about a good day.
