You are walking through a parking lot and see a wallet on the ground. A phone is left on a bench. In each of these moments, the Torah has something to say, and it is more than "be nice." Returning lost things, *hashavat aveidah*, is a mitzvah in its own right.

## The mitzvah

"You shall not see your brother's ox or his sheep wandering and ignore them; you shall surely return them to your brother... And so shall you do with his garment, and so shall you do with any lost thing of your brother's which he has lost and you have found; you may not ignore it" (Deuteronomy 22:1-3).

The sages counted two commandments here: a positive one, to return what is lost, and a prohibition, not to look away. The Torah knows how tempting it is to keep walking, to tell ourselves that someone else will deal with it. "You may not ignore it" closes that door.

## What to do when you find something

1. **Pick it up.** Once you have seen a lost object that clearly has an owner, Jewish law asks you to take responsibility for it.
2. **Look for the owner.** A wallet usually has an ID; a phone can be answered when it rings or shown to a store's front desk; a set of keys may have a tag.
3. **Return it gladly.** The sages taught that the reward for a mitzvah is the mitzvah itself.

## Signs that identify an owner

The Talmud's central idea about lost objects is the *siman*, an identifying sign. An owner proves that something is theirs by describing what a stranger could not know: the contents of the wallet, the lock-screen photo on the phone, a scratch on the case, the exact place it was lost. When a claimant can give such a sign, we return it.

Some things have no sign and are usually not expected to be returned: a single loose coin on the street, or a plain item identical to a thousand others, because the owner has given up hope of finding it (*yeush*). When you are not sure, ask the Rabbi.

## Everyday examples

- **A wallet.** Return it with everything in it. If there is cash, every dollar goes back.
- **Extra change.** If a cashier or a self-checkout gives you too much, go back and say so.
- **A phone.** Charge it if you can so the owner can call it, answer when it rings, and arrange to hand it over.
The Talmud tells of Shimon ben Shetach, whose students bought him a donkey from a non-Jew and found a precious stone hanging from its neck. He returned the stone at once, saying that he would rather hear the owner say "Blessed is the G-d of the Jews" than own all the riches in the world. Returning a lost object is a [kiddush Hashem](/learn/kiddush-hashem), a way of honoring G-d's name in public.

## Honesty as a habit

Chassidic teaching sees every mitzvah as a way of refining ourselves. Hashavat aveidah trains the heart to see other people's property as truly theirs. See [honesty and truth](/learn/honesty-and-truth) and [business ethics](/learn/business-ethics).

## Teaching children

Children notice everything. When they see you walk back into a store to return extra change, they learn more than a lecture could teach. A few ideas:

- Make a game of looking for the owner: "Whose do you think this is? How could we find them?"- When a child finds money at home, teach them to ask whose it is before keeping it.

See [raising kind children](/learn/raising-kind-children) and [children, money and tzedakah](/learn/children-money-and-tzedakah).

## Common questions

### Is it stealing if I just keep something I found?
If the object has an identifying sign and the owner has not given up hope, keeping it is taking what belongs to someone else. The Torah says "you may not ignore it," and even more so you may not keep it.

### What if I cannot find the owner after trying?
Keep the item safe and keep a note of where and when you found it, so that if the owner ever turns up you can return it. For a specific case, ask the Rabbi.

### Can I accept a reward?
You should not demand one, but you may accept a gift an owner offers freely. Many people decline and say, "It was a mitzvah." Either way, return the item first.
