A friend does not answer your message. A driver cuts you off. A relative forgets your birthday. In a split second the mind writes a story: they do not care, they are rude, they are selfish. The Torah asks us to write a different story.

## "Judge every person favorably"

The sage Yehoshua ben Perachia taught, "Judge every person favorably" (Ethics of the Fathers 1:6). The Hebrew, *dan l'kaf zechut*, means to tip the scale toward merit. When something can be seen two ways, choose the kinder one.

## The worker who was not paid

The Talmud tells of a man who worked for an employer for three years. On the eve of Yom Kippur he asked for his wages to bring home to his family. The employer said he had no money, no fruit, no land, no cattle, no clothing. The worker went home empty-handed and did not complain. After the holiday the employer came with his wages and three donkeys loaded with gifts, and asked: "What did you think when I said I had nothing?" Each time, the worker had found a kind explanation. The employer revealed that every one of them was true, and blessed him: "As you judged me favorably, so may G-d judge you favorably."

## Why it matters

- **It is usually true.** We see a moment; we do not see the sick child, the bad news, the phone that died.
- **It protects friendships and families.** Most quarrels begin with a story we told ourselves.
- **It changes us.** The Baal Shem Tov taught that the world is like a mirror: when we notice a fault in another person, it is a sign to look at ourselves. A generous eye makes a generous heart.
- **It brings blessing.** The Talmud says that one who judges others favorably is judged favorably by G-d.

## How to practice

1. **Pause before the story.** Notice the first, harsh explanation, and do not believe it yet.
2. **Find three other explanations.** They were busy, they did not see it, they were having a hard day.
3. **Ask, kindly.** "I noticed... is everything okay?" opens a door that an accusation closes.
4. **Speak well of others,** and avoid passing on the harsh story. See [speaking kindly about others](/learn/lashon-hara).
5. **Start with your family.** Those closest to us need our benefit of the doubt most.

## Common questions

### Does this mean I should ignore real wrongdoing?
No. Judging favorably is about the many moments we cannot know the full story. When someone truly hurts others, Judaism asks us to protect people and to speak up wisely. Ask the Rabbi about a hard situation.

### What if the person really did mean it?
Then forgive if you can, and try to make peace. See [forgiveness and making peace](/learn/forgiveness-and-making-peace).

### How do I judge myself favorably?
The same way: with honesty and kindness. Notice what went wrong, learn, and begin again. See [changing a bad habit](/learn/changing-a-bad-habit).
