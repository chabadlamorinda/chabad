A sick child recovers. A near-accident ends with everyone safe. A chance meeting changes a life. Most of us have a story we can only call a miracle. Judaism has a lot to say about such moments, and even more about the quiet wonders we stop noticing.

## Open miracles and hidden miracles

The Torah tells of **open miracles**: the Ten Plagues, the splitting of the Sea, manna in the desert. On Chanukah we celebrate the small jug of pure oil that burned for eight days. These are moments when G-d sets nature aside and everyone can see it.

The sages also speak of **hidden miracles**, wrapped in nature so well that they look like ordinary events. Purim is the great example: the Book of Esther never mentions G-d's name, yet a chain of "coincidences" saved the Jewish people. The great commentator Nachmanides (the Ramban) taught that a person who truly believes sees that everything, the rain, health, a good livelihood, is a hidden miracle.

## Nature is a miracle too

Chassidic teaching, from the Baal Shem Tov and the Tanya, goes a step further: G-d does not create the world once and then leave it alone. He creates it anew every moment. Nature is not a machine running by itself; it is G-d's constant kindness, so regular that we call it "nature." The Hebrew word for nature, *teva*, has the same numerical value as *Elokim*, a name of G-d that describes Him hidden in the world.

That is why three times a day, in the Amidah, we thank G-d "for Your miracles that are with us every day, and for Your wonders and kindnesses at every time, evening, morning and noon."

## Noticing your own miracles

The Talmud says that the person a miracle happens to often does not recognize it. A few Jewish habits help us notice:

- **Say a blessing.** There are blessings for lightning, a rainbow, the ocean and a tree in bloom. See [blessings on nature](/learn/blessings-on-nature).
- **Say Birkat HaGomel** after coming through danger, an illness or a flight over the ocean. See [Birkat HaGomel](/learn/birkat-hagomel).
- **Thank out loud.** Tell your family the story at the Shabbat table. Gratitude grows when it is shared. See [gratitude](/learn/hakarat-hatov).
- **Give tzedakah** in thanks; many families give *chai*, 18, after good news.

## Do we rely on miracles?

No. Judaism teaches that we do not rely on miracles: we see a doctor, drive carefully and work for a living, and we also pray and trust. The Maccabees picked up swords, and then G-d gave them victory. Our effort is the vessel; the blessing is G-d's. See [bitachon, trust in G-d](/learn/bitachon).

## When the miracle does not come

Sometimes we pray with all our heart and the answer is not what we hoped. Judaism does not ask us to pretend otherwise. It allows us to cry, to ask "why," and to be held by community. Many people find that even in the darkest times, small kindnesses appeared that they could only see later. If you are going through this, please talk with the Rabbi or the Rebbetzin.

## Common questions

### What miracle do we celebrate on Chanukah?
Two: the victory of the few Maccabees over the mighty Greek army, and the jug of pure oil, enough for one day, that burned for eight. We add Al HaNissim, "for the miracles," to our prayers all eight days. See [the Chanukah story](/learn/chanukah-story).

### Do miracles still happen today?
Yes. Judaism teaches that G-d is involved in every detail of life, which Chassidic teaching calls *hashgacha pratit*, divine providence. See [divine providence](/learn/hashgacha-pratit).

### Is there a blessing when a miracle happens to me?
After coming safely through danger, people say Birkat HaGomel at the Torah reading. When you return to a place where a miracle happened to you, there is a blessing too. Ask the Rabbi about your situation.

### Can I pray for a miracle?
Yes. You can pray for anything, in your own words, in any language. See [talking to G-d in your own words](/learn/talking-to-g-d-in-your-own-words).
