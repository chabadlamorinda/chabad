In Jewish tradition, growing older is a gain, not a loss: the Torah uses the word "old" (zaken, זָקֵן) as another word for "wise," and the Lubavitcher Rebbe taught that every added year is a sign that a person's mission is not yet finished. Chabad teaching does not divide life into a working stage and a resting stage; it asks each of us to keep contributing, learning and growing at every age.

## "Old" means "wise"

The Hebrew word zaken is read in Jewish teaching as "one who has acquired wisdom." Chabad.org, presenting the Rebbe's teachings, explains that elders deserve honor even if they were never scholars, because a lifetime of experience gives a kind of wisdom that even a brilliant young person cannot yet have. The Torah says of Abraham that he "grew old and came along in days," which is understood to mean that he became greater with each passing day.

The Rebbe also pointed out that Jewish law asks us to stand in respect for the elderly. Learn more in [honoring the elderly](/learn/honoring-the-elderly).

## Strength of body, strength of mind

The Rebbe spoke sharply about a modern habit of measuring a person's worth by physical strength. When that is the yardstick, he said, older people begin to feel useless, and that feeling can bring on depression. The Torah measures differently. The body may slow down, but the mind and the soul can grow stronger. Many people find that when the pressures of earning a living ease, they finally have room to ask the questions that matter most.

## No "retirement" from purpose

One of the Rebbe's best-known teachings is that the concept of retirement, as a stage of life with nothing left to accomplish, does not exist in the Torah. Rest and the enjoyment of our efforts belong to the World to Come. In this world, every additional day is a gift with a task attached. Chabad teaching explains that we were created to be partners with G-d in finishing His world, so there is always something to repair, learn or give.

This does not mean you must keep working at your old job. It means that leaving a career is the start of a new chapter of purpose. For more on this, read [a Jewish view of retirement](/learn/retirement-jewish-view).

## Ways to grow at any age

The Rebbe suggested setting up Torah-study groups for older adults, called Tiferes Zekeinim (תִּפְאֶרֶת זְקֵנִים, "the beauty of the elderly"). He envisioned groups that meet at fixed times, study the weekly Torah portion, and keep a charity box, so that learning, prayer and giving go together. He also proposed groups for women. Here are some ways to follow that spirit:

- **Learn.** Join a class, or read a little each day. See [daily Torah study](/learn/daily-torah-study) and [Torah classes in Lamorinda](/learn/torah-classes-in-lamorinda).
- **Mentor.** Pass on what you know to a younger person, a grandchild or a neighbor. See [Jewish grandparents](/learn/jewish-grandparents).
- **Give.** Tzedakah and kindness are open to everyone. Read about [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah).
- **Put it in writing.** In one of his letters the Rebbe mentioned the traditional saying that writing a will is itself a segulah, a spiritual help, for long life. An [ethical will](/learn/ethical-will) shares your values and your story with your family. Ask the Rabbi about your own situation.

## Common questions

### What does Judaism say about growing old?
Judaism sees old age as a time of wisdom and honor. The Torah links the word for "old" with "wise," and Jewish law asks us to show respect to the elderly.

### Is there retirement in Judaism?
The Rebbe taught that the Torah has no concept of retirement as a time without purpose. You may stop a job, but every day of life still carries a mission, and rest belongs to the World to Come.

### How can seniors stay connected to Jewish life in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda?

### Why did the Rebbe stress honoring the elderly?
He taught that society should not waste the experience of older people, and that younger generations will one day be old too. The Fifth Commandment links honoring parents with lengthened days.
