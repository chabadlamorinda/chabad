Aleinu is the prayer that closes every Jewish service: morning, afternoon and evening. Its words say who we are and what we hope for: that we thank G-d for choosing to give us the Torah, and that one day the whole world will recognize one G-d. We bow at one line of it, and stand with focus for the rest.

## What the word means

*Aleinu* means "it is upon us." The first line is, "It is upon us to praise the Master of all, to give greatness to the Creator of the world." Praise is not an extra; it is our job, and our privilege.

The prayer has two paragraphs. Each has its own mood.

## The first paragraph: our gratitude

The first paragraph thanks G-d that we were given a path and a purpose. We say that G-d made us part of a people with a mission, a people who bring awareness of the One G-d into the world. It is a prayer of humility and of dignity together: we are small, and we are chosen to do something great.

**Why we bow.** The prayer itself says "and we bend the knee, bow down and give thanks before the King of kings." Bowing is a sign of humility: it shows we are servants of G-d. Customs vary. Some bend the knees, some only bow the head, and some stay upright. If your custom differs from the congregation's, bow along with them so you do not stand out. On Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, in the Musaf service, many go down in full prostration at this line, as was done in the Holy Temple.

## The second paragraph: our hope

The second paragraph begins *Al ken nekaveh*, "therefore we hope." It looks forward to a world healed: when falsehood and cruelty fade, and every person turns to goodness. It ends with the verse, "And G-d will be King over all the earth; on that day G-d will be One and His name One." Chassidic teaching sees in this a daily rehearsal for a perfected world. Each time we say it, we remind ourselves that the work of repairing the world is ours.

## When it is said

- Aleinu closes **Shacharit** (the morning service), **Mincha** (afternoon) and **Maariv** (evening).
- It is also said on Rosh Hashanah as a central part of the Musaf service.
- It is said standing, with feet together, and many bow at the right moment. Children can say it too: it is short enough to learn by heart.

Some communities have small differences in wording or in the tune. Chabad follows its own prayer book (Nusach Ari). If you are unsure what to do at a given line, ask the Rabbi.

## How to start

1. Open a siddur to the end of the morning service and read Aleinu once in English to catch the meaning.
2. The next day, read it in Hebrew with the English beside it.
3. At the service, stand with everyone, bow at the line, and listen to the tune. It is easier than it looks.

## Common questions

### What does Aleinu mean?
*Aleinu* means "it is upon us." The prayer begins by saying it is our duty to praise the Master of all, and it ends with the hope that G-d will be recognized as One by the whole world.

### Why do we bow during Aleinu?
The prayer says "and we bend the knee, bow down and give thanks." Bowing shows humility and that we serve G-d. Some people bend the knees, some bow the head; follow your custom or the congregation's.

### When is Aleinu said?
At the end of the morning, afternoon and evening services, and in the Musaf service of Rosh Hashanah.

### Who wrote Aleinu?
It has been part of Jewish prayer for many centuries, and traditions differ about who composed it. If you want to know more, ask the Rabbi.

### Do I have to know Hebrew to say Aleinu?
No. Read it in English at first, and learn the Hebrew a line at a time.

### Why does Aleinu end the service?
It sends us out into the world with a mission: to bring what we felt in prayer into our homes, work and streets.
