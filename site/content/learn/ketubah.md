A ketubah is the Jewish marriage contract, written in Aramaic, in which the groom commits to care for and support his wife. It is signed by two witnesses before the ceremony, read aloud under the chuppah, and given to the bride to keep for life.

## What the ketubah says

The ketubah sets out the groom's obligations to his wife, which the Torah and the Sages require of every Jewish husband: to provide food, clothing and a home, to respect her, to be close to her, and to provide for her if the marriage ends. It records the date in the Hebrew calendar, the place, and the names of the bride, the groom and their fathers.

The Sages instituted the ketubah more than two thousand years ago to protect the wife. It made marriage a serious, binding commitment, and it is one of the earliest documents in history to give a wife legal rights.

## Who signs it

The ketubah is signed by **two witnesses**, who must be observant Jewish men who are not related to the bride, the groom or each other. Before signing, the groom formally accepts the obligations through a *kinyan*, by lifting an object, usually a pen or handkerchief, handed to him by the Rabbi in front of the witnesses.

Many couples choose a beautifully decorated ketubah to hang in their home. The art is a matter of taste, but the text must be correct and filled in by someone who knows the laws, usually the Rabbi officiating.

## The wedding ring

In a Jewish wedding, the groom "betroths" the bride by giving her something of value. The custom is to use a ring. Under the chuppah, he places it on the index finger of her right hand and says:

**Hebrew:** הֲרֵי אַתְּ מְקֻדֶּשֶׁת לִי בְּטַבַּעַת זוֹ כְּדַת משֶׁה וְיִשְׂרָאֵל

**Transliteration:** Harei at mekudeshet li b'taba'at zo k'dat Moshe v'Yisrael.

**Translation:** Behold, you are consecrated to me with this ring, according to the law of Moses and Israel.

The ring should be:
- **A plain band** of metal, usually gold, with no stones, so its value is clear and simple.
- **Owned by the groom.** It cannot be borrowed.
- **Accepted willingly** by the bride, in front of two witnesses.

After the ceremony, the bride may move the ring to whichever finger she likes.

## The order of the ceremony

1. The ketubah is prepared and signed by the witnesses.
2. The groom covers the bride's face with her veil (*badeken*).
3. Under the chuppah, blessings are said over wine.
4. The groom gives the ring and says the betrothal words.
5. The ketubah is read aloud and handed to the groom, who gives it to his bride.
6. The Seven Blessings are said, and the groom breaks a glass.

## Common questions

### Does the bride give the groom a ring?
In a traditional ceremony, only the groom gives a ring under the chuppah, since the Torah describes the groom betrothing the bride. Many brides give their husband a ring later, away from the ceremony. Ask the Rabbi if this matters to you.

### Can we use a ring with diamonds or an heirloom?
Under the chuppah, a plain band is used. An engagement ring with stones is worn separately. An heirloom band can be used if it truly belongs to the groom; ask the Rabbi.

### What if the ketubah is lost or damaged?
A couple may not live together without a ketubah, so a replacement must be written as soon as possible. Call the Rabbi, who can prepare one for you.

### Can we write our own ketubah text?
The traditional text has binding legal meaning in Jewish law and should not be changed. If you would like to share personal words with each other, ask the Rabbi about the best way and moment to do it.
