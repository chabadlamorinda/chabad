Synagogue etiquette comes down to one idea: a synagogue is a holy place, so we act with respect. Turn off your phone, keep quiet during prayers, dress modestly, and follow the people around you when they stand or sit. Nobody expects a newcomer to know everything, and if you slip up, the most you will get is a friendly smile.

## Why etiquette matters in a synagogue

The Talmud calls a synagogue a "miniature sanctuary," a small echo of the Holy Temple in Jerusalem. According to Chabad.org, that is why it is treated with respect even when no service is going on. In Hebrew a synagogue is a *beit knesset* (בית כנסת), "house of gathering," and many people say *shul*, the Yiddish word. The rules below are not meant to make anyone feel unwelcome. They protect the quiet in which prayer can happen.

## The basics

Chabad.org's synagogue guide lists a few simple habits to keep:

- **Show respect.** Do not run or shout.
- **Avoid idle talk and gossip.**
- **Do not eat** in the sanctuary, and do not read newspapers.
- **Do not use a cell phone.** Switch it off before you come in. On Shabbat and holidays, Jewish law does not allow using a phone at all, so leaving it at home is easiest.
- **Keep the place clean.**

## Talking during services

Quiet is most important during the silent [Amidah](/learn/amidah), during the Torah reading, and when the prayer leader is saying the Kaddish. If you need to speak, step out into the lobby. Never walk in front of someone who is praying the Amidah. Wait until they have finished, or go around behind them. When someone says a blessing aloud, you can answer "Amen." That is a mitzvah in itself.

## Standing, sitting and bowing

You will see a lot of standing and sitting. Do not worry about getting it perfect. A few common moments:

- Everyone stands for the Amidah; the [Shema](/learn/shema) is usually said seated.
- Everyone stands when the ark is opened.
- Many people stand as the Torah scroll is carried through the room.

Customs differ from one community to another. If you cannot stand for long, or have trouble with a knee or a back, it is perfectly fine to stay seated. If you are unsure, just watch the people next to you, or ask quietly.

## Dress, kippah and seating

Men cover their heads with a [kippah](/learn/kippah); there are usually some at the entrance. Many people dress a bit nicer on Shabbat, and women often choose modest clothing with sleeves, but nobody will turn you away. In a traditional synagogue, including Chabad, men and women sit in separate sections. Read [why in the mechitzah guide](/learn/mechitzah).

## Shabbat extras

On Shabbat and holidays, please do not take photos, write, or use electronics in the building. If you are visiting with children, see [Bringing Children to Synagogue](/learn/children-in-synagogue).

## Common questions

### What should I not do in a synagogue?
Do not use your phone, talk during prayers, eat in the sanctuary, or walk in front of someone saying the Amidah. On Shabbat, also avoid photos and writing.

### Do I have to turn off my phone in synagogue?
Yes. Chabad.org lists cell phone use among the things to avoid in synagogue. On Shabbat and holidays, observant Jews do not use phones at all, so it is kindest to switch yours off before you walk in.

### Can I talk in synagogue?
Keep conversation for the lobby or the Kiddush. During the prayers and the Torah reading, stay silent, and Chabad.org asks us to avoid idle talk and gossip in the synagogue at any time.

### When do you stand in synagogue?
In general, people stand for the Amidah, when the ark is open, and as the Torah is carried. Customs vary, so watch those around you and ask the Rabbi if you wish.

### What should I wear to synagogue?
Wear neat, modest clothing. Men wear a kippah. If you do not have nice clothes with you, come anyway. Everyone is welcome.

### What if I do something wrong by mistake?
Do not worry. Everyone was new once. Just smile and carry on, and ask a neighbor or the Rabbi if you want to know.
