In Israel, the Chanukah dreidel carries the letter pey (פ) instead of shin (ש), so its letters spell "A great miracle happened here" instead of "there." The holiday is also marked by jelly doughnuts called sufganiyot, public menorahs in city and town centers, and the modern Hebrew word hanukkiah for the menorah.

For Israelis in Lamorinda, these are the sights and tastes of home. For everyone else, they are a lovely way to see Chanukah through the eyes of the land where the miracle happened.

## The dreidel that says "here"

The classic dreidel has four letters: nun (נ), gimmel (ג), hei (ה) and shin (ש). They stand for *Nes gadol haya sham*, "A great miracle happened there," meaning in the Land of Israel. Jews who live in the Land of Israel replaced the shin with pey (פ), for *po* (פֹּה), "here." So an Israeli dreidel reads *Nes gadol haya po*, "A great miracle happened here."

Jewish tradition notes a hidden hint in the change. The four Israeli letters add up to 138 (nun 50, gimmel 3, hei 5, pey 80), the same value as *tzemach* (צֶמַח), a name for the Messiah in the Book of Zechariah, and of the name Menachem. Read about [how to play dreidel](/learn/dreidel) (the rules are the same) and about [the Hebrew letters](/learn/hebrew-alphabet).

## Sufganiyot: Israel's Chanukah doughnut

Fried foods recall the oil that burned in the Temple. The tradition notes that the oldest form of this custom was dough fried in oil, the forerunner of today's jelly-filled doughnut, the *sufganiya* (plural *sufganiyot*), which is very common in Israel today. Latkes are also eaten by many families, so some enjoy both. See [Chanukah foods](/learn/chanukah-foods) and [latkes](/learn/latkes).

## A hanukkiah, not just a menorah

In Modern Hebrew, the Chanukah lamp is called a *hanukkiah* (חֲנֻכִּיָּה). In English, both menorah and hanukkiah are widely used. The straight row of nine flames, with the shamash set apart, is the same everywhere. See [the Chanukah menorah](/learn/chanukah-menorah).

## Lights in public places

Chanukah's mitzvah is meant to be public, and this is how it looks in Israel. In 2012 there were reports on Chabad's "Operation Chanukah," in which young men went to army bases and outposts to light menorahs with soldiers, and on "Chanukah Mobiles," vehicles with a menorah on the roof that drove through city centers, malls, medical centers and nursing homes. All 289 Chabad centers in Israel ran Chanukah activities, and Jerusalem had more than 50 public menorahs at landmarks such as the Old City walls and the Tower of David. The Rebbe also directed that menorahs be lit on every Israeli army base. Read more on [public menorah lightings](/learn/public-menorah-lighting) and [Israel and the Jewish people](/learn/israel-and-the-jewish-people).

## Common questions

### How is Chanukah celebrated in Israel?
Families light the menorah each night, eat sufganiyot and other fried foods, play dreidel and sing. Public menorahs are lit in many cities and towns, and Chabad centers hold events across the country.

### What does the Israeli dreidel say?
It says *Nes gadol haya po*, "A great miracle happened here." The letter pey (פ) takes the place of the shin (ש) on the dreidel used outside Israel.

### Why does the Israeli dreidel say "here"?
Because the miracle happened in the Land of Israel, so Jews living there read the letters as "here" (פֹּה). Elsewhere, the shin (ש) stands for "there" (שָׁם).

### What is a sufganiya?
A sufganiya (סופגניה) is a deep-fried doughnut, usually filled with jelly. It is the Israeli favorite among Chanukah foods.

### What is the difference between a menorah and a hanukkiah?
They name the same Chanukah lamp. Hanukkiah is the Modern Hebrew word, while menorah is the older, more common English one. Both refer to the nine-branched lamp.
