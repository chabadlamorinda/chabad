King Munbaz was a ruler who, according to the Talmud (Bava Batra 11a), gave away his own treasures and those of his ancestors to feed the poor in years of hunger. When his family protested, he answered: "My fathers stored treasures below; I store them above." The story teaches that what we give to others is what we truly keep.

## The story as the Talmud tells it

There were years of famine, and King Munbaz opened his storehouses. Chabad.org tells that he spent the wealth he had inherited, together with the treasures his forefathers had gathered, to feed the poor.

His brothers and his father's household were upset. They told him, in effect: your ancestors saved and added to what their ancestors saved, and you are throwing it all away.

His reply is one of the most quoted lines about giving. As Chabad.org presents it, he said that his ancestors stored below and he stored above; they stored in a place where a foreign hand can reach, and he in a place where no hand can reach; they stored things that bear no fruit, and he stored things that bear fruit; they stored money, and he stored souls; they stored for this world, and he stored for the World to Come.

## What "above" means

Money kept in a vault can be lost, taxed, stolen or spent by someone else. A good deed cannot. Judaism teaches that a person's mitzvot and acts of kindness are what truly go with them. [Tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah) (צְדָקָה, "justice," what we call charity) turns something material into something lasting.

Notice also what King Munbaz did not say. He did not say that wealth is bad. He used his wealth, and his family's, for the best possible purpose. The [Jewish view of wealth](/learn/jewish-view-on-wealth) is that money is a tool entrusted to us, and it becomes valuable when it helps a life.

## "I stored souls"

One of his phrases deserves a second look: "my ancestors stored money, and I stored souls." Feeding the hungry in a famine keeps people alive. Giving to Jewish education keeps a people alive. Both are ways of investing in living souls rather than in objects.

## Why this story matters for a legacy

A legacy gift, whether in a will, a trust, a retirement account or life insurance, is a way of choosing in advance what "above" will mean for you. It asks one gentle question: of everything I will one day leave, what part do I want to be doing good after me?

For Chabad of Lamorinda, a gift of this kind is education for future generations, keeping Judaism alive and strengthening Jewish continuity for families in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda. Chabad of Lamorinda accepts legacy gifts (a bequest in a will or trust, a retirement account or life insurance), donor-advised fund grants and gifts of stock. ## Common questions

### Who was King Munbaz?
King Munbaz is a ruler mentioned in the Talmud (Bava Batra 11a) as one who gave away his treasures to feed the poor in years of famine. The story is told for its lesson about giving, not for history.

### What did King Munbaz mean by "I stored them above"?
He meant that his ancestors saved money where it could be lost or taken, while he turned his wealth into good deeds that last. The Talmud as retold by Chabad.org adds that he stored "souls" and "for the World to Come."

### Does Judaism say we should give away everything?
No. Jewish law sets a standard of giving a tenth, with up to a fifth especially praiseworthy; see [Maaser](/learn/maaser). The story shows a spirit of generosity, not a rule for everyone. About maaser itself, the Torah makes a promise: in Malachi (3:10) G-d says to bring the tithes and "test Me," and the Talmud (Taanit 9a) teaches "give a tenth so that you will become rich," as Chabad.org presents it. Maaser is G-d's promise of success. For your own situation, or if times are hard, call the Rabbi at 925-384-7242.
