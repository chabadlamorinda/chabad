Yes, Jewish women are obligated to pray every day. Although women are generally exempt from mitzvot that must be done at a set time, prayer is an exception; according to most authorities a woman prays the Amidah in the morning (Shacharit) and the afternoon (Mincha), and the evening prayer (Maariv) is optional.

## Why prayer is different

Chassidic teaching explains that there are two views about the source of a woman's obligation:

- **The first view:** the core obligation is biblical and has no fixed times. One request to G-d each day fulfills it. Many women historically followed this view, and authorities allow relying on it at times.
- **The second view:** the Men of the Great Assembly set fixed prayer times, and the sages included women. Most authorities follow this view, which has women pray the Amidah twice a day.

Which approach is right for you is a personal question: ask the Rabbi or Rebbetzin.

## The three prayers

- **Shacharit (morning),** שחרית: the Amidah is part of the daily minimum.
- **Mincha (afternoon),** מנחה: it is included in a woman's daily prayer, and if someone must choose between Mincha and Musaf, Mincha comes first.
- **Maariv (evening),** מעריב: optional for women, and, Jewish tradition notes, rarely recited by them.

The **Amidah** (עמידה) is the quiet, standing prayer at the heart of each service. See [daily prayers](/learn/daily-prayers) and [prayer](/learn/prayer).

## What is the minimum?

For a woman at a minimum, the minimum is the morning blessings, the blessing for learning Torah, the passage about the daily offering, the first verse of the Shema, a prayer called *Emet Veyatziv*, the Shacharit Amidah and (optionally) Aleinu. Other parts of the service, such as the rest of the Shema and the Psalms of praise, are optional, for those with more time.

A mother with young children has real constraints. The tradition says a woman caring for young children may be exempt from full prayer, and in that case should at least say the morning blessings. It also gives guidance on pausing to care for a child. Ask the Rabbi for specifics. See [morning blessings](/learn/morning-blessings).

## A Chabad custom

Some women say a morning blessing that thanks G-d for making them "according to His will" in place of the one men say. Among Chabad women, according to Jewish tradition, the widespread custom is to omit it, though some say it by family tradition.

## Prayer in your own words

Jewish tradition recommends praying in Hebrew or in a language you understand, and stresses concentration over length. Hannah is the great example: she prayed from the depths of her heart, her lips moving silently. The tradition connects her silent prayer to the custom of saying the Amidah quietly. See [Hannah and her prayer](/learn/hannah-and-her-prayer).

Candle lighting is also a special moment: after lighting and the blessing, with the eyes covered, is an especially auspicious time for private prayer. See [praying for your children](/learn/praying-for-your-children) and [Shabbat candles](/learn/shabbat-candles). You can also say Psalms on the [Tehillim page](/tehillim).

## Common questions

### Do Jewish women have to pray?
Yes. Women are obligated to pray daily, and most authorities say the Amidah of both morning and afternoon.

### Do women have to pray three times a day?
No. According to Jewish tradition, Shacharit and Mincha are the main obligation, and Maariv is optional for women.

### Can a mother of small children skip prayer?
Jewish tradition says a woman caring for young children may be exempt from full prayer, and should at least say the morning blessings. Ask the Rabbi about your own situation.

### Can women pray in English?
Yes, the tradition recommends a language you understand, though the standard prayers are in Hebrew. A siddur with translation helps; see [can I pray in English?](/learn/can-i-pray-in-english).

### Do women need a minyan?
A minyan is a quorum of ten men over Bar Mitzvah age, and a woman's daily prayer does not depend on one; ask the Rabbi about your situation. See [praying alone at home](/learn/praying-alone-at-home).
