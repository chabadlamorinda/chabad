In Judaism, how you give matters as much as what you give. The Sages said that how you give matters more than how much, and that tzedakah should be given with a smile and genuine warmth, protecting the dignity of the person receiving.

## Give with a smile and warmth

Give with a smile and real warmth. In its article on Maimonides' eight levels of giving, the lowest level is "giving grudgingly, with a sour countenance." The article says it is still better than not giving at all, but it is the lowest form, because it comes from guilt or obligation rather than real care. The contrast is that true tzedakah comes with "warm words and gentleness."

The next level up is giving less than you can afford, but pleasantly. A warm, empathetic response can ease the sting of a small gift and give the recipient emotional strength. So even when your gift is small, your face and words can make it a larger gift. See [giving when money is tight](/learn/giving-when-money-is-tight).

## If you have nothing to give

If someone asks you for food, you must give something. If someone asks for money, you may ask questions to check that the need is real. If you have nothing to give, show empathy and offer kind, uplifting words. You may never simply walk on. For more, read [giving to people who ask](/learn/giving-to-people-who-ask).

## Protect the receiver's dignity

The value of a gift depends on how well the giver protects the recipient's dignity and self-respect, not only on the amount. It describes levels:

1. **Give before you are asked.** This means looking for needs, rather than waiting for a cry for help. People often find it hard to ask.
2. **If you are asked, do not turn the person away.** Even if you can help only within your limits, you can refer them elsewhere.
3. **Give anonymously when possible.** Giving where the receiver does not know the giver protects against shame. See [giving anonymously](/learn/giving-anonymously).
4. **Help someone become self-sufficient.** This is the highest level: helping someone find work or start a business restores their sense of capability and can turn them into a giver. Advice should build the person's confidence in solving their own problems.

For the full list of the eight levels, see [Tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah).

## Time and skill count too

Giving is more than money. The eight levels include examples: offering someone a lift, carrying a friend's heavy load, cheering up a friend who is down, and sharing what you know. See [chesed](/learn/chesed).

## Common questions

### How do you give charity the Jewish way?
With a smile and warmth, and with respect for the receiver's dignity. How you give matters more than how much.

### What is the lowest level of giving?
In Maimonides' list, it is giving grudgingly, with a sour countenance. Even this is better than not giving, but giving warmly is much better.

### What if I cannot give money to someone who asks?
Show empathy and offer kind words; you may not simply walk on. You can also refer the person to another source of help.

### Is it better to give anonymously?
Give anonymously when possible, and give before being asked, to spare the receiver embarrassment.

### What is the highest level of giving?
Helping someone become self-sufficient, such as through a loan, a job or a partnership. This restores the person's sense of capability.

### Can I ask questions before giving to someone who asks for money?
You may ask questions to check the need. Ask the Rabbi if you are unsure about a specific situation.
