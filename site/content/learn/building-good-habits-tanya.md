Tanya's way of building good habits is to set fixed times, act through thought, speech and deed, and repeat until goodness becomes second nature. Chapter 14 says that repeated practice becomes second nature, and Chapter 41 describes a daily rhythm of prayer, study and renewed intention.

Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi did not write a "habit book" in the modern sense, but the Tanya contains a quiet, practical plan for steady growth.

## Three ways to act: thought, speech and deed

Chapter 4, as presented on Chabad.org, teaches that the soul has three "garments," its instruments of expression: thought, speech and action. Doing a physical mitzvah clothes the soul in action; learning Torah clothes it in speech; understanding Torah clothes it in thought.

For a habit, this means you have three places to build it. A good habit can be something you do (giving charity, lighting Shabbat candles), something you say (a blessing, a kind word), or something you think (a few moments of gratitude). The more of the three the better, since each carries goodness into your life.

## Fixed times, not only inspiration

Both Chapter 14 and Chapter 41 speak of fixed times. Chapter 14 says to set aside times to cultivate distaste for worldly pleasures, by reflecting on how fleeting they are, and to cultivate joy in G-d by thinking about His greatness. It says that repeated practice becomes second nature, and that this effort may bring inspiration from above.

Chapter 41 also advises setting fixed times for reflection and for regular study, after prayer, moving from the house of prayer to the house of study. In other words, do not wait until you feel like it. Choose a time and show up.

## Start the day deliberately

Chapter 41 describes how the morning blessings and prayer remind us that the soul is a gift from G-d, and to begin the day by dedicating that soul to Him. For ideas on a morning routine, see [a Jewish morning routine](/learn/jewish-morning-routine). You do not need to do it all at once; even a short first moment of gratitude is a start.

## Renew your intention

Tanya says that even a beinoni, an ordinary striving person, needs the right intention at the start of an act of study or a mitzvah, and during a long session should renew it at least once an hour (Chapter 41). Habits fade when the reason fades. A small renewal, "Why am I doing this?", keeps a habit alive.

## Small beats big

Tanya's examples are small: a pause before a permitted meal (see [itkafya](/learn/itkafya-self-discipline)), a daily commitment to Torah study, a moment of reflection. For breaking a bad habit, which is a different task, see [changing a bad habit](/learn/changing-a-bad-habit).

If you have one habit to start, many people find it easiest to attach it to something already in the day. That is our suggestion, not Tanya's. Ask the Rabbi what would suit your life.

## Making every day count

A habit is a way of caring about the days you have. See [making every day count](/learn/making-every-day-count). In Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda, many people find that learning with others helps them stay consistent; the weekly Tanya class is one way.

## Common questions

### How do I build a good spiritual habit?
Tanya's approach is to choose fixed times, act through thought, speech and deed, and repeat. Chapter 14 says that repeated practice becomes second nature.

### Should I wait until I feel inspired?
No. Tanya says that the beinoni's level depends on choice and action, not on feeling. Set a time and begin.

### What are the three garments of the soul?
Thought, speech and action, the ways the soul expresses itself. Torah and mitzvot clothe each of them.

### How often should I renew my intention?
For long study, Chapter 41 mentions renewing it at least once an hour. For everyday habits, ask the Rabbi what suits you.

### What if I miss a day?
Start again. Tanya's approach is steady effort without discouragement; see [why do I still struggle](/learn/why-do-i-still-struggle).
