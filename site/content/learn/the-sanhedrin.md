The Sanhedrin (סנהדרין) was the supreme court and Torah authority of the Jewish people in ancient Israel, made up of 71 sages. It sat in the Temple in Jerusalem, and after the Temple was destroyed it kept meeting in a series of towns in the Land of Israel.

## What does Sanhedrin mean?

The word Sanhedrin comes from the Greek for "sitting together," and the Great Sanhedrin was the highest court of the nation. It decided the cases and questions that lower courts could not settle, and it guided the people in Jewish law. If you want to understand where [halacha](/learn/what-is-halacha) and the [Oral Torah](/learn/oral-torah) were taught and decided, this court is the answer.

## How many judges were in the Sanhedrin?

According to Chabad.org, the Great Sanhedrin had 71 members, led by a Nasi (president), who by tradition descended from King David. The number 71 comes from the Torah (Numbers 11:16). Chabad.org teaches that it goes back to Moses himself, who gathered 70 elders to help him guide the nation. With Moses, that makes 71.

## Where did the Sanhedrin meet?

The Sanhedrin began in the Lishkat HaGazit, the Chamber of Hewn Stone, next to and partly inside the Temple courtyard. It had two entrances. The judges sat in a semicircle in order of seniority, with the Nasi in the center. This was the place where the hardest questions of the law were decided.

## The smaller courts

The Great Sanhedrin was the top of a whole system. Chabad.org describes a court of 23 judges in Israel's larger cities, authorized to rule on the most serious cases, and a local court of three for money cases and everyday matters in smaller communities. The idea of smaller and larger courts goes back to Jethro, Moses' father-in-law. He saw Moses taking the whole burden of justice on himself and suggested a system of lower and higher courts. Chabad.org adds that Moses knew his own system could not last, and so he accepted the advice.

## The Sanhedrin travels

In the time of Roman rule, the Sanhedrin was forced to leave its home. Chabad.org follows the Talmud's list of ten moves in the first and second centuries:

1. From the Chamber of Hewn Stone to the Temple Mount.
2. From the Temple Mount to Jerusalem.
3. From Jerusalem to Yavneh, after the Temple was destroyed.
4. From Yavneh to Usha.
5. Back to Yavneh.
6. To Usha again, after Roman persecutions.
7. From Usha to Shefaram.
8. To Beit She'arim.
9. To Tzippori.
10. To Tiberias.

At times it met in secret, even in an attic in Lod. The move to Yavneh was the work of [Rabbi Yochanan ben Zakkai](/learn/rabbi-yochanan-ben-zakkai), who asked the Romans for "Yavneh and its sages" so that Torah leadership would survive the loss of [the Holy Temple](/learn/the-holy-temple). The Sanhedrin returned to Yavneh and Usha after periods of relief, and Tiberias was its last home.

## The hope of its return

The Talmud calls Tiberias the lowest of the Sanhedrin's locations, but the story ends with hope. Rabbi Yochanan says that the Redemption will arise from that city. For Jews, the Sanhedrin is not only history. It is something we hope will be restored.

## Why this matters to you

Most of what Jewish law teaches us today, from Shabbat to the calendar, was passed down by the sages who studied the Torah together and applied it. The [Talmud](/learn/talmud) records their discussions. Today, we ask a rabbi when we have a question, and whether you live in Lafayette, Moraga, Orinda or anywhere else in Contra Costa County, you are welcome to ask Rabbi Labkowski. Details of how the Sanhedrin worked in each case are complex, so ask the Rabbi for anything beyond this overview.

## Common questions

### What was the Sanhedrin?
The Sanhedrin was the supreme court and Torah authority of the Jewish people in ancient Israel, composed of 71 sages. It decided cases that lower courts could not settle.

### How many judges were in the Sanhedrin?
The Great Sanhedrin had 71 members, headed by a Nasi who by tradition descended from King David. Courts of 23 sat in larger cities.

### Where did the Sanhedrin meet?
It began in the Chamber of Hewn Stone in the Temple in Jerusalem. After the Temple fell it met in Yavneh, Usha, Shefaram, Beit She'arim, Tzippori and finally Tiberias.

### Who started the idea of courts in the Torah?
Chabad.org notes that Jethro, Moses' father-in-law, suggested dividing the work of justice among smaller and larger courts. Moses accepted the advice.

Jewish tradition holds the hope that it will be restored. The Talmud teaches that the Redemption will arise from Tiberias, the Sanhedrin's last location. Ask the Rabbi to explore this further.
