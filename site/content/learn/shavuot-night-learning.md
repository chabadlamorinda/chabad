Once a year, Jews around the world do something unusual: they stay up all night studying Torah. It happens on the first night of Shavuot, the holiday of the giving of the Torah, and it is called *Tikkun Leil Shavuot*, "the repair of Shavuot night." Coffee, cheesecake and a room full of people learning until the sky turns light: for many it is the most memorable night of the Jewish year.

## Why we stay awake

The Midrash tells that on the morning the Torah was to be given at Mount Sinai, the Jewish people overslept. G-d Himself had to wake them with thunder and lightning. Ever since, we make up for that morning by staying awake the whole night before, showing that this time we are ready, eager and waiting.

Chassidic teaching adds a deeper layer. Each year on Shavuot the Torah is given anew, and each of us receives it according to how much we want it. A night of learning is the way we say: we want it.

## What is learned

The traditional text, also called the Tikkun, is a collection of short passages: the beginning and end of every book of the Torah and the Prophets, selections from the Mishnah, the 613 mitzvot, and passages of the Zohar. Reading it is a way of touching the whole Torah in one night.

Most communities today also offer classes and discussions through the night, on anything from the [Ten Commandments](/learn/ten-commandments) to Jewish ethics, the Book of Ruth (read on Shavuot), Kabbalah or current questions. Any Torah learning counts. The point is not to finish a book but to spend the night with the Torah. See [daily Torah study](/learn/daily-torah-study) for how to keep it going afterward.

## Doing it at home

If you cannot come out, or you have small children asleep, learn at home. A few ideas:

- Read the story of the giving of the Torah, Exodus chapters 19 and 20, and talk it through.
- Read the Book of Ruth, a short and beautiful story of loyalty and kindness.
- Pick one of the [613 mitzvot](/learn/613-mitzvot) you have never thought about and find out what it means.
- Learn with a partner, in person or by phone before the holiday begins; Shavuot is a Yom Tov, so phones and screens are put away once it starts.
## The next morning: the Ten Commandments

On the first morning of Shavuot the Ten Commandments are read from the Torah, and the whole congregation stands to hear them, as our ancestors stood at Sinai. The Rebbe asked that every Jewish child, including babies, be brought to hear the reading. The Midrash says that when G-d asked the Jewish people for guarantors that they would keep the Torah, He accepted only one answer: "Our children." Hearing the Ten Commandments is each child's way of standing at Sinai too, and many communities hold a children's program with ice cream right after.

Shavuot is also the holiday of [dairy foods](/learn/shavuot-dairy-foods), and it comes at the end of [counting the Omer](/learn/counting-the-omer), seven weeks after Passover. See [this year's dates and times](/holidays/shavuot).

## Common questions

### Do I have to stay up the whole night?
No. Staying up all night is a beloved custom, not a law. Learn as long as you comfortably can. Those who do stay up say the morning prayers and then sleep during the day.

### I have no Jewish background. Can I still come?
Yes. Shavuot night classes are for everyone, and questions are the best part. Classes are usually in English, with the Hebrew explained. Come as you are.

### Can my children come to the Ten Commandments reading?
Yes, please bring them, even the smallest. The reading is short, and children are the reason we read it. Many synagogues have something for children afterwards; ask the Rabbi what is planned this year.

### Can I drive to the learning on Shavuot night?
Shavuot is a Yom Tov, when driving is traditionally not done, so many people walk or stay nearby. If that is not possible for you, speak with the Rabbi about what works for your family.
