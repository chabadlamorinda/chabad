Yes, it is okay to be angry at G-d. Chabad.org teaches that anger at G-d is permitted and can even be a sign of closeness, because people hold back their feelings from those they are distant from and speak openly to those they trust. If you are hurting, you are not a bad Jew, and you have not lost your place.

If you are in Lafayette, Moraga, Orinda or elsewhere in the East Bay and the pain is fresh, you can call Rabbi Yisroel Labkowski or Rebbetzin Tzipora Labkowski at 925-384-7242 and talk privately. You do not have to have it figured out.

## Anger is a sign of relationship, not the end of it

A Chabad.org writer uses a parent as an analogy. When a parent does something the children cannot understand, they protest. That protest is a sign that they care and that they believe the parent should have a good reason. People who are indifferent do not argue. Another Chabad.org answer, to someone who asked this very question, says that G-d will be embracing you however you respond, and urges you to "stay engaged" and not walk away.

Chabad.org also notes that simply saying "G-d must have His reasons" is not what the Jewish tradition expects from a devastated family. It is permissible, even important, to cry out.

## Our ancestors argued with G-d

This is not new. In the Torah, Abraham challenges G-d over the fate of Sodom: "Shall the Judge of all the earth not do justice?" Moses, after things get worse for the Israelites in Egypt, asks G-d, "Why have You done evil to this people?" The prophets and the Psalms are full of protest and tears. The Torah did not hide these voices. It kept them.

If you want to read more about why suffering exists, see [why bad things happen](/learn/why-bad-things-happen).

## How to bring your anger into prayer

Prayer does not have to be polite. You can say what is in your heart in any language, in your own words, see [talking to G-d in your own words](/learn/talking-to-g-d-in-your-own-words).

1. **Say it out loud or write it down.** "I am angry. I do not understand. This hurts."
2. **Say it to G-d, not only about G-d.** Chabad.org writes that a person cries out passionately only to someone with whom they are truly and intimately engaged.
3. **Ask a different question when you are ready.** Chabad.org suggests moving gradually from "Why did this happen?" to "What can I do now?" since the "why" may never be fully answered in this life.
4. **Do one small mitzvah.** Light Shabbat candles, give charity, or say a Psalm. It does not have to feel inspiring.

## What the Rebbe taught about pain

According to Chabad.org, the Rebbe, Rabbi Menachem M. Schneerson, said he could not give a definitive reason for any specific tragedy. He taught that life has a before and an after, that the soul continues beyond death, and that pain may look senseless only because our view is limited. "Much more will be revealed in the Afterlife." This is not meant as a quick fix and it will not erase anyone's loss. It is an invitation to hold on to hope. See [hope in hard times](/learn/hope-in-hard-times).

## Kaddish: faith inside pain

Mourners say [Kaddish](/learn/kaddish), which praises G-d's greatness, even in their grief. Many describe this as saying "I am broken, and I am still here." That is faith standing next to anger. You are allowed to hold both.

## Common questions

### Is it okay to be angry at G-d in Judaism?
Yes. Chabad.org says that anger at G-d is permitted and can reflect a close relationship. Silence and walking away are what Judaism worries about, not honest anger.

### Does being angry at G-d mean I do not believe?
No. You cannot be angry at someone you think is not there. Anger often shows that you are engaged.

### Did anyone in the Torah argue with G-d?
Yes. Abraham pleaded for Sodom, and Moses asked why the Israelites suffered more after his first visit to Pharaoh.

### What should I say to G-d when I am angry?
Say exactly what you feel, in your own words. You can also say Psalms (see [Tehillim](/learn/tehillim)). There is no wrong way.

### Why did G-d let this happen?
Chabad.org says the Rebbe taught that no one can give a definite reason for a tragedy and that much will be revealed later. If this is your question after a loss, please talk privately with the Rabbi.

### Can I still come to synagogue and the holidays if I am angry?
Yes, always. You are welcome as you are. See [grief during the holidays](/learn/grief-during-the-holidays).
