Bringing a meal to someone who is sick, grieving or in need is a classic act of Jewish kindness: ask first what would help, bring food that fits how the family eats, and keep it simple. In Jewish tradition, neighbors and friends traditionally provide the mourners' first meal after a funeral, so cooking for others is part of how a community cares.

## Why a meal matters

Chesed (חֶסֶד) is the Hebrew word for kindness. Chabad.org explains that chesed generally means non-monetary acts such as visiting the sick and inviting guests. A meal is a kindness of both hands and heart. Chabad.org's guide to visiting the sick says to find out what the person needs and to offer help. For someone who is unwell or overwhelmed, a prepared meal removes one worry. See [Chesed](/learn/chesed).

## For mourners: the first meal

Chabad.org describes the seudat havara'ah (סְעוּדַת הֲבָרָאָה), the "meal of recovery," that mourners eat right after returning from the funeral. It is provided by neighbors, close friends or relatives, not by the mourners themselves, though if no one provides it, they may use their own food.

- **The foods:** bread, preferably round rolls such as bagels, and peeled hard-boiled eggs. After that, other foods and drinks are permitted.
- **Why:** the meal comforts the mourners by showing they are not alone, and it encourages those who may not feel like eating to eat.

After that first meal, Chabad.org notes that many communities continue supplying mourners with food, since they have so much else on their minds. At the same time, it says that most Ashkenazic communities, including Chabad, do not generally put out food for visitors during shivah. Chabad families do toast "l'chaim" on the seventh day, right after shivah ends. For visiting a shivah house, see [What to Bring to a Shiva House](/learn/what-to-bring-to-a-shiva-house).

## For someone who is sick

Chabad.org encourages practical help when you visit the sick, and a meal is one more way to help. Ask what the person and the household need. If the illness is serious, the family may be too tired to cook. See [Bikur Cholim](/learn/bikur-cholim).

## For new parents

Bringing food after a birth is also a mitzvah. See [Bringing Meals to New Parents](/learn/meals-for-new-parents) for the details.

## Before you cook, check these things

- **Ask first.** A short call or text lets the family say what they need and when.
- **Ask about kosher.** If the family keeps kosher, food must be prepared and packaged accordingly. Ask them how they keep kosher and what they would like. If you are unsure, ask the Rabbi. [Bay Kosher](/programs/bay-kosher), a project of Chabad of Lamorinda, is a local source of kosher items and is always kosher.
- **Ask about allergies and dietary needs.**
- **Do not overwhelm.** One meal at a time, perhaps in coordination with others, so the family is not given more than it can store.
- **Respect dignity.** Offer warmly and quietly, as a friend, not as charity. See [Giving Anonymously](/learn/giving-anonymously).

## Common questions

### What do you bring to a Jewish family sitting shivah?
Food is welcome, as long as the family keeps kosher and you have asked how. Chabad.org notes that Chabad generally does not put out food for visitors during shivah, so ask the family or the Rabbi what they prefer.

### Who provides the first meal after a Jewish funeral?
Neighbors, close friends or relatives. That meal is called the seudat havara'ah, and it traditionally includes bread (often round rolls) and hard-boiled eggs.

### Should food for a Jewish family be kosher?
Ask whether they keep kosher. If they do, bring only kosher food prepared as they keep it. When unsure, ask the Rabbi.

### How can I bring a meal to someone who is sick without being a burden?
Ask first what would help, and offer to bring it at a time that suits the family.

### What if I do not know the family well?
That is fine. A short note and a meal are enough, and you can ask the Rabbi for guidance.
