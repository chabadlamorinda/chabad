Caring for someone you love who is ill, disabled or aging is one of the greatest acts of kindness, and one of the most exhausting. If you are a caregiver, this guide is for you.

## What you are doing is holy

Visiting and caring for the sick (bikur cholim) is a mitzvah that G-d Himself did, visiting Abraham after his brit. Caring for a parent fulfills honoring parents; caring for a spouse is the heart of a Jewish marriage; caring for a child is the deepest love. Every meal, ride and sleepless night counts.

## You cannot pour from an empty cup

Jewish law teaches that a person must also guard their own health. Burnout helps no one.

- **Sleep, eat and move,** even a little.
- **Take breaks** and accept respite care when available.
- **Keep one thing that is yours:** a class, a walk, Shabbat with friends.
- **Watch for warning signs:** constant exhaustion, irritability, hopelessness. Talk to a doctor or counselor.

## Ask for help, and let people help

People often want to help but don't know how. Give them specific tasks: a meal on Tuesday, a ride to an appointment, an hour with your mother so you can rest. Letting others do a mitzvah is a gift to them too.

## Spiritual strength

- **Prayer:** ask the Rabbi to say a [Mi Sheberach](/learn/mi-sheberach) for your loved one; add their name to the Tehillim list.
- **Shabbat:** even a simple Shabbat gives a weekly island of rest.
- **[Bitachon](/learn/bitachon):** doing our part and trusting G-d with the rest.

## Common questions

### I feel guilty that I sometimes resent caregiving. Is that wrong?
It is human. Feelings are not sins. What matters is the love you show in action, and getting support so you can keep going.

### Is it okay to move my parent to a care facility?
Often it is the most responsible choice for their safety and your health. Jewish law focuses on making sure they are well cared for and honored. Ask the Rabbi about your situation.
