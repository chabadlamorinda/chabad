Judaism teaches that work is holy but never meant to consume you: be "occupied" with your work but not "preoccupied" by it. Burnout, the exhaustion that comes when work drains you in body, mind and spirit, is a sign to rest, reset and, where possible, change something.

For parents and young professionals in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda, who often commute, manage teams and still run the household, this is a very Jewish question.

## Work is a vessel, not the source

A Chassidic teaching on "passive labor," on Chabad.org, says that a believer works with the hands while the mind and heart stay with G-d. Work does not itself create income. It is the vessel that receives G-d's blessing. So we are occupied with our tasks, not preoccupied by them.

Another Chabad.org article puts it this way: "Our efforts are the vessel, but it is G-d who fills the vessel with His blessing." That is freeing. If you are not the sole source of your family's income, you can do your best without carrying the whole world.

## When work drains you

A Chabad.org response to a reader stuck in a draining job says that work which exhausts a person harms physical, emotional, spiritual and family health. It notes that the body is the soul's vessel, so caring for your health is a religious duty, not a luxury.

Some steps it suggests:

1. **Notice what energizes you.** Keep a short journal of the activities and people that lift your spirit and those that leave you heavy.
2. **Explore gradually.** Talk to people about their careers, learn what different roles involve, consider part-time options.
3. **Talk with your spouse.** Share your wish for a healthier job, acknowledge the financial concern and say you are exploring, not leaping.
4. **Be patient and kind to yourself.** Large changes grow from small steps.

If you feel low, hopeless or unable to function, that is more than a bad week. Read [the Jewish view of mental health](/learn/mental-health-jewish-view) and speak to a doctor or therapist. Seeking help is a mitzvah, not a weakness.

## Shabbat: the built-in off switch

If the six workdays are lived with some inner distance, [Shabbat](/learn/shabbat) brings real rest. For 25 hours there is no email, no laptop, no phone. Many professionals in tech and medicine say that Shabbat is the one thing that has kept them going.

Busy parents can start small. See [Shabbat for working parents](/learn/shabbat-for-working-parents) for practical ideas, and [Shabbat times](/shabbat-times) for this week.

## Start the day with thanks, not email

Before you pick up your phone, say [Modeh Ani](/learn/modeh-ani), the short thank-you prayer for waking up. It takes ten seconds. It reminds you that you start the day with a soul returned by G-d, not with an inbox. Many people find that one small habit changes the tone of the day.

## Purpose in what you do

Judaism sees honest work as a way to serve G-d: providing for your family, supporting charity, treating colleagues with respect. Even a tedious job can carry meaning when it feeds a family or funds [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah). Meaning will not fix an impossible workload, but it can change how you carry it. For deeper reading, see [work and livelihood](/learn/parnassah-work-and-livelihood).

## Common questions

### What does Judaism say about burnout?
Judaism teaches that your health matters, that work is a vessel for G-d's blessing and not the source of it, and that Shabbat is a weekly rest for everyone. If a job is harming your health or family, it is appropriate to look for change gradually and ask for advice.

### Is rest a Jewish value?
Yes. Shabbat, one day in seven, is a commandment to stop working and rest, and it is among the Ten Commandments. Jewish tradition treats rest as holy, not lazy.

### What if I am too busy to pray?
Start small: Modeh Ani when you wake up takes seconds. Add more when you can, and ask the Rabbi to help build a routine that fits your life.

### How can I have work-life balance as a Jew?
Be fully present for work during the week, and then really stop on Shabbat. Keep the mindset that you are "occupied, not preoccupied" with work. Ask the Rabbi about your particular situation.

### When should I get professional help?
If you feel constantly low, anxious, hopeless or unable to function, see a doctor or therapist, and talk to the Rabbi too. Judaism encourages seeking medical care.
