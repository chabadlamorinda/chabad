Itkafya (אתכפיא) is the Aramaic word Tanya uses for subduing your own impulses, and in Chapter 27 it also means small, deliberate acts of holding back, even from things that are permitted. Tanya teaches that such a small act of self-discipline here below has a great effect Above.

This is a gentle and very practical form of self-discipline: not harsh, not about suffering, but about showing yourself that you are in charge of your impulses.

## What is itkafya?

According to Chabad.org's presentation of Chapter 27, itkafya means subduing the "other side" within us (the sitra achara). Each time a person pushes away a wrong thought, he subdues it below. The Alter Rebbe, Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi, then extends the idea in a surprising direction: to things that are completely permitted.

Permitted activities done with no higher purpose, the chapter explains, draw their life from the "other side." So a person who holds himself back from a permitted act for a moment, and uses that time for something holy, has subdued it.

## Small examples from Tanya

Chapter 27 gives concrete cases:

- **Delaying a permitted meal** for an hour or less, and using that time for Torah study.
- **Holding back permitted speech** about everyday matters.
- **Holding back permitted thoughts** about mundane things.

The examples are small. This is what makes the idea so usable. It does not ask for fasting, giving up everything you enjoy, or a monastic life. It asks for a pause.

## Why a small act matters so much

The Alter Rebbe quotes the Sages' teaching that a person who sanctifies himself a little below is sanctified greatly from above. The chapter says that an effort from below causes a corresponding stirring above. It also cites the Zohar: when the negative force is subdued here, G-d's glory is raised above all, more than by any other praise.

You do not have to understand the mystical details to take the practical point: small steps count, and a small act of discipline is not small.

## How to try it

These are suggestions in the spirit of Tanya's examples, not rules from the chapter:

1. **Pick one small thing.** Wait a few minutes before checking your phone, or take a breath before sending a clever reply.
2. **Use the time for something good**, such as a verse, a [blessing](/learn/eating-as-a-mitzvah) or a kind thought.
3. **Keep it small and cheerful.** Tanya's whole approach to the struggle is joy and alacrity (see [Tanya on sadness and joy](/learn/tanya-on-sadness-and-joy)).
4. **Notice the effect.** Do you feel more in charge of the rest of your day?

For turning small efforts into lasting routines, see [changing a bad habit](/learn/changing-a-bad-habit).

## Limits and care

Itkafya is not a license for extreme dieting, fasting or denying yourself needed rest or food. Judaism values health and the enjoyment of permitted things. If you struggle with an eating disorder, or if self-denial is becoming harmful, please see a doctor or therapist; Tanya's guidance is spiritual and professional help is also right. For personal questions, ask the Rabbi.

## Common questions

### What does itkafya mean?
Itkafya means subduing or bending your own impulses. In Tanya, Chapter 27, it includes pushing away wrong thoughts and also small acts of holding back from permitted things.

### Does Judaism want me to give up pleasures?
No. Tanya's examples are small and temporary, like waiting a short time before eating. Judaism values enjoying permitted things in the right way.

### How long should I delay a meal?
Tanya's example is a short delay, an hour or less, used for Torah study; it is not a fast or a diet. If you have diabetes or any medical condition, are pregnant or nursing, or have ever struggled with eating, choose a different small act, such as holding back a word or a thought, and ask your doctor and the Rabbi.

### How is this different from resisting temptation?
Temptation is about forbidden things. Itkafya is also about permitted things, to show that you are in charge. See [how to resist temptation](/learn/tanya-on-temptation).

### Why does a small act of self-control matter?
The Sages teach that a person who sanctifies himself a little below is sanctified greatly from above. Tanya applies this to small acts of holding back.
