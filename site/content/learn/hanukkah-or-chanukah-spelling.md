Both Hanukkah and Chanukah are correct English spellings of the Hebrew word חֲנֻכָּה. The Hebrew begins with the letter chet (ח), a throat-clearing sound English lacks, so "Chanukah" is pronounced *kha-noo-kah*, never "tcha-noo-kah."

Chabad.org uses both and says that regardless of how you spell it, the holiday is celebrated the same way: by lighting the menorah each night, singing praises to G-d and expressing gratitude.

## How to say it

The Hebrew word is חנוכה, and it is pronounced *kha-noo-kah*. The first sound is the Hebrew letter chet (ח). In Ashkenazic tradition, chet is pronounced as a guttural sound made at the back of the throat, as in German. In Sephardic tradition it is a softer sound, close to an English "h." The word starts with that sound, not with the "ch" of "church." For a simple guide to the letters, see [the Hebrew alphabet](/learn/hebrew-alphabet).

## Why there are so many spellings

Hebrew has sounds that English does not, so every English spelling is an approximation. Chabad.org explains the main choices:

- **Hanukkah.** "H" is the most common English spelling. It is imperfect but easy to pronounce. Sephardic Jews and many non-Jews favor it, since it matches the Sephardic sound.
- **Chanukah.** Ashkenazic Jews traditionally favor "ch," which represents the guttural sound, as in German and in names like Menachem and Pesach.
- **Doubled k.** Some purists double the "k" to show the dagesh, a dot in the Hebrew letter. That gives "Hanukkah," as in "Shabbat." Most Ashkenazic and some Sephardic traditions do not keep that distinction, so the single "k" is just as common.
- **Khanike.** Yiddish scholars sometimes use this, but Chabad.org calls it uncommon and confusing in English text.

Chabad.org's holiday FAQ puts it simply: both Chanukah and Hanukkah are correct. Chanukah is preferred by Ashkenazim, and Hanukkah is more common among Sephardim, in Modern Hebrew translations and among non-Jews. Our website uses Chanukah, the Ashkenazic spelling.

## What the name means

Chanukah means "dedication" (it is also translated "inauguration"), recalling the rededication of the Holy Temple by the Maccabees. For more, read [what Chanukah means](/learn/what-does-chanukah-mean) and the [Chanukah story](/learn/chanukah-story).

## What to say to a friend

The most common greeting is simply "Happy Hanukkah" or "Happy Chanukah," and it works in any setting. In Hebrew it is *Chanukah sameach* (חנוכה שמח), and in Yiddish, *Freilichen Chanukah*. Chabad.org's glossary lists both traditional greetings. See [holiday greetings](/learn/holiday-greetings) and [Yiddish words and phrases](/learn/yiddish-words-and-phrases).

## Common questions

### Is it Hanukkah or Chanukah?
Both are correct. Chanukah is the spelling Ashkenazic Jews traditionally prefer, and Hanukkah is more common among Sephardim, in Modern Hebrew translations and among non-Jews.

### How do you pronounce Chanukah?
Say *kha-noo-kah*, with the first sound made at the back of the throat. It is never "tcha-noo-kah" as in "church."

### Why is there a "C" in Chanukah?
The "ch" in Chanukah stands for the Hebrew letter chet (ח), which English has no letter for. Ashkenazic Jews chose "ch," as in the names Menachem and Pesach.

### How do you say "Happy Chanukah" in Hebrew and Yiddish?
In Hebrew say *Chanukah sameach*, and in Yiddish say *Freilichen Chanukah*. In English, "Happy Chanukah" works everywhere.

### Why do some people write Hanukkah with two k's?
Some purists double the "k" to show the dagesh in the Hebrew spelling, as in "Shabbat." Most traditions do not keep the distinction, so one "k" is just as correct.
