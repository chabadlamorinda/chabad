Chanukah is a home holiday, but life does not always keep us home for eight nights. The good news: the menorah travels well. Here is the general guidance; for your own situation, ask the Rabbi.

## The rule of thumb: light where you sleep

The mitzvah of Chanukah lights belongs to the home, and your "home" on any given night is the place where you sleep and eat. A traveler lights in the hotel room; a student lights in the dorm; a guest lights in the host's house. Whatever the place, the basics are the same: the lights go in a window or doorway where people can see them, they are lit after nightfall, and they burn for at least half an hour. See [how to light the menorah](/learn/chanukah-menorah).

## When you are a guest in someone's home

If you are eating and sleeping at someone else's home, you become part of that household for the night. Traditionally a guest shares in the host's menorah by contributing a small coin toward the oil or candles, and is then covered by the host's lighting. Many people prefer to light their own menorah alongside the host's, which is the Ashkenazi custom in any case; the more light, the better.

## When your family lights at home without you

A married person whose spouse lights at home is, in principle, covered by that lighting even from far away. Still, many travelers light their own menorah, and whether to say the blessings depends on customs and details. This is the classic "ask the Rabbi" case: one call before the trip settles it.

## College

A student living away from home lights in the dorm room or apartment, with the blessings. Fire rules are the practical problem: some campuses forbid open flames in rooms. Many students light in a kitchen or lounge where a flame is permitted, or at the campus Chabad house with other students. Where no flame is allowed at all, an electric menorah may be lit without the blessings. See [Chanukah at work, in a dorm or while traveling](/learn/chanukah-at-work-and-college) and [Jewish college students](/learn/jewish-college-students).

## Hotels and travel

In a hotel room, light near the window if the hotel allows candles, on a tray with nothing flammable nearby, and stay in the room while they burn. Small travel menorahs and tea lights make this easy; pack them in checked luggage if you are flying. If the hotel does not allow flames, ask whether you may light in the lobby, which many hotels welcome, or use an electric menorah without the blessings. Never light on an airplane. Coming home late? You can still light as long as someone in the house is awake to see the lights; if you will be out all night, ask the Rabbi. On Friday and Saturday night the timing changes: read [Chanukah on Shabbat](/learn/chanukah-on-shabbat) and check [Shabbat times](/shabbat-times) for where you will be. See [kosher when traveling](/learn/kosher-when-traveling) for the rest of the trip.

## In the hospital

Hospitals do not allow open flames, and health always comes first; Judaism is clear about that. A patient can light an electric menorah by the bed, without the blessings, say Haneirot Halalu and sing; many hospitals have a menorah lighting in a lobby or chapel. If the patient's family lights at home, the patient shares in their lighting too. Call the Rabbi if you or someone you love is in the hospital in Lafayette, Moraga or Orinda. See also [Shabbat in the hospital](/learn/shabbat-in-the-hospital) and [when someone is sick](/help/someone-is-sick).

## Common questions

### I forgot to pack a menorah. What now?
Any safe arrangement of candles in a row, at the same height and far enough apart to look like separate flames, is a kosher menorah. Tea lights on a foil-covered tray work. Or call the nearest Chabad; they almost always have a menorah and candles to give you.

### Can I light at home before I leave for an evening out?
Lighting before nightfall is a question of details; sometimes it is allowed from a certain point in the late afternoon with enough fuel to burn into the night. Ask the Rabbi for your case. Lighting late at night when you return is usually the better option.

### Does an electric menorah count?
Electric lights do not fulfill the mitzvah, so the blessings are not said over them, but where real flames are forbidden they still proclaim the miracle. You can also light [tonight's candles online](/menorah) with the blessings.
