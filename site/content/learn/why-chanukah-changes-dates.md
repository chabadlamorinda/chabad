Chanukah begins on the eve of the 25th of Kislev every year on the Hebrew calendar, so it only seems to move on the secular calendar. The Hebrew calendar follows the moon, and the secular calendar follows the sun, so Chanukah lands on a different date each year.

To find this year's dates, light times and the nightly order, see [/jewish-holidays](/jewish-holidays), [/menorah](/menorah) and [/this-week](/this-week). We keep them up to date automatically, so you will not need to count.

## The date never changes on the Jewish calendar

Jewish tradition gives the same answer every year: Chanukah begins on 25 Kislev and lasts eight days. It starts on the evening before, the evening that begins the 25th, and it ends when the eighth day is over. The month of Kislev is the month of Chanukah, and you can read about it in [Kislev: the month of Chanukah](/learn/kislev). Jewish law also notes that on the secular calendar Chanukah usually falls in December.

## Why it moves on the secular calendar

The Hebrew calendar is based on the moon. As the tradition explains, twelve lunar months make 354 days, a little more than 11 days short of the solar year of about 365 and a quarter days. A calendar that followed only twelve lunar months would drift through the seasons. The secular calendar follows the sun and so stays fixed to the seasons. When two calendars of different length run side by side, a date on one slides across the other. For the Hebrew calendar in general, see [the Jewish calendar](/learn/jewish-calendar).

## How leap years keep the holidays in their seasons

To keep the festivals in the seasons the Torah gives them, Jewish years are grouped in cycles of 19 years, and seven times in each cycle a leap year adds an extra month. A leap year has 13 months instead of 12. The extra month is Adar I, added before the regular Adar, which is then called Adar II. Because of this, the festivals keep their place in the seasons, and Chanukah stays a winter holiday instead of sliding around the year. Read more in [the Jewish leap year and calendar](/learn/jewish-leap-year-and-calendar).

## Chanukah can start on almost any day of the week

Here is a surprising fact. Most Jewish holidays begin on only four days of the week. Because the month before Kislev, Cheshvan, can have 29 or 30 days, Chanukah can start on any day of the week except Tuesday. This is why in some years the first night falls on a Friday, so the menorah is lit before the Shabbat candles, and in other years it begins on a different day. See [Chanukah on Shabbat](/learn/chanukah-on-shabbat).

## Common questions

### Why does Chanukah change dates every year?
Chanukah is always 25 Kislev on the Jewish calendar, but the Jewish calendar follows the moon and the secular calendar follows the sun, so the date shifts on the secular calendar.

### Is Chanukah always in December?
Not always, but it usually is. Jewish tradition says it usually falls in December. See [/jewish-holidays](/jewish-holidays) for each year's dates.

### What day does Chanukah start on the Hebrew calendar?
It begins on the evening before 25 Kislev and lasts eight days, ending when the eighth day is over.

### Why does the Jewish calendar have a leap year?
A year of twelve lunar months is about 11 days shorter than the solar year. Seven times in every 19 years an extra month, Adar I, is added to keep the festivals in their proper seasons.

### Can Chanukah start on any day of the week?
It can start on any day except Tuesday, because the month of Cheshvan can have 29 or 30 days.
