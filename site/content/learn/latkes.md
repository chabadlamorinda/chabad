Latkes are crispy fried potato pancakes, a favorite Chanukah food because they are cooked in oil, which recalls the miracle of the oil that burned for eight days. The secret to a crisp latke is pressing as much water as you can out of the grated potatoes before frying.

## Why we eat latkes on Chanukah

Chanukah remembers the Temple oil that lasted eight days when there was only enough for one. Because of that miracle, it is customary to eat foods cooked in oil, and Chabad.org names potato pancakes (*latkes*) and jelly doughnuts (*sufganiyot*, see our [sufganiyot](/learn/sufganiyot) guide) as popular favorites. Read more in [Chanukah foods](/learn/chanukah-foods) and the [Chanukah story](/learn/chanukah-story).

## A simple latke recipe

This recipe is retold from a basic recipe on Chabad.org. It is pareve (neither meat nor dairy), serves 4 to 6, and takes 30 to 60 minutes.

**You will need:**

- 5 large potatoes, peeled
- 1 large onion
- 3 eggs
- 1/3 cup flour
- 1 teaspoon salt
- 1/4 teaspoon pepper
- About 3/4 cup oil for frying, and a 10-inch skillet

**Steps:**

1. Grate the potatoes and onion on the fine side of a grater, or in a food processor.
2. Put the mixture in a colander and press out as much liquid as you can.
3. Stir in the eggs, flour, salt and pepper.
4. Heat about 1/2 cup of oil in the skillet, then lower the flame.
5. Drop in the batter by the large tablespoon. Fry about 5 minutes on the first side until golden, then turn and fry 2 to 3 minutes on the other.
6. Drain on paper towels and add more oil as needed for each batch.
7. Serve warm with applesauce, or sour cream if you are serving a dairy meal.

## Tips for crisp latkes

- **Squeeze well.** Extra water makes latkes soggy.
- **Do not crowd the pan,** so each latke has room to brown.
- **Serve right away,** when they are crispiest.

## Variations

Chabad.org suggests replacing the potatoes with 5 medium zucchini or 5 medium carrots for vegetable latkes. Some families add herbs or serve a mix of kinds so everyone can choose. There is also a dairy side to Chanukah: Chabad.org notes the custom of eating dairy foods, based on the story of Judith, who used cheese to defeat the enemy general. So cheese dishes at your Chanukah table have a place too.

## Kosher notes

Keeping a kosher kitchen makes latkes simple. Use kosher-certified processed ingredients (see [kosher symbols](/learn/kosher-symbols)), check any leafy greens or herbs for insects, and keep meat and dairy separate. Latkes with only the ingredients above are [pareve](/learn/pareve), so they can go with a meat meal; if you serve them with sour cream, plan a dairy meal. If you are unsure about your kitchen, ask the Rabbi.

## Frying safely with children

Hot oil can burn. Keep the pan on the back burner, handles turned in, children at a distance, and an adult always standing at the stove. Let kids help with the safe parts: mixing, setting the table and choosing toppings.

## Common questions

### Why do we eat latkes on Chanukah?
Latkes are fried in oil, recalling the miracle of the Temple oil that lasted eight days. Chabad.org lists potato pancakes and jelly doughnuts as customary oily foods.

### How do I make latkes crispy?
Squeeze as much liquid as possible out of the grated potatoes and onion, and fry in hot oil without crowding the pan. Serve them right away.

### Are latkes pareve?
A latke made with potatoes, onion, eggs, flour, salt, pepper and oil is pareve if the ingredients are kosher certified. Toppings like sour cream make them dairy.

### Can I make latkes with other vegetables?
Yes. Chabad.org suggests using 5 medium zucchini or 5 medium carrots in place of the potatoes.

### Why do some people eat dairy on Chanukah?
Chabad.org notes the custom, based on the story of Judith, who used cheese to entice and defeat the enemy general.
