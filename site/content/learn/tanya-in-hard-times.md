The Tanya does not tell a suffering person to smile. It offers a different strength: the idea that G-d is present even in pain, and that what hurts may hold a good that is hidden from us for now. This guide is an invitation to that wisdom, never a demand.

## First, a word of honesty

If you are going through illness, a lost job, a broken marriage or any real pain, nobody should tell you to "just be happy." Crying is human. Asking for help, from a doctor, a counselor, friends or the Rabbi, is right and good. See [angry at G-d](/learn/angry-at-g-d) for the Jewish view that your anger and pain are welcome in prayer.

Tanya's guidance is spiritual. It sits beside professional help, not in place of it.

## Hidden good: what Epistle 11 and Chapter 26 teach

The [Tanya](/learn/tanya) was written by Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi, the Alter Rebbe, founder of Chabad. Two places in it speak directly to hard times. We tell them in our own words, as they appear on Chabad.org.

**Epistle 11 (Iggeret HaKodesh).** The Alter Rebbe writes that nothing evil comes down from Above. Everything is good, but sometimes its goodness is too lofty for us to see, and the good can be "clothed" in pain until it is revealed. This is the idea in [Tanya on worry and trust](/learn/tanya-on-worry-and-trust).

**Chapter 26.** Chapter 26 says that just as one blesses G-d for good, one should accept hardship with joy. It explains that hardship seems negative because it comes from a hidden level of G-dliness, higher than the open good we usually recognize. The chapter says those who love G-d, and value closeness to Him above comfort, can find joy even in suffering.

These are big ideas. Few people feel them in the middle of a crisis, and the Tanya does not say you must.

## What Tanya offers when we cannot feel any of this

Here is what the same pages give us, in simple terms.

- **Strength from joy.** Chapter 26 compares the inner struggle to wrestling. A strong person who is sluggish still loses. Energy and an open heart win. Joy here means inner life and energy, not a forced smile.
- **Fixed time for sadness, then release.** The chapter says real sadness has its place, in prayer and in contemplating one's shortcomings, and then one should let it go and trust G-d's forgiveness. It is not meant to follow you through every hour.
- **A purpose in the struggle.** The chapter says the reward of those who accept hardship is a fuller revelation of G-d, in time.
- **You are not alone.** Your soul, in Tanya's words on Chabad.org, is "a part of G-d above" (see [the soul](/learn/the-soul)). Even in the dark, that connection is not broken.

## Small steps for hard days

These are suggestions in the spirit of the text, not quotations from it.

1. Let yourself say it out loud, to G-d in your own words and to a person.
2. Do one small mitzvah today: give a coin to tzedakah, light Shabbat candles, say Modeh Ani.
3. Ask for help, practically and without shame. See [hope in hard times](/learn/hope-in-hard-times).
4. Remember that [why bad things happen](/learn/why-bad-things-happen) is a question Judaism has always asked, openly.

## Common questions

### What does the Tanya say about suffering?
The Tanya teaches that nothing truly evil comes from Above, that apparent suffering holds a hidden good, and that we should serve G-d with joy. It also offers strength, not guilt. For a personal situation, ask the Rabbi.

### Does Tanya say I have to be happy when things are bad?
No. The Tanya invites us toward joy as a source of strength, and describes a high ideal, but nothing in it says that crying or asking for help is wrong. Be gentle with yourself.

### Is sadness a sin in Judaism?
Sadness is human. The Tanya says real sadness has a time and place, such as remorse during prayer, and that we should not let heaviness take over our whole day. If sadness is heavy or lasting, professional help is also the right step.

### Where can I find comfort in Jewish tradition when I am struggling?

### Is this the same as saying "everything happens for a reason"?
Not quite. The Tanya says the good may be hidden, and it never tells a suffering person that their pain is small. It asks us to hold faith and to keep acting kindly and asking for help.
