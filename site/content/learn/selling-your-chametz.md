Every spring, Jews around the world do something that sounds strange the first time you hear it: they sell their bread, pasta, whisky and cereal to a non-Jew for the week of Passover, and buy it all back afterwards. It is one of the most practical customs in Jewish life, and you can do it from your phone. Here is what it is and why it matters.

## Why we sell chametz

The Torah does not only forbid eating [chametz](/learn/chametz) on Passover; it forbids owning it. "No leaven shall be seen with you, and no leaven shall be found in your borders." For most of Jewish history this meant finishing or burning everything before the holiday. But a family may own more chametz than it can use up, and a business may hold a warehouse of it. So the sages arranged a real, legally binding sale to a non-Jew for the days of Passover. The chametz is no longer yours, so you own none on the holiday, exactly as the Torah asks.

There is a second reason too. Chametz that a Jew owned during Passover may not be eaten or benefited from even after the holiday. A sale protects you from that: when you buy it back, it was never a Jew's chametz on Passover.

## How the sale works

You do not sell the chametz yourself. You appoint the Rabbi as your agent, in a short document called a *shtar harsha'ah* (authorization), listing roughly where your chametz is. Before the time chametz becomes forbidden on the morning before Passover, the Rabbi sells all the chametz of everyone who authorized him to a non-Jew, in a binding contract with a down payment, according to both Jewish and civil law. The buyer may come and take it; in practice he does not, but the sale is real, and that is the point.

## What to put away, and where

Gather every chametz item you are keeping: breads and crackers, pasta, cereals, flour, cookies, beer and grain spirits, sauces and other foods that are not kosher for Passover, and anything you are unsure about. Put them in one or a few places: a cabinet, a closet, a corner of the garage, a shelf of the pantry. Close it, tape it shut or cover it, and write "Chametz, sold" on it. During Passover these places are rented to the buyer along with the chametz, so you do not open them or use what is inside.

Pots, pans and dishes used for chametz are also put away for the holiday; the chametz residue on them is included in the sale. Everything else in the home is cleaned, searched the night before Passover and burned in the morning. See [chametz](/learn/chametz) and [kosher for Passover](/learn/kosher-for-passover).

## Buying it back

After Passover ends, the Rabbi buys the chametz back from the non-Jew on behalf of everyone who sold. Wait a little while after the holiday is over, about an hour, before opening the cabinet; the Rabbi announces when the buyback is done.

## Common questions

### Is selling chametz really valid, if everyone knows I will buy it back?
Yes. The sale is a true contract: the buyer pays, takes ownership and could keep the goods by paying the balance. That he usually chooses to sell it back is his decision.

### I do not keep a kosher kitchen. Should I still sell my chametz?
Yes. Every Jew is asked not to own chametz on Passover, whatever else they do or do not keep. Selling it is an easy mitzvah that takes two minutes, and many families find it is the first step toward a fuller Passover.

### Can I sell chametz that is in my office or my car?
Yes. List every place in the form: home, office, car, a second home, a storage unit. Put the chametz in a closed place in each, and it is included.
