To talk to your children about antisemitism, stay calm, use simple words for their age, listen first, and ground the conversation in Jewish pride rather than fear. Children who know and love who they are, and who know a trusted adult will always listen, can face hurtful words with strength.

## Start from pride, not fear

Chabad.org's teachings from the Rebbe emphasize maintaining pride in yourself as a Jew and in your Jewish observance, rather than focusing on hatred itself. The same article cautions against confronting people by labeling them antisemites, and against changing Jewish practice to appease anyone. That is a good frame for children too: before they hear about hate, they should hear what is beautiful about being Jewish. Read [Jewish pride](/learn/jewish-pride) and [facing antisemitism](/learn/facing-antisemitism).

## By age

**Ages 3 to 7.** Keep it very short. If a child comes home with a hurtful remark: "Some people say unkind things about Jews because they do not understand. It is wrong. You are loved, and being Jewish is wonderful." Reassure them they are safe.

**Ages 8 to 12.** Answer questions honestly without frightening details. Ask "What did you hear? How did it feel?" and listen without interrupting. Explain that hateful words say something about the speaker, not about us. Practice a calm sentence: "That is not okay."

**Teens.** Teens may want history and depth. Be honest and calm, and invite their questions. Encourage them to talk with a trusted adult before reacting online. See [Jewish identity for teens](/learn/jewish-identity-for-teens). This guide is non-political, so for conversations about current events, it is best to speak with the Rabbi.

## If something happens at school

We cannot give legal or school-policy advice, but families often find these steps helpful:

1. **Listen and thank your child** for telling you. Stay calm and believe them.
2. **Write down what happened:** what was said, when, who was present. Save any messages.
3. **Tell the school** promptly and ask what they will do. Keep your tone steady.
4. **Check in with your child** in the days afterward.
5. **Tell the Rabbi.** Call 925-384-7242. We can listen, help you think through next steps and support your child.

If a child's safety is at risk, contact the school and the proper authorities immediately.

## Answer hate with more light

The Rebbe taught that darkness is not driven out by fighting the dark but by adding light. Chabad.org describes how the Rebbe encouraged channeling difficult moments into a deeper engagement with Jewish identity. For a family, this can mean:

- Lighting [Shabbat candles](/learn/shabbat-candles) on Friday night.
- Doing one extra mitzvah or act of [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah) together.
- Celebrating [Chanukah](/holidays/chanukah) proudly, with the menorah in the window.
- Joining a community that celebrates, so children see Jewish joy is bigger than any insult.

## Common questions

### How do I talk to my child about antisemitism?
Stay calm, use age-appropriate words, listen first, and root the conversation in Jewish pride. Reassure your child that they are loved and safe.

### What should I do if my child experienced antisemitism at school?
Listen, write down what happened, tell the school, check in with your child, and call the Rabbi at 925-384-7242 for support.

### At what age should I begin?
Begin when a child asks or encounters something, with a short, calm answer. Offer more detail as they grow.

### How do I give my child Jewish pride?
Celebrate Shabbat and holidays with joy, tell Jewish stories, and connect them to other Jewish children.

### Should I confront the person who said it?
Not in anger. The Rebbe's guidance, as described by Chabad.org, advises against confronting people by calling them antisemites. Ask the Rabbi how to handle a particular case.
