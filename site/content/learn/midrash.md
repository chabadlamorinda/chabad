Midrash (מִדְרָשׁ, from the root "to seek out" or "to explore") is the body of teachings by the Sages that explores the deeper meaning of the Torah's words. It comes in two main kinds: stories and lessons (Midrash Aggadah), and legal teachings (Midrash Halachah).

## The two kinds of midrash

Chabad.org explains the two types:

- **Midrash Aggadah** (מדרש אגדה) is homiletic: it extends the Torah's narratives or tells parables with a moral. "Aggadah" means "telling."
- **Midrash Halachah** (מדרש הלכה) draws further details of Jewish law from the words of the Torah.

The best-known collection is **Midrash Rabbah**. When people say "the Midrash" without saying more, they usually mean Midrash Rabbah, which has a volume for each of the Five Books of Moses and one for each of the five megillot, such as Esther and Ruth. Other collections exist too. The teachings were collected over many centuries, so ask the Rabbi where a particular story comes from.

## Famous examples

- **Abraham and the idols.** The Torah does not say how Abraham found G-d. The Midrash tells that as a young man he smashed his father's idols, leaving a stick in the hands of the biggest one. His father asked who did it, and Abraham said the big idol did, so Terach asked how an idol could do that.
- **Moses and the lost lamb.** The Midrash tells that Moses, as a shepherd, ran after a lamb that fled and carried it back, saying, "It is tired." G-d said that someone so caring was fit to shepherd Israel.
- **Nachshon at the sea.** The Midrash tells that when the Israelites stood at the Sea of Reeds, Nachshon walked into the water first, and then the sea split.

## How to read a midrash

A midrash is not a news report. The Sages knew how the Torah's text reads; they were drawing out the lessons hidden between the lines, often answering a question that the verse raises. A good way to approach it is: "What does this story teach me about G-d, people and my own life?" Whether a given detail is meant literally is a question for each teaching, so ask the Rabbi.

Many midrashim are quoted by [Rashi](/learn/rashi) in his commentary on the Torah, often to explain a puzzling word. That is why they appear in weekly Torah study, and it ties into the [Torah](/learn/torah) and [Talmud](/learn/talmud), which preserve the oral tradition.

## Common questions

### What is midrash?
Midrash is the Sages' teachings that explore the Torah's deeper meaning, through stories, lessons and legal analysis.

### What are the two types of midrash?
Midrash Aggadah, which has stories and moral lessons, and Midrash Halachah, which derives Jewish law from the Torah's text.

### What is Midrash Rabbah?
Midrash Rabbah is the best-known collection of midrash, with one volume for each of the Five Books of Moses and for each of the five megillot.

### Is midrash true?
Midrash teaches truth through story. The Sages did not mistake it for the Torah's plain text. Whether a given detail is meant literally depends on the teaching; ask the Rabbi.

### What is a famous example of midrash?
The story of Abraham smashing his father's idols, which is not written in the Torah but appears in the Midrash.
