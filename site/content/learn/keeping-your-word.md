"I'll call you tomorrow." "I'll be there at six." "I'll give something to that cause." We say these things easily, and we often forget them just as easily. The Torah asks us to take our words far more seriously, because in Judaism, a word is a real thing.

## Words create worlds

The world itself was created with speech: "And G-d said, let there be light." The sages count ten utterances with which the world was made. Human beings, created in G-d's image, share this power. Our words can build a friendship or break it, calm a child or wound them. Chassidic teaching explains that speech is the bridge between what is inside a person and the world outside.

## "He shall not profane his word"

The Torah says of a person who makes a vow or an oath: "He shall not profane his word; whatever comes out of his mouth he shall do" (Numbers 30:3). The word for "profane" is the same one used for treating something holy as if it were ordinary. A promise, in other words, is a bit of holiness. Breaking it does not just disappoint someone; it cheapens the gift of speech itself. The Talmud teaches that even where no formal vow was made, one's "yes" should be a true yes and one's "no" a true no.

## Why Jews say "bli neder"

Because a promise is so serious, Jewish law treats a spoken commitment to give charity or to do a mitzvah as a vow. So Jews learned to add two small words: *bli neder*, "without a vow." "I'll try to come Friday night, bli neder."

This is not a loophole. It is humility. We honestly intend to do the thing, but we admit that we do not control tomorrow. Bli neder says, "I mean it, and I am not G-d." It also protects us from carrying the weight of an unfulfilled vow, which is why the Kol Nidre prayer on Yom Kippur asks that vows we could not keep be released. See [Kol Nidre](/learn/kol-nidre).

## Say little, do much

Shammai taught: "Say little and do much" (Ethics of the Fathers 1:15). The sages point to Abraham, who offered his guests "a morsel of bread" and then ran to prepare a feast. In practice:

- **Do not promise in the heat of the moment.** "Let me check and get back to you" is an honest answer.
- **Be specific or be modest.** "I'll help you move on Sunday morning" is a promise you can keep; "I'm always here for you" is a feeling, not a plan.
- **Treat small promises like big ones**, especially to children, who learn from us whether words mean anything.

## When you cannot keep your word

Sometimes life truly gets in the way. Then Judaism asks for honesty rather than silence. Tell the person as early as you can, without excuses. Say plainly, "I said I would, and I did not. I'm sorry." Ask what would make it right, and do that. In business, backing out of a deal agreed in words is a serious breach of trust in Jewish law even without a signed contract. See [honesty and truth](/learn/honesty-and-truth) and [business ethics](/learn/business-ethics). A broken promise that is owned and repaired can deepen trust; one that is ignored quietly erodes it.

## Common questions

### Is a promise to myself binding?
A resolution like "I'll stop eating sweets" can take on the force of a vow in Jewish law if said as a firm commitment, so it is wise to add "bli neder." Better still, make the good habit without an oath. See [changing a bad habit](/learn/changing-a-bad-habit).

### I pledged a donation and now I cannot afford it. What do I do?
Speak to the Rabbi or the organization honestly and soon. Jewish law has ways to release a vow made in error or in changed circumstances, and no one is helped by silence. Rabbi Labkowski can guide you.

### Does saying "bli neder" mean I do not have to do it?
No. You still fully intend to do it, and you should. Bli neder only means that if circumstances truly prevent you, you have not violated a vow.
