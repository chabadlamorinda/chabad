Asarah B'Tevet (עֲשָׂרָה בְּטֵבֵת), the Tenth of Tevet, is a fast day that marks the start of the Babylonian siege of Jerusalem. It is kept from dawn until nightfall, and in recent times it has also become a day of Kaddish for Holocaust victims whose date of death is unknown.

For Jewish families in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda, it arrives about a week after Chanukah, a quiet turn from the lights of the menorah to a day of reflection. Find the date on [Jewish holidays](/jewish-holidays) and [this week](/this-week).

## What happened on the 10th of Tevet

According to Chabad.org, on the 10th of Tevet in 425 BCE the Babylonian army of Nebuchadnezzar began its siege of Jerusalem. It was the first step in a chain of events that ended with the destruction of the First Temple and exile. Chabad.org adds that the nearby dates of 8 and 9 Tevet are remembered for the translation of the Torah into Greek and the passing of Ezra the Scribe.

The Prophet Ezekiel, who lived in exile, was told by G-d to record this very day (Ezekiel 24:1-2), which is why Jews have marked it ever since. The fast is one of four fasts connected with the destruction of the Temple; see [minor fast days](/learn/minor-fast-days) and [fast days](/learn/fast-days).

## How the fast is kept

- **Dawn to nightfall.** No eating or drinking from the first light of the morning until nightfall. Check a Jewish calendar or [Shabbat times](/shabbat-times) for the hours.
- **Prayers.** Special Selichot (prayers of forgiveness) are added to the services, with extra readings from the Torah and Haftarah. Anyone can add Psalms.
- **Charity and reflection.** A custom is to give extra tzedakah and to reflect on repentance; see [teshuvah](/learn/teshuvah).
- **Ordinary life.** Unlike Yom Kippur and Tisha B'Av, you can wash, wear leather shoes and live normally.

## Who should not fast?

Anyone who is ill, elderly, pregnant or nursing, or a child, should not fast without asking the Rabbi. Health always comes first; call 925-384-7242 or write on [talk to the Rabbi](/help/talk-to-the-rabbi).

## When it falls on Friday

Asarah B'Tevet is the only fast day that can fall on a Friday. Chabad.org explains that, unlike other fasts, it is kept even then, although it makes Shabbat preparations harder. Plan the cooking and shopping ahead of time, finish the fast at nightfall, and bring in Shabbat on time. Candle lighting is in [Shabbat times](/shabbat-times). Some prayers (Tachanun and the afternoon Avinu Malkeinu) are omitted when it falls on Friday.

## A day of Kaddish for Holocaust victims

Chabad.org notes that in recent times the Tenth of Tevet has become the custom for saying Kaddish for Holocaust victims, many of whom have no known date of death. Families who do not know the date a loved one was murdered can light a yahrzeit candle and say Kaddish on this day. Read more in [Holocaust remembrance](/learn/holocaust-remembrance) and [Kaddish](/learn/kaddish), and speak to the Rabbi about how to remember.

## The Rebbe's message

The Rebbe taught that the purpose of a fast is not sadness but growth. The Prophet promises that these fast days will one day become "days of joy and gladness" (Zechariah 8:19). Each fast is a chance to return, to improve and to hasten that day. A fast that begins in mourning ends as a day of growth.

## Common questions

### What is Asarah B'Tevet?

It is a fast on the 10th of Tevet marking the start of the siege of Jerusalem by Nebuchadnezzar, and a day of repentance.

### When does the fast start and end?

It begins at dawn and ends at nightfall. Check the times on a Jewish calendar.

### Can the Tenth of Tevet fall on Friday?

Yes. It is the only fast that can. It is kept until nightfall, and Shabbat is prepared in advance.

### Who is exempt from fasting?

The ill, elderly, pregnant and nursing women, and children. Ask the Rabbi.

### What is the General Kaddish Day?

It is the Tenth of Tevet, when Jews say Kaddish for Holocaust victims whose date of death is unknown.

### Can I eat or drink if I feel unwell during the fast?

Health comes first. If you feel unwell, call the Rabbi at 925-384-7242.
