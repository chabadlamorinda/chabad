Public grills are generally not kosher, because they are used for non-kosher meat. The simplest way to grill kosher at a park or picnic is to bring your own grill, or your own grates, and cook kosher food on them.

## Why a public grill is a problem

Chabad.org teaches that food should not be placed directly on non-kosher surfaces, even if they are clean. Public grills are usually used for all kinds of meat, so the grates have absorbed non-kosher food. A public grill can in theory be koshered by heating the grates until they are red-hot or sparking, but this is difficult. Chabad.org says that bringing your own grill or grates is best.

The frame and the grill box usually do not need koshering. To be safe, cover the sides with foil, clean the grill box and burn the coals for several minutes before use.

## Grilling at home or at a picnic

For a park cookout in Lafayette, Moraga or Orinda, check the park's own rules about grills and fires before you go. The Jewish side is simple:

1. **Bring your own grates or grill**, or a disposable one.
2. **Cover the sides of any grill you use** with foil, clean the grill box and burn the coals for several minutes first.
3. **Use kosher meat** (see [kosher meat](/learn/kosher-meat)) and kosher products with a reliable symbol (see [kosher symbols](/learn/kosher-symbols)).

## Meat and fish on the same grill

Chabad.org says fish and meat may not be cooked together, or in the same oven at the same time, unless one is fully covered. The same pot can be used for both if they are cooked separately and the pot is thoroughly cleaned in between.

Grilling fish after meat is, in theory, allowed if the grill is thoroughly cleaned. In practice, it is best not to share a rack: double-wrap the fish in foil or use separate racks. If fish and meat are on separate racks at the same time, do not close the hood.

## Meat and dairy at the cookout

Keep meat and dairy apart as you would at home (see [meat and milk](/learn/meat-and-milk)): separate plates, utensils and serving spoons. If you have cheese or ice cream at your picnic, wait the right time after the meat meal.

## Washing hands and the blessings outdoors

Before eating bread, we wash our hands. According to Chabad.org, bring water and a cup, or use water from a fountain, river or non-salty lake. Water from a bathroom should be taken outside to wash. If you will be moving around, Birkat Hamazon should be said where you ate, and you may have in mind at Hamotzi that you will finish the meal elsewhere.

## Shabbat picnics

Chabad.org notes that on a Shabbat picnic in your own yard with an eruv (where carrying is allowed), make sure no liquid falls on the lawn or plants, and it is better not to serve drinks or soups outdoors on Shabbat. Questions about carrying and eruvs: ask the Rabbi.

## Common questions

### Can I use a public grill and keep kosher?
Public grills are generally not kosher, since they are used for non-kosher meat. Bring your own grill or grates. Do not put food directly on the public grates, even if they look clean.

### Can a public grill be koshered?
In theory, by heating the grates until red-hot or sparking, but that is hard. Chabad.org says bringing your own grill or grates is best.

### Can I grill fish and meat together?
Not together unless one is fully covered. Chabad.org says it is best not to share a rack, and to double-wrap fish in foil or use separate racks.

### Do I need to cover the sides of a public grill?
Parts that do not touch food usually do not need koshering. To be safe, cover the sides with foil, clean the grill box and burn the coals for several minutes first.

### Where should I wash my hands at a picnic?
Bring water and a cup, or use water from a fountain, river or non-salty lake. Ask the Rabbi if you have no water.

### Can I have a kosher barbecue on Shabbat?
Cooking on Shabbat is not permitted. Food is prepared before. See [Shabbat for beginners](/learn/shabbat-for-beginners) and ask the Rabbi about carrying and eruvs.
