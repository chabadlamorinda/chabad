Every parent wants a kind child. Judaism wants it too, and it does not leave it to chance. The Mishnah teaches that the world stands on three things: Torah, prayer and acts of kindness, and a Jewish home is a workshop where children practice all three. Kindness, in Jewish eyes, is not a trait some children are born with; it is a mitzvah, something we do, and it grows with practice.

## Kindness is taught by hands, not only by words

Children copy what they see. When a child watches a parent bring soup to a neighbor, call a lonely relative, or hold the door for a stranger, a lesson is learned that no speech could give. The Torah describes Abraham running to welcome three tired travelers, and the sages note that his son Isaac was right there, learning. Before you plan a program for raising a kind child, look at your own week: what acts of [chesed](/learn/chesed) does your child see you do?

## A pushka in the kitchen

The simplest Jewish habit of kindness is a tzedakah box, a [pushka](/learn/pushka), where a child can reach it. The amount hardly matters; the motion does. A child who drops a coin into a box every week learns in their body that what we have is meant to be shared. When the box is full, let your child help decide where it goes. See [children, money and tzedakah](/learn/children-money-and-tzedakah).

## Guests at the table

[Welcoming guests](/learn/hachnasat-orchim) is one of the great mitzvot, and a Shabbat table is the easiest place to practice it. Give children real jobs: setting an extra place, greeting people at the door, offering the challah to the guest first. A child who hears a guest thank the family learns that kindness feels wonderful.

## Visiting grandparents and the sick

Honoring grandparents and [visiting the sick](/learn/bikur-cholim) are mitzvot that children can do fully, not only in a child-sized way. A drawing for Grandma, a weekly video call with Grandpa, a visit to a classmate with a broken leg: each one teaches that other people's needs are real and that we can make a difference.

## Words that build

The sages taught that the tongue can give life or take it. A home where people speak gently, say thank you, and refuse to make fun of others raises children who do the same. Teach the Jewish habit of [judging others favorably](/learn/judging-others-favorably): when a friend was unkind, help your child find a kind explanation before a harsh one. See [speaking kindly about others](/learn/lashon-hara).

## Make kindness normal, not heroic

Notice and name small kindnesses: "You shared your snack; that was a mitzvah." Avoid turning every good deed into a prize, which teaches children to be kind for a reward. Chassidic teaching says that a mitzvah done out of habit grows into a mitzvah done from the heart; small, ordinary kindnesses become who the child is.

## When children are unkind

Every child has moments of selfishness, teasing or anger. Judaism does not expect perfect children; it expects [teshuvah](/learn/teshuvah), which means noticing, apologizing and trying again. Help your child make it right with the person they hurt, then move on without labels. A child who hears "that was unkind, and I know you are a kind person" learns both the standard and the hope. For ongoing struggles between siblings, see [sibling rivalry](/learn/sibling-rivalry).

## Common questions

### At what age can children start doing acts of kindness?
From the time they can hold a coin. Toddlers can put a coin in the pushka, hand a cookie to a guest and hug a grandparent. Older children can cook for a family in need, visit the sick and write cards. Match the mitzvah to the age, and let it grow.

### My child does not want to share or give. Is something wrong?
No. Sharing is hard for every child, and Judaism asks us to act kindly even before we feel kind. Keep the habits going calmly and without shaming; the feelings usually follow the actions.

### How can our family do kindness together?
Pick one regular project: a Friday delivery of challah to someone alone, a monthly visit to a senior residence, or a tzedakah project the children choose. Ask the Rabbi or Rebbetzin who in our community in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda could use a visit or a meal.
