Maot Chitim (מעות חיטים, "wheat money") is the Jewish custom of giving charity before Passover so that every person, rich or poor, can celebrate the holiday with matzah, wine and a festive meal. It is also called Kimcha Depischa (קמחא דפסחא, "Passover flour"), and Jewish tradition treats it as a special obligation, separate from ordinary giving all year.

## Why Passover has its own charity

The Haggadah opens with the words "All who are hungry, let them come and eat." But a family that cannot afford a Seder is not likely to knock on our door on the night itself. So Jewish tradition says the invitation must go out early: we send what they need to them, in time to prepare. Passover is the holiday of freedom, and no Jew should be left out of it for lack of food.

The custom began with matzah, which is why it is called "wheat" or "flour" money. Over time it grew to include food, wine, holiday clothes and other needs of the holiday.

## How old is this custom?

Chabad.org notes that the Jerusalem Talmud, compiled about 1,600 years ago, already speaks of communities collecting money for the local poor to buy matzah. When Jewish communities had organized leadership, residents were required to contribute, and a person who did business in two cities gave in both. In Jewish communities today, it is carried out by rabbis, Chabad centers and charities, and by families who simply decide to help.

## When and how to give

1. **Start 30 days before Passover.** That is when the collection traditionally begins, and the money should reach the families by the Shabbat before the holiday. This is the same period when we begin studying the laws of Passover.
2. **Give what you can.** There is no fixed amount. Each person gives according to his or her means.
3. **Give locally first.** Jewish law says the needy of your own community come first. That means helping people in Lafayette, Moraga, Orinda and the East Bay before sending far away.
4. **Don't wait for the perfect moment.** If the Shabbat before Passover has passed, give anyway, right up until the holiday begins.
5. **Involve your children.** Let them put the coins in the box, or help pack a bag of matzah and grape juice. See [tzedakah for children](/learn/children-money-and-tzedakah).

## A gift of dignity

Many people who need help at Passover are neighbors who look fine from the outside: a family after a job loss, an elderly person living alone, a student far from home. Giving through a trusted organization or the Rabbi lets help arrive quietly and with respect. The best form of giving, Jewish tradition teaches, is the kind where the receiver's dignity is protected. For more on how to choose where to give, see [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah) and [how to prioritize your giving](/learn/tzedakah-priorities).

## Beyond money: invite someone

Maot Chitim feeds the table. The Seder itself also needs guests. If you know someone who would be alone this Passover, invite them, sincerely and warmly, as described in [hosting your first Seder](/learn/hosting-your-first-seder). Even one extra chair is a mitzvah.

## Common questions

### What does Maot Chitim mean?
Maot Chitim means "wheat money" in Hebrew. It is charity collected before Passover to help the needy buy matzah and holiday food, and it is also called Kimcha Depischa, "Passover flour."

### When do you give Maot Chitim?
Collection traditionally begins 30 days before Passover, and the funds should be distributed by the Shabbat before the holiday. If you miss that time, you should still give until Passover begins.

### How much should I give for Maot Chitim?
There is no set amount. Each person gives according to his or her means. If you are unsure what is right for your family, ask the Rabbi.

### Who receives Maot Chitim?
Under Jewish law the local poor come first, so help your own community before others. Anyone who needs a Passover meal can receive it, whatever their background.

### Is Maot Chitim the same as regular tzedakah?
It is separate. It is a special communal obligation before Passover, over and above your usual giving, so it should not come out of your regular tzedakah budget.
