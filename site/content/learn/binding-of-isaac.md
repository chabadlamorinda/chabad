The Binding of Isaac, called the Akeidah (עֲקֵדָה, "binding"), is the Torah story in which G-d tests Abraham by asking him to offer his son Isaac, and then stops him at the last moment. Jews read it on the second day of Rosh Hashanah, and the ram's horn we blow, the shofar, recalls the ram that was offered in Isaac's place.

For families in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda, it is a story that raises real questions. This guide tells it simply and shares how Chabad teaching understands it.

## What happens in the story

The story is in Genesis, chapter 22. Abraham and Sarah had received Isaac miraculously, when Abraham was a hundred years old. Then G-d tells Abraham to take Isaac and offer him on a mountain.

Abraham rises early and sets out without delay. He binds Isaac on the altar. At the decisive moment a voice calls to stop: "Do not touch the child, and do nothing to him." Abraham sees a ram caught nearby and offers it instead. G-d says, "Now I know that you fear G-d."

Isaac lives. He and Abraham return home, and G-d renews his blessings to Abraham.

## What kind of test was this?

Judaism forbids human sacrifice, and the story ends with G-d stopping it. The test was never about harming a child. It was about whether Abraham's devotion to G-d would outweigh even his deepest hopes and instincts.

Chabad.org explains that Abraham had faced earlier tests, yet the Akeidah stands above them. The Talmud (Sanhedrin 89b) says G-d asked for this last test so that the earlier ones would not be seen as empty. Chassidic teaching adds that many people give their lives for something they care about, which is still a form of self-expression. Abraham was willing to set aside his *self*, including his own hopes for the future, because G-d asked. That is why the Akeidah is called the central image of Jewish devotion.

It is also why the Torah account is read in the daily morning prayers, so we recall it every day, not only on Rosh Hashanah.

## The ram and the shofar

The ram that took Isaac's place is the reason the shofar is made from a ram's horn. On Rosh Hashanah, when the world is judged, the sound of the shofar is a plea. If we have little merit of our own, we ask G-d to remember Abraham's devotion and the covenant that began with him. Read more in the guide to [the shofar](/learn/shofar) and the [Rosh Hashanah guide](/learn/rosh-hashanah-for-beginners).

## What the Akeidah asks of us

Most of us will never face a test like Abraham's. The story still invites a few quiet questions:

1. **What matters most to me?** Abraham showed that devotion to G-d can outweigh even our strongest instincts.
2. **Can I act with trust when I do not understand?** Abraham did not know where it would lead.
3. **Do my earlier efforts count?** The Talmud teaches that the final test gave the earlier ones meaning. A single good decision today can give meaning to years of effort.
4. **Can I begin again?** Rosh Hashanah is the day to renew our commitment. See [teshuvah](/learn/teshuvah).

## When the story troubles you

It is natural to find this story hard, especially as a parent. Jewish learning welcomes the question. The sages and Chassidic teachers have asked it for centuries, and it is fine to say the story is difficult. One thing every reading shares is that Isaac lives and the sacrifice is stopped.

If it troubles your child or you, please do not carry it alone. Speak with Rabbi Yisroel Labkowski or Rebbetzin Tzipora Labkowski. They would be glad to learn it with you.

## Common questions

### What is the Binding of Isaac?
It is the story in Genesis 22 in which G-d asks Abraham to offer his son Isaac and then stops him at the last moment. A ram is offered in Isaac's place. In Hebrew it is called the Akeidah.

### Why do Jews read the Akeidah on Rosh Hashanah?
Jews read it on the second day of Rosh Hashanah because the day is one of judgment, and Abraham's devotion is the merit we ask G-d to remember. The shofar blasts bring the story to mind.

### Did G-d really want Abraham to sacrifice Isaac?
Judaism does not allow human sacrifice, and G-d stops Abraham before any harm is done. The Torah calls it a test of Abraham's devotion. For deeper questions, ask the Rabbi.

### Why is the shofar a ram's horn?
The shofar recalls the ram that was offered in place of Isaac. Chabad.org explains that blowing it is a plea for G-d to remember Abraham's deed.

### How do I explain the Akeidah to children?
Keep to the heart of the story: Abraham loves G-d, G-d stops him, and Isaac is safe. Then talk about trust. If a child is frightened, ask the Rabbi for help.
