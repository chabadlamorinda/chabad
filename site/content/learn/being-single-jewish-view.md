Judaism loves marriage. It also loves you, exactly where you are today. If you are single, whether you have never married, are divorced or are widowed, this guide is not about what is missing. It is about the whole, rich Jewish life that is already yours, and about the hope Judaism holds for your future.

## You are not half a person

It is true that the Torah calls a husband and wife "one flesh," and Jewish teaching speaks of two halves of one soul. But a half-soul is not half a person. Every Jew carries a complete soul, a complete set of mitzvot and a complete relationship with G-d. Avraham was called "one" when he was alone in his faith. The Rebbe's approach was always to see each person as whole and precious now, and never as someone waiting to begin.

## A full Jewish life today

Nothing in Judaism is on hold until you marry.

- **Shabbat is yours.** Light candles, make Kiddush over wine or grape juice, and sit down to a real meal. If cooking for one feels flat, invite a friend or come to Chabad: there is a chair for you. See [Shabbat when you live alone](/learn/shabbat-alone) and [Shabbat times](/shabbat-times).
- **Community is yours.** Prayer, classes, holiday meals, volunteering. Communities are built on people who have time to learn and to help.
- **Mitzvot are yours.** Tefillin, tzedakah, Torah study, visiting the sick, kindness to parents. None of them wait for a wedding.
- **A Jewish home is yours.** A mezuzah on your door, Jewish books on your shelf, a pushka in your kitchen. Your apartment is already a Jewish home. See [setting up a Jewish home](/learn/setting-up-a-jewish-home).

## Holding hope without holding your breath

Judaism teaches that matches are made in Heaven. The Talmud says that forty days before a child is formed, a heavenly voice announces who they will marry. That means the search is real, but it is not a test you can fail. Your job is to do your part, with effort and with prayer, and to trust that G-d is working on the rest. See [trust in G-d](/learn/bitachon).

Prayer for a match is a Jewish tradition thousands of years old. Many people add a few words of their own to the daily Amidah, say a chapter of [Tehillim](/tehillim), or give tzedakah in the merit of finding their match. Praying for a friend to find their match is considered especially powerful. If you would like a blessing from the Rabbi, simply ask.

## When it hurts

There are evenings when being single is lonely, and holidays when every question from a relative lands hard. Judaism does not ask you to pretend. Tell G-d honestly how you feel; King David did. Let the community be a family. If sadness or anxiety is weighing on you for a long time, speak with a therapist as well as with the Rabbi or Rebbetzin; Judaism considers caring for your mind a mitzvah. See [feeling lonely](/learn/feeling-lonely) and [mental health, the Jewish view](/learn/mental-health-jewish-view).

## Common questions

### Can a single woman light Shabbat candles?
Yes. Every Jewish woman and girl lights Shabbat candles, married or not. A single woman lights one candle (some light two); ask the Rabbi about your family's custom. See [Shabbat candles](/learn/shabbat-candles).

### Is it a problem to come to synagogue or Shabbat dinner alone?
Not at all. Many people come alone, and at Chabad you will be seated and introduced. Coming alone is often how friendships begin.

### I am divorced or widowed. Does Judaism expect me to remarry?
Judaism holds marriage as a blessing at any age and warmly supports those who seek it again, but it never pressures. Take the time you need; the Rabbi and Rebbetzin are here to listen whenever you are ready.

### How do I answer relatives who keep asking when I will marry?
With a smile and a short answer: "G-d willing, soon; please keep me in your prayers." Then change the subject. You owe no one an explanation for G-d's timing.
