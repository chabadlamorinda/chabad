At most bar mitzvah celebrations, the bar mitzvah boy stands up and shares a short Torah thought, called a d'var Torah or drasha. For many boys it is the first time they teach Torah in public. It does not need to be long or complicated. It needs to be his.

## A simple structure

A good speech has four parts:

1. **Opening.** Welcome everyone and say what a special day it is.
2. **The Torah idea.** Share one thought from the Torah, usually from the portion of the week of the bar mitzvah.
3. **The lesson.** Explain what the idea means for him, now that he is 13 and responsible for the mitzvot.
4. **Thank you.** Thank G-d, his parents, family, teachers and guests.

Five to ten minutes is plenty. One clear idea is better than three rushed ones.

## Connecting to the Torah portion

Find the [weekly parsha](/learn/weekly-parsha) for the week of his Hebrew birthday or the Shabbat he is called to the Torah. Read a summary together and look for one story, word or mitzvah that speaks to him. Then ask:

- What is happening here?
- What question would a curious reader ask?
- What answer do the commentaries give?
- What does it teach me about being a Jewish adult?

## Adding a Chassidic thought

A Chassidic insight gives the speech depth. Chassidus often looks at the inner meaning of a verse: how a story in the Torah is also happening inside each of us. A favorite theme for a bar mitzvah is [tefillin](/learn/tefillin), which binds the mind and the heart to G-d. The Rabbi can suggest an idea from the Rebbe's talks on his portion.

## The Chabad custom: the maamar

In Chabad communities, the bar mitzvah boy traditionally recites a maamar, a Chassidic discourse, about the mitzvah of tefillin, which begins with the words "Lehavin inyan tefillin." Some boys learn the whole maamar by heart, others say part of it or read it, often in addition to a short talk in English. It is a beautiful custom, and it is fine to choose a simpler talk instead. Ask the Rabbi what suits your son.

## Thanking parents and family

The thank-you section is often the part people remember most. Encourage your son to be specific and sincere: a moment he remembers, something a parent taught him, a grandparent who inspired him. Honoring parents is itself a mitzvah. See [Honoring Parents](/learn/honoring-parents).

## Tips for delivering it

- **Practice out loud** several times, in front of family.
- **Print it in large type** with space between lines.
- **Speak slowly** and look up at the audience now and then.
- **It is fine to be nervous.** Everyone in the room is on his side.

## Common questions

### Does the speech have to be about the Torah portion?
No. The portion is the most common choice, but a speech about tefillin, a mitzvah he is taking on, or the upcoming holiday is also wonderful.

### Should my son write it himself?
Yes, as much as possible. Parents and the Rabbi can guide and edit, but the speech is most meaningful when the ideas and words are his own.

### When should we start working on it?
A few months before the bar mitzvah is a good time. This leaves room for a draft, a rewrite and plenty of practice.
