Lashon hara means "evil speech": saying something negative about another person, even when it is true. The Torah forbids it, along with gossip and slander, because words have the power to hurt people, damage relationships and divide communities.

## What is lashon hara?

The prohibition comes from the verse "Do not go around as a gossiper among your people" (Leviticus 19:16). Jewish law describes several related kinds of harmful speech:

- **Lashon hara:** saying something true but negative or damaging about someone.
- **Motzi shem ra:** spreading lies about someone, which is even worse.
- **Rechilut:** gossip that stirs up bad feelings, such as telling someone, "Do you know what she said about you?"
- **Avak lashon hara,** "the dust of lashon hara": hints, tone, or comments that suggest something negative without saying it outright.

Listening to and believing lashon hara is also forbidden.

## Why it is so serious

The Talmud says lashon hara harms three people: the one who speaks it, the one who hears it, and the one it is about. The Sages compare words to arrows: once released, they cannot be called back.

The Torah's story of Miriam, who spoke about her brother Moses and was struck with tzara'at, a skin affliction, shows how seriously the Torah treats it (Numbers 12). The Talmud also teaches that the Second Temple was destroyed because of baseless hatred, which is often fed by negative speech.

The great modern teacher of these laws, Rabbi Yisrael Meir Kagan, wrote a whole book on the topic, called Chafetz Chaim, "One who desires life," from the verse "Who is the person who desires life... Guard your tongue from evil" (Psalms 34:13 to 14).

## When it is permitted to share information

Sometimes speaking up is necessary, and even a mitzvah. You may share negative information when it is needed to protect someone from harm, such as:

- Warning someone about a dishonest business partner.
- Answering a sincere question about a potential employee or marriage partner.
- Reporting abuse or danger.

Even then, conditions apply: the information should be accurate, shared only with those who need it, without exaggeration, and with the goal of helping, not hurting. Ask the Rabbi about a specific case.

## How to guard your tongue, step by step

1. **Pause before speaking** about anyone who is not present.
2. **Ask: is it true, is it necessary, is it kind?**
3. **Change the subject** when a conversation turns to gossip.
4. **Judge favorably.** Assume the best about others, as the Mishnah teaches.
5. **Praise instead.** Speak about the good you see in others.
6. **Start small.** Many people take on one hour a day of careful speech, and grow from there.

## Speech in the Chassidic view

Chassidic teaching explains that speech reveals the soul. Just as speaking badly causes harm, speaking positively about another Jew brings out their good, both in our eyes and in reality. The Baal Shem Tov taught that seeing faults in another can be a mirror for working on ourselves.

## Common questions

### Is it lashon hara if it is true?
Yes. That is exactly what lashon hara is. Saying something false is a separate, more serious sin.

### Can I talk to a therapist or friend for advice about someone?
Seeking advice or emotional support is generally permitted when it is truly needed for help and not to spread gossip. Share only what is necessary.

### Is it lashon hara to speak about public figures?
Factual discussion of public actions can be appropriate. Mocking or spreading negative gossip about anyone, public or private, is not.

### What should I do if I spoke lashon hara?
Do [teshuvah](/learn/teshuvah): regret it, resolve to change, and if the person found out or was harmed, ask their forgiveness. Then try to repair any damage.
