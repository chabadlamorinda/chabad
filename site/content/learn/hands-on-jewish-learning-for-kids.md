Children learn Judaism best by doing. When a child braids challah, builds a sukkah model, sings a blessing, acts out a Torah story or plays a Hebrew letter game, they use their hands, eyes, ears and voice together, and the lesson becomes a memory. That is why Jewish learning for children should be fun, exciting and hands-on.

## "The main thing is the doing"

The sages taught, "Not study but deed is the main thing" (Ethics of the Fathers 1:17). Judaism is a life of mitzvahs, things we do: lighting candles, giving tzedakah, welcoming guests, saying blessings. So it makes sense that children learn it best by doing it.

Jewish tradition has always taught children this way. On Passover, the Seder is a hands-on lesson full of tastes, questions and songs, designed so that children will ask. On Sukkot children help build and decorate the sukkah. On Chanukah they light the menorah and spin the dreidel. The holidays themselves are a hands-on curriculum.

## Why hands-on works

- **More senses, stronger memories.** A child who smells fresh challah and says the blessing remembers it far longer than one who only reads about it.
- **Every kind of learner.** Some children learn by listening, some by seeing, some by moving. Multisensory lessons reach them all.
- **Joy.** Children want to come back to what they enjoy, and joy is a Jewish value in itself.
- **Confidence.** A child who has done a mitzvah can do it again, at home, with family and friends.

## Ideas to try at home

**Shabbat**
- Bake or braid challah together on Friday, even from frozen dough.
- Let your child set the Shabbat table and put a coin in the [pushka](/learn/pushka) before candle lighting.

**Hebrew letters**
- Shape letters from play dough, pipe cleaners or pretzels.
- Hide letter cards around the house and have a letter hunt. See [teaching kids the Aleph-Bet](/learn/teaching-kids-the-aleph-bet).

**The weekly Torah portion**
- Act out the story with stuffed animals or costumes.
- Draw the week's story and hang it on the fridge. See [parsha for kids](/learn/parsha-for-kids).

**The holidays**
- Make a Chanukah craft, a Purim costume or a Passover Seder plate. See [Chanukah crafts for kids](/learn/chanukah-crafts-for-kids).
- Let your child be the one who explains the holiday to the family.

**Mitzvahs and kindness**
- Bake cookies for a neighbor or someone who is sick.
- Pick a tzedakah cause together and fill a pushka. See [children, money and tzedakah](/learn/children-money-and-tzedakah).

## Common questions

### What does hands-on Jewish learning mean?
Learning by doing: baking, building, singing, playing games, acting out stories and doing mitzvahs, so that children use all their senses and remember what they learn.

### Is hands-on learning serious enough?
Yes. Jewish tradition has always taught children through doing, from the Passover Seder to the sukkah. Children who enjoy learning learn more.

### I'm not crafty. What can I do at home?
Keep it simple: a blessing before a snack, a coin in the pushka, a question about the parsha at dinner. Doing it together is what counts.

### What age is best for hands-on learning?
Every age. Toddlers love songs and touching things; older children love games, challenges and projects.
