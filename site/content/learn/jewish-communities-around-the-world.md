Jews live in communities all over the world because, after the Temple was destroyed, the Jewish people was scattered to many lands, and wherever Jews settled they carried the same Torah with them. Jews of Ashkenazi, Sephardic and Mizrahi backgrounds have different customs and foods but share one faith, one people and one story.

## How the Jewish people spread

Chabad.org tells that after the destruction of the Second Temple, Jews were dispersed around the world. The first great communities took root in Babylon. Communities also formed in Spain and North Africa. Over centuries of persecution and expulsion, many Jews moved gradually eastward through France and Germany to Poland and Russia. Jews settled where they were allowed to live, and even in hard times, magnificent Jewish communities were built. For the longer story, see [Jewish History: An Overview](/learn/jewish-history-overview).

## Ashkenazim and Sephardim

Jewish communities are often described by the lands where they developed (see [Ashkenazi and Sephardi Jews](/learn/ashkenazi-and-sephardi)).

- **Ashkenazim** developed their culture in Europe. Many Ashkenazim follow the rulings of Rabbi Moshe Isserles on the Shulchan Aruch. Foods such as chicken soup with kneidlach, which many Americans call "Jewish food," come from Ashkenazi kitchens.
- **Sephardim** come from Sepharad, the ancient Hebrew name for Spain. Chabad.org notes that in 1492, hundreds of thousands left Spain for Morocco, Portugal, Turkey and other places. Many Sephardim follow the rulings of Rabbi Yosef Caro and have their own Mediterranean-style cuisine. Ladino, a Judeo-Spanish language written in Hebrew letters, was spoken in many of these communities (see [Jewish Languages](/learn/jewish-languages)).
- **Mizrahim or Eidot HaMizrach**, "Communities of the East," is a more accurate term, according to Chabad.org, for Jews of Eastern origin. Yemenite Jews, whose tradition is older and did not come through Spain, and Persian Jews, who trace their roots to the Babylonian exile, are not Sephardic.

Chabad.org notes that roughly half of Israel's Jews are Sephardim, and that the first Jews in what became the United States and Canada were Sephardic.

## Jews in far-off places

Wherever there are Jews, even a few, there is a Jewish story. Chabad.org tells of Jewish communities in places such as Perth in Western Australia, Manaus in the Amazon, St. John's in Newfoundland and Kaua'i in Hawaii, where a Chabad house serves as a hub of Jewish life. Chabad.org also reports that more than 6,500 Chabad couples now serve Jewish communities in every U.S. state and in more than 100 countries (see [Chabad](/learn/chabad)).

## Reuniting after hard times

Sephardic communities in Greece, Italy, the Balkans and Romania were nearly destroyed in the Holocaust. Jewish life in the Soviet Union was repressed for decades, and Jews slowly rebuilt it afterward (see [Soviet Jewry](/learn/soviet-jewry-history)). For many families in the Bay Area, that is part of their own story.

## Common questions

### Why do Jews live all over the world?
After the Second Temple was destroyed, Jews were dispersed. They settled where they could live, from Babylon to Spain, North Africa, Poland and Russia, and later the Americas.

### What is the difference between Ashkenazi and Sephardic Jews?
Ashkenazim developed in Europe, and Sephardim trace their origins to Spain. Their customs, foods and some rulings differ, but the Torah is the same.

### Are Mizrahi Jews the same as Sephardic Jews?
Often they are called by the same name, but Chabad.org says "Communities of the East" is more accurate. Yemenite and Persian Jews, for example, are not Sephardic.

### What language did Jews speak around the world?
Jews spoke the languages of their lands, as well as Hebrew for prayer. Ladino and Yiddish are well-known examples (see [Jewish Languages](/learn/jewish-languages)).

### Does Chabad serve Jews in every country?
Chabad.org says that Chabad couples serve every U.S. state and more than 100 countries.
