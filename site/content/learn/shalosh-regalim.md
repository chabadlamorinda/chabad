The three pilgrimage festivals, called Shalosh Regalim (שָׁלֹשׁ רְגָלִים), are Passover, Shavuot and Sukkot, the three holidays on which the Torah commands Jews to travel to Jerusalem and rejoice at the Holy Temple. They celebrate freedom, the giving of the Torah and the ingathering of the harvest, and today each one is still marked with special prayers and joy.

For families in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda, they are the Jewish year's great anchors. Here is how they fit together.

## What does "regalim" mean?

*Regel* (רֶגֶל) means "foot" or "leg," and *regalim* is its plural. The Torah (Exodus 23:14) says, "Three times a year you shall celebrate for Me." The name stuck because most Jews walked to Jerusalem on these days.

Chabad.org explains the commandment this way: we travel to Jerusalem three times a year, we rejoice at the Temple, and we do not come empty-handed. Those who came brought an offering. Read about [the Holy Temple](/learn/the-holy-temple).

## The three festivals in one map

Chabad.org points out that each festival lines up with both the farming year and a spiritual story:

- **Passover (Pesach), spring.** The wheat is ripening but not yet ready. It celebrates the Exodus from Egypt and the freedom that began a journey. See [Passover for beginners](/learn/passover-for-beginners).
- **Shavuot, the grain harvest.** It marks the giving of the Torah at Mount Sinai. The Torah is received, and now we must absorb it. See [Shavuot for beginners](/learn/shavuot-for-beginners).
- **Sukkot, the ingathering.** The harvest is stored and enjoyed. Sukkot comes just after Yom Kippur, when we have been forgiven. We live in a sukkah and take the four species. See [Sukkot for beginners](/learn/sukkot-for-beginners).

Freedom, Torah, joy: first we leave Egypt, then we receive the Torah, then we bring it home and rejoice.

## Nature and miracle together

Chabad.org teaches that the natural world and miracles are not opposites. Nature itself expresses G-d's creative power. According to Kabbalah, the physical reflects the spiritual, so each festival celebrates both a material harvest and a spiritual one. That is why the holidays are tied to the seasons of the land.

## What was it like to go up to Jerusalem?

In the days of the Temple, Jews from across the land streamed to Jerusalem. At Passover they brought the Passover lamb. On Sukkot there were nightly celebrations in the Temple courtyard. Pilgrims shared meals with family, guests and the needy.

The Temple is gone, but the festivals continue. We observe them through the Seder, learning, the sukkah and the festive meals, and through prayer.

## Joy, Hallel and Yizkor today

- **Joy.** The festivals are meant for rejoicing: festive clothes, good food, singing and time with family and guests.
- **Hallel.** On festival mornings we sing Hallel (הַלֵּל), psalms of praise. In Sukkot it is recited every day.
- **Yizkor.** A memorial prayer is recited on Yom Kippur and on the last days of the festivals. Many people pledge charity in memory of loved ones. See the guide to [Yizkor](/learn/yizkor).
- **Yom Tov rules.** On the festival days we avoid work as on Shabbat, with exceptions such as cooking for the festival. Ask the Rabbi about details.

## Common questions

### What are the three pilgrimage festivals?
They are Passover, Shavuot and Sukkot. The Torah commands Jews to travel to Jerusalem on these three holidays.

### Why are they called Shalosh Regalim?
*Regalim* comes from *regel*, "foot." Jews traveled on foot to Jerusalem three times a year.

### What does each pilgrimage festival celebrate?
Passover celebrates the Exodus, Shavuot the giving of the Torah and Sukkot the ingathering of the harvest and G-d's protection. Each also matches a season in the land of Israel.

### Do Jews still go to Jerusalem on these festivals?
There is no Temple today, so the obligation of the offering does not apply. Many Jews do visit Jerusalem for the festivals. In every community the festivals are marked by prayer, meals and joy.

### Is Yizkor said on all three festivals?
Yizkor is said on the last day of Passover, on Shavuot and on Shemini Atzeret after Sukkot, and also on Yom Kippur. Ask the Rabbi about your community's custom.
