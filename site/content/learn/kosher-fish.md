A fish is kosher if it has both fins and scales, so salmon, cod, tuna, trout and herring are kosher, while shrimp, lobster, crab, shark and catfish are not. Fish needs no special slaughter or salting, which makes it the simplest kosher food to begin with.

## Which fish are kosher? The fins-and-scales rule

The Torah gives one test for sea creatures: "everything in the water that has fins and scales you may eat" (Leviticus 11:9). Both signs must be present. Fins are easy to see. Scales are the part that needs care, because not everything people call a "scale" counts in Jewish law. A kosher scale can be removed from the skin without tearing it.

Fish that are commonly eaten and kosher include salmon, cod, flounder, haddock, halibut, herring, mackerel, pike, trout and whitefish. Tuna, sea bass and tilapia are also widely accepted as kosher, but if you are unsure about a species, ask the Rabbi.

## Is shrimp kosher? What is not kosher

No. Shrimp, lobster, crab, clams, oysters, mussels, octopus and squid are shellfish or sea creatures without fins and scales, so they are not kosher. Shark, swordfish, eel and skate are also not kosher. Sturgeon and catfish, which people often ask about, are not kosher either.

The rule applies to anything made from these animals, too. Fish oil, gelatin and some sauces can come from non-kosher fish, which is why labels matter (see [kosher symbols](/learn/kosher-symbols)).

## Buying fish: how to be sure

1. **Buy the fish whole when you can.** You can see the fins and scales yourself.
2. **If it is a fillet, look for a skin patch.** Fish sold skinned cannot be identified, so a piece of skin with scales should be left on, or the fish should come from a trusted kosher source.
3. **Do not buy cut or ground fish at a counter that also sells non-kosher fish.** The knife, board and grinder may have touched shellfish or non-kosher fish. Buy only where kosher fish is handled separately, or with a kosher certification.
4. **Check the label on anything packaged.** Canned, smoked and prepared fish need reliable kosher certification. Smoked fish should be certified as smoked only with other kosher fish. Read more about [kosher certification](/learn/kosher-certification).

## Sushi and eating fish out

Raw kosher fish is permitted, so sushi can be kosher. The concerns are the kitchen and the ingredients: the knives and boards, imitation crab, eel sauce, tempura and sauces. Without kosher supervision it is hard to be sure, so in a restaurant look for [kosher certification](/learn/eating-out-kosher) and ask the Rabbi about your own situation.

## Fish with meat

Fish is pareve, neither meat nor dairy, so it can be eaten with dairy (though some have a custom not to). Jewish tradition, however, forbids eating fish and meat together, because of a health concern raised by the Sages. At a Shabbat meal, serve fish as its own course, then rinse your mouth or eat something plain before the meat. Many families also use separate plates for the fish course. Learn more in [what pareve means](/learn/pareve) and [meat and milk](/learn/meat-and-milk). The details vary by custom, so ask the Rabbi about yours.

## Common questions

### Which fish are kosher?
Any fish with both fins and scales is kosher, including salmon, cod, tuna, trout, herring and halibut. If you are unsure about a species, ask the Rabbi.

### Is shrimp kosher?
No. Shrimp, lobster, crab, clams and oysters are not kosher, because they do not have fins and scales.

### Is salmon kosher?
Yes. Salmon has fins and scales and is one of the most popular kosher fish. Buy it whole, or from a kosher source if it is cut.

### Is sushi kosher?
It can be. Raw fish with fins and scales is allowed, but the knives, the sauces and imitation crab can be a problem, so look for kosher supervision.

### Can I eat fish and meat together?
Not in the same dish. Jewish tradition keeps fish and meat apart, so serve fish as a separate course and rinse your mouth in between.
