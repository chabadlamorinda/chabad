In almost every Jewish community there is a quiet institution with no sign on the door. Someone keeps a fund and lends money to neighbors who need it, with no interest and no shame. Someone else keeps a closet of wedding gowns, a shelf of folding tables, a drawer of baby carriers, all lent for free. This is a **gemach**, one of the most beautiful ideas Judaism gave the world.

## What the word means

*Gemach* is short for *gemilut chasadim*, "acts of loving-kindness," one of the three pillars the world stands on (Ethics of the Fathers 1:2). The sages explain that kindness is greater than tzedakah in one way: tzedakah is given with money, to the poor, while kindness can be done with money or with our own hands, for the poor and the rich alike. A gemach is kindness made into a system, so that help is ready before anyone has to ask twice.

## Lending without interest is a mitzvah

The Torah says: "When you lend money to My people, to the poor person with you, do not act toward him as a creditor; do not charge him interest" (Exodus 22:24). The sages read the opening word, usually translated "if," as "when": lending to someone in need is not optional, it is a commandment. The ban on interest between Jews ([ribbit](/learn/ribbit)) is the other half of the same idea: a loan is an act of kindness, not a business.

Why a loan and not a gift? Dignity. A person who borrows and repays stays standing on their own feet. Maimonides lists eight levels of tzedakah, and at the very top he places helping someone become self-supporting, through a gift, a loan, a partnership or a job, "so that he will not need to ask." A gemach lives on that highest rung every day. See [tzedakah priorities](/learn/tzedakah-priorities).

## Kinds of gemachs

- **Money.** A free-loan fund lends sums for rent, a car repair, tuition or a business getting started. The borrower signs a simple note, often with a guarantor, and repays in installments. The same dollars are lent again and again, so one donation helps dozens of families over the years.
- **Simcha items.** Wedding gowns, centerpieces, chuppah poles, folding chairs and tables, large pots and urns: everything that makes a celebration beautiful but is used once. A simcha gemach lets a family in a tight year make a wedding or Bar Mitzvah with joy.
- **Clothing and baby gear.** Maternity clothes, children's coats, strollers, cribs, Shabbat clothes for a child who outgrew last year's.

Chassidic teaching adds a warm thought: when you lend an object, part of your home travels into another home. Every gemach quietly ties a community together.

## How to start one

1. **Pick one need you keep seeing.** The best gemachs begin small: one closet, one fund, one kind of item.
2. **Find a few partners.** Two or three families who will give the seed money or items and share the work.
3. **Set simple, written rules.** For money: maximum loan, repayment schedule, a guarantor, no interest ever, strict privacy. For items: how long, who cleans, how to return.
4. **Guard dignity above all.** No lists on the wall, no questions beyond what is needed, a quiet way to ask. The Talmud praises the one who gives before being asked.
5. **Speak with the Rabbi.** Loans between Jews have specific laws, and a money gemach should be set up correctly from the start. Rabbi Labkowski can guide you and connect you with others in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda who want to help.

## Common questions

### Is it really forbidden to charge even a little interest?
Between Jews, yes, in almost every form, and the borrower may not pay it either. Business investments use a halachic partnership agreement called a *heter iska*; see [ribbit](/learn/ribbit) and ask the Rabbi before signing anything.

### What if the borrower cannot repay?
A gemach does not pursue people the way a bank does. Many funds quietly extend the schedule or turn the loan into a gift. This is why gemachs often ask for a guarantor, so the fund survives to help the next family.

### Is giving to a gemach considered tzedakah?
Yes. Money given to a free-loan fund counts toward [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah) and maaser, and because it is lent over and over, your gift keeps giving for years.
