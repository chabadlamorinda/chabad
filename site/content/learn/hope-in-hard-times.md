Hard times come to everyone: an illness, a job lost, a marriage in trouble, a loss that does not heal. Judaism does not pretend these do not hurt. It does teach that the story is not over.

## A people built on hope

The national anthem of Israel is called *Hatikvah*, "The Hope." That is no accident. After every darkness, Jews lit candles again, built schools again, had children again. Our ancestors in Egypt, our grandparents after the Holocaust, the Jews of the Soviet Union who kept Judaism alive in secret: none of them could see how the story would turn, and they held on. Their strength is in you.

## Hope is not wishful thinking

The Hebrew word for hope, *tikvah*, is related to the word for a cord: something you hold on to that pulls you forward. Jewish hope is not a prediction that everything will turn out as we want; it is a decision to stay attached to G-d and to the future, even when we cannot see it. "Hope to G-d; be strong and let your heart take courage, and hope to G-d" (Psalm 27:14). Hope is a kind of courage.

## Trust: "Think good and it will be good"

Chassidic teaching adds something bold. *Bitachon*, trust, means more than believing G-d is in charge. It means trusting that He is good, and that He is good to me, now. The third Chabad Rebbe, the Tzemach Tzedek, famously told a worried Chassid: "Think good, and it will be good." Chabad understands this to mean that trust itself opens channels for blessing.

This is not magic, and it is not a demand to fake cheerfulness. It is a practice: each time the fearful thought comes, answer it with a thought of trust. "G-d is with me in this. He has carried me before." Over time the trusting thought gets stronger than the fearful one. See [trust in G-d](/learn/bitachon).

## Finding meaning in the dark

The Talmud tells of Nachum Ish Gamzu, who met every setback with the words *gam zu l'tovah*, "this too is for the good." He did not know how; he trusted that it was. Chassidic teaching explains that some good is visible and some is hidden. That does not make pain less painful; it does mean the pain is not meaningless. See [why bad things happen](/learn/why-bad-things-happen).

## Small steps, every day

The Tanya teaches that "a little light dispels much darkness." You do not have to fix everything. You have to light one candle today.

- **Say Modeh Ani** when you wake: one sentence of gratitude before the worries begin.
- **Say a Psalm.** Jews in trouble have turned to [Tehillim](/learn/tehillim) for three thousand years; Psalms 23, 27 and 121 are good places to start.
- **Do one kindness.** Giving when you feel empty reminds the soul that it is strong.
- **Keep Shabbat's rhythm.** Friday candles put a floor under a hard week.
- **Talk to G-d in your own words.** Tell Him exactly how hard it is.
- **Move your body, see the sky, sleep.** Caring for the body is a mitzvah.

## You are not meant to do this alone

Judaism is a team sport. Come to services even if you cannot pray; sit at a Shabbat table even if you cannot smile. Tell Rabbi Labkowski or Rebbetzin Labkowski what is happening; that is what they are here for. See [feeling lonely](/learn/feeling-lonely).

## When to ask for help

Hope and trust in G-d go hand in hand with getting real help. If you cannot sleep or eat, if the sadness does not lift for weeks, or if you have thoughts of harming yourself, speak to a doctor or therapist right away and tell someone close to you today. Judaism commands us to guard our health; seeing a professional is part of trusting G-d, not a failure of it. If you are in immediate danger, call or text 988 in the United States. See [mental health and Judaism](/learn/mental-health-jewish-view) and [getting through grief and hard times](/help/grief-and-hard-times).

## Common questions

### Is it a lack of faith to feel afraid or sad?
No. King David poured out fear and grief in the Psalms. Faith is not the absence of hard feelings; it is where you turn with them.

### What if I prayed and things did not get better?
No prayer is wasted, and "not yet" is not "no." Keep praying, keep taking the steps in front of you, and lean on people. See [why pray](/learn/why-pray).
