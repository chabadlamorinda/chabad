Too much time on the phone, a short temper, gossip, junk food, putting things off. Almost everyone has a habit they would love to change. Judaism is full of practical wisdom about change, because it believes every person can change.

## You are not your habits

The Torah teaches that every person has free choice. The Tanya, the classic book of Chabad teaching, explains that even when we feel a strong pull, our mind can lead our heart. We may not control every feeling, but we can choose what we think about, what we say and what we do. Those three "garments of the soul" are where change happens. See [free will](/learn/free-will).

## Start small

The sages say, "One good deed brings another" (Ethics of the Fathers 4:2). Maimonides teaches that a good trait is built by repetition: giving a single coin a thousand times shapes a generous heart more than giving a thousand coins once. Choose one small, clear step you can keep every day, rather than a big promise that will not last.

## Go a little the other way

Maimonides advises someone who leans too far toward a trait to practice the opposite for a while, until they come back to the healthy middle. Someone quick to anger might practice staying silent in tense moments; someone who spends too freely might wait a day before every purchase.

## Fill the space with light

The Rebbe often taught that you do not fight darkness with a stick; you light a candle. Instead of only fighting a bad habit, add something good in its place: a few minutes of Torah study instead of scrolling, a blessing before eating, a walk with a friend. A little light pushes away a lot of darkness.

## A fresh start every day

Every morning we say Modeh Ani and thank G-d for returning our soul: a new day, a new chance. If you slip, do not give up. *Teshuvah*, returning, means regretting the past, stopping, and deciding for the future, and it is always possible. See [teshuvah](/learn/teshuvah).

## Practical steps

- **Name it.** Write down the one habit you want to change and why it matters to you.
- **Change the setting.** Leave the phone outside the bedroom; do not keep the cookies in the house.
- **Pick a time.** Habits stick to moments: after Modeh Ani, before dinner, on the way home.
- **Tell someone.** A friend, your spouse or the Rabbi can encourage you.
- **Count the days.** Like counting the Omer, counting gives each day a meaning.
- **Ask for help from Above.** Say a few words of prayer in your own language.

## Common questions

### What if I keep failing?
Every attempt counts. The Tanya teaches that the struggle itself is precious to G-d. Get up, start again, and make the step smaller if you need to.

### Is there a Jewish time for making resolutions?
Every day is a fresh start, and Rosh Hashanah, your Hebrew birthday and the start of a new month are especially good times. See [making every day count](/learn/making-every-day-count).

### When is a habit a sign of something more serious?
If a habit is harming your health, your family or your work, or you cannot stop, please reach out for professional help as well. Judaism sees seeking help as a mitzvah. See [addiction and recovery](/learn/addiction-and-recovery), and you can always talk privately with the Rabbi or the Rebbetzin.
