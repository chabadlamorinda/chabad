Tanya's answer to how a decent person can honestly feel humbler than anyone else is in Chapter 30: do not judge your fellow until you stand in his place, and measure your own effort honestly against the struggle he faces. Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi teaches humility as an honest comparison that breaks pride, not as a pretense of being worthless.

For the general Jewish view, see [humility](/learn/humility). Here we follow Tanya's own argument.

## The puzzle

The Mishnah says to be "lowly of spirit before every man." Chabad.org's presentation of Chapter 30 notes that Hebrew has two words. *Anavah* is knowing your strengths without boasting, because they are gifts. *Shefalut* is feeling truly lower than others. The Mishnah's words point to the second. But how can someone who keeps the Torah sincerely think he is lower than a person who does wrong? The Alter Rebbe rejects the easy answer that this only means being polite; he wants it to be sincere.

## Step one: stand in his place

Tanya's answer begins with the Sages' words, "Judge not your fellow until you have stood in his place." Circumstances and temperament shape how hard a struggle is. A person whose work keeps him in a world full of temptations, or who is born with strong passions, faces a harder battle than someone whose life is quiet and calm. We rarely know what another person is up against.

## Step two: check your own effort

The chapter then turns the question inward. Even a person with strong temptations has no excuse, since the mind can govern the heart; but each of us should ask, honestly, do I struggle with my own impulses with the same intensity that the most tempted person would need to bring to his? The chapter lists everyday places where real effort is needed:

- praying with real focus, which is a wrestling match with the body and the animal soul
- saying blessings and Grace after Meals with intention
- doing mitzvot for their own sake
- studying Torah beyond what comes naturally
- giving charity generously
- avoiding words that put another person down, even a remark that seems harmless or a story that makes oneself look good

Many of us, if we are honest, are not fighting in these areas as hard as someone fighting a far more difficult battle. Tanya also notes that a person who knows better is held to a higher standard.

## What humility does for you

The chapter's aim is that this honest reflection breaks pride, the arrogance of the animal soul, so that the light of the soul can shine through. Notice what it is not. It is not hating yourself or saying you have no talents. It begins from an accurate view of both. For the balance between humility and healthy self-worth, see [Jewish views on self-esteem](/learn/self-esteem-jewish-view). If you are struggling with feeling worthless, that is a different matter and professional help is right; ask the Rabbi.

## Common questions

### What does Tanya say about humility?
Chapter 30 says to be lowly of spirit before everyone, and explains how: do not judge your fellow until you stand in his place, and measure your own effort honestly against his struggle.

### What is the difference between anavah and shefalut?
According to Chabad.org's presentation, *anavah* is recognizing your strengths without boasting, because they are gifts. *Shefalut* is feeling truly lower than others.

### Does humility mean thinking I am worthless?
No. Tanya's humility is an honest view that breaks pride. It does not deny your gifts or your worth.

### How can I be humbler than someone who does wrong?
Tanya says you may not know his circumstances and temperament, and that your own small lapses may count more given what you know and have been given.

### Is it a sin to judge others?
Tanya quotes the Sages not to judge until you stand in his place. For case-by-case questions, ask the Rabbi.
