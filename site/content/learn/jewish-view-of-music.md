In Jewish tradition, music is a way for the soul to reach higher than words can. A niggun (נִגּוּן) is a Chassidic melody, usually without words, and Chassidic teaching describes it as a path to higher consciousness. Rabbi Schneur Zalman of Liadi, the founder of Chabad, put it this way: words are the pen of the heart, and song is the pen of the soul.

## Why a niggun has no words

Rabbi Tzvi Freeman explains in his writing on the niggun that words carry meaning downward, from G-d's thought through the sages and prophets into human hearts. Song moves in the opposite direction: it lifts the soul upward. Because words define and limit, a niggun usually has none, so the soul can break past those limits.

## The tzaddik's melody

A tzaddik (צַדִּיק) is a righteous person. Chassidic teaching teaches that a tzaddik encodes his soul in his niggunim. When we sing a niggun, we connect to the innermost layers of that tzaddik's soul and to the light he reached. For this reason, every note and nuance must be learned and sung with care, like a sacred text. You can read about the Chassidic masters in [Chassidus](/learn/chassidus) and about the founder of the movement in [the Baal Shem Tov](/learn/baal-shem-tov).

## Gates, repetition and patience

Jewish sources say that the sections of a niggun are called "gates," each one an entrance from one spiritual world to a higher one. Rabbi Sholom DovBer, the fifth Rebbe of Chabad, taught that each gate should be sung twice: the first time it traces a form, and the second time it carves deeply into the soul. A niggun should never be rushed. Its pace and its pauses are part of how it reaches deep inside.

## Music opens the heart

The Chabad teachings that Freeman cites say that contemplation can stay in the cold intellect, while a niggun draws what is hidden in the soul down into the mind and then into the heart. Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak of Lubavitch taught that a niggun opens windows in the soul.

The article also notes that the ancient prophets sang and played instruments while awaiting prophecy, and that David's songs healed King Saul's troubled spirit. See [King David](/learn/king-david). According to Rabbi Dovber, the second Rebbe of Chabad, song lies at the core of life: every created thing yearns for its source, and that yearning is its song.

## Song unites us

Chassidic teaching quotes the Lubavitcher Rebbe, Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson, who taught that spoken words are understood differently by each listener, but song unites singers and listeners in one pulse and one melody. A niggun is like a circle: it has no clear beginning or end.

## Common questions

### What is a niggun?
A niggun is a Chassidic melody, usually wordless, that is meant to express and stir the soul. Chassidic thought calls it a path to higher consciousness.

### Why do Chassidim sing niggunim without words?
We are taught that words define and limit, while a niggun lifts the soul past those boundaries.

### Whose soul is in a niggun?
It is explained that a tzaddik encodes his soul in his niggunim. For specific melodies, ask the Rabbi.

### Why is a niggun sung twice or slowly?
Rabbi Sholom DovBer taught that each gate of a niggun should be sung twice, and that a niggun should never be rushed.

### Does music have power to heal?
Jewish sources say that a niggun brings renewed life, softens a bitter soul and fills a home with light, and compares it to David's songs that healed King Saul.
