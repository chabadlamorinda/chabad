If your child is the only Jewish kid in class at Chanukah, the best thing you can do is help them feel proud and prepared, not different. Chanukah is a holiday about a small group staying true to who they were, and it gives a child the perfect story, and a bright menorah, to share.

## Start at home: pride before school

Children take their cue from us. If Chanukah at home is joyful, with lights, songs, gelt and the family together, your child walks into school feeling that being Jewish is something good to have. Tell them the story in simple words (see the [Chanukah story](/learn/chanukah-story)): a few brave people, one little jar of oil, and a light that kept going.

Chabad.org's teaching about the menorah offers a beautiful image. The Chanukah menorah is lit in a window or doorway facing the street, so the light of the home shines outward to everyone. The idea is that a strong, warm Jewish home gives a child the confidence to share its light with the world. See [Jewish pride](/learn/jewish-pride) for more.

## Give your child words

Children feel more confident when they know what they might say. Practice a few short sentences together:

- "Chanukah is the Jewish Festival of Lights. We light a candle each night for eight nights."
- "A long time ago the Jews won a war for the right to be Jewish. We remember a little jar of oil that lasted eight days."
- "I get to light the menorah with my family, and I love it."
- "A dreidel is a spinning top with Hebrew letters: nun, gimmel, hey, shin, for 'A great miracle happened there.'"

Role-play it as a game. If a classmate asks a question your child cannot answer, "I will ask my Rabbi and tell you tomorrow" is a fine reply.

## Talk with the teacher

A friendly note or conversation before the holiday opens many doors.

1. **Introduce yourself.** Tell the teacher that your family celebrates Chanukah and that your child is happy to share, if the child wants to.
2. **Offer something simple.** A short story, a dreidel game or kosher treats the class can enjoy. Our guide on [presenting a Jewish holiday in class](/learn/presenting-a-jewish-holiday-in-class) has a ready plan.
3. **Ask about the calendar.** If there is a school event on a night when you light, or classroom food that your child cannot eat, ask early. See [Jewish holidays at school and work](/learn/jewish-holidays-at-school-and-work).
4. **Let your child decide.** Some children love being the expert; others prefer to be quiet. Both are fine.

## Bring the light to others

Chanukah's message is to spread light. Your child could:

- Invite a friend over to see the menorah and enjoy a latke or donut.
- Make a card for a classmate or teacher, with a short thank-you for being kind.
- Share the story of the menorah: not a speech, just a few warm sentences.

Teach them that being Jewish in a small group is not a burden but a chance to shine. If a hurtful comment ever happens, listen without panic and see our guide to [talking to kids about antisemitism](/learn/talking-to-kids-about-antisemitism), and call the Rabbi.

## Common questions

### How do I help my child feel proud to be Jewish at school?
Make Chanukah and other Jewish moments joyful at home, tell the stories simply, and practice a few sentences your child can say. Pride grows from warmth, not pressure.

### Should my child share Chanukah with the class?
If they want to, yes: a short story, a dreidel game or a treat is plenty. Talk to the teacher first. If your child prefers not to present, that is fine too.

### What should I say to the teacher?
A friendly note: that your family celebrates Chanukah, what your child may enjoy sharing, and any food or schedule concerns. See [presenting a Jewish holiday in class](/learn/presenting-a-jewish-holiday-in-class).

### What if my child feels left out?
Listen first and tell them it is natural to feel different. Remind them of what they are part of, and look for Jewish friends. Ask the Rabbi for ideas.

### How can we keep the menorah visible if we live in an apartment?
Place it in a window facing the street where it is safe, or by the main doorway of the room most used. Ask the Rabbi about your building.
