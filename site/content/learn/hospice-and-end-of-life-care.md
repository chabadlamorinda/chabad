Judaism teaches that every moment of life is precious, and that a person who is dying deserves comfort, care and company. Many families in the East Bay face hospice and end-of-life choices, and Jewish law has clear principles, but because every case is different, please call a rabbi early, before a form is signed.

This page is general guidance, not medical or legal advice. Your doctors and your family's own attorney advise you on those parts.

## Why life is so precious

Chabad.org explains that life belongs to G-d, who entrusted it to us to guard. Life has full value for as long as the soul is in the body, even when a person is weak, unaware or in pain. That is why Jewish law forbids hastening death, and why it also does not ask us to prolong suffering without real benefit.

## Comfort and pain relief

Caring for someone who is dying means easing pain and bringing comfort, and a hospice team can be a blessing. Hospice (a program focused on comfort rather than cure) is not "giving up" in Jewish eyes. Many Jewish families choose it. What is important is that the decisions are made with guidance, so that comfort care does not become hastening death. A rabbi who knows your family can discuss each medicine and step with the medical team. See also [pikuach nefesh](/learn/pikuach-nefesh).

## Treatment, feeding and machines

According to Chabad.org:

- Treatment that only prolongs dying and suffering, with no real benefit, need not be used.
- Actively hastening death is forbidden.
- Removing a respirator or feeding tube raises serious concerns and requires expert rabbinic guidance.
- Providing food and water is obligatory and is not considered medical treatment.

These are principles, not a script. What applies to your parent or spouse depends on their condition, so ask the Rabbi.

## DNR, POLST and other forms

A broad "Do Not Resuscitate" order or a general advance directive can be written in ways that conflict with Jewish law. Please do not sign any form before speaking to a rabbi. A rabbi can help you choose wording or a form that fits Jewish law, and a [halachic will](/learn/halachic-will) or health-care proxy can name a rabbi to advise the person making decisions. Ask an attorney for the legal side.

## Assisted dying

Judaism does not permit assisted suicide or euthanasia, and holds that life has infinite value. This is not a political statement. It is a view about the dignity of every moment, and the answer for a family in pain is more care and more company, not less. If someone you love is asking about this, talk to the Rabbi, who will listen with compassion.

## Common questions

### What is the Jewish view of hospice?
Judaism values comfort and care for the dying, and many Jewish families use hospice. The key is to speak with a rabbi so that comfort never becomes hastening death.

### Is a DNR allowed in Jewish law?
It depends on the person's condition and the form. Ask a rabbi before signing any DNR or advance directive.

### Is a feeding tube required in Jewish law?
Providing food and water is considered obligatory and not a medical treatment, but cases differ, so ask the Rabbi.

### Is pain medication allowed at the end of life?
Easing pain is important and permitted, as long as the goal is comfort, not hastening death. Have a rabbi speak with the doctor.

### What does Judaism say about assisted dying?
It is forbidden, because every moment of life has value. The answer is more care and companionship. Talk to the Rabbi if you or a loved one is struggling.
