Hospitals do not allow open flames, so a patient cannot light a real menorah in a hospital room. Jewish law puts health and safety first, and it offers gentle options: a family member lights at home, a battery or electric menorah brings the light into the room without the blessings, and visitors bring Chanukah warmth with songs, stories and presence.

## Why can't a patient light a flame in the hospital?

Fire in a room with oxygen and medical equipment is dangerous, and a hospital's rules are there to protect every patient. Judaism agrees: saving and guarding life overrides almost everything else, and no one is asked to risk their own safety or anyone else's to light a menorah.

The mitzvah of Chanukah (חֲנֻכָּה) is to kindle lights with fuel, wick and flame. Chabad.org explains that electric lights generally do not fulfill it, and that where a flame truly is not possible, you should ask a rabbi. Chabad.org also says that a battery-powered light may be considered in difficult circumstances, but that the blessings are not recited over it.

## Options for a patient

1. **Light at home, with the patient in mind.** A spouse or relative lights a real menorah at home. A call that evening, or a photo of the lights, lets the patient feel part of it. Whether the patient fulfills the mitzvah through someone else's lighting depends on the situation, so ask the Rabbi.
2. **A battery menorah by the bed.** Many families place a small electric or battery menorah near the bed, if the hospital allows it. It does not take the place of the mitzvah and is lit without blessings, but it brings the holiday into the room.
3. **A lighting in a lobby or chapel.** Some hospitals may have a menorah in a shared space. Ask the nurse or the hospital's spiritual care office; whether any flame is allowed is the hospital's decision.
4. **Songs and words.** Sing [Haneirot Halalu or Maoz Tzur](/learn/chanukah-songs), say a few words of the story, and let the patient simply listen. Even a short moment helps.
5. **Prayer and Tehillim.** If it is comforting, say a chapter of [Tehillim](/tehillim) together, or send a name to the [prayer list](/tehillim).

## What a visit can bring

Chanukah is a holiday of bringing light into dark places. Chabad.org shares a story of a woman whose husband was in intensive care during Chanukah. Candles were not allowed, so she brought an electric menorah and set it across from his bed. The small light stayed with the family for years. It shows that a modest light, offered with love, matters.

If you visit a patient this Chanukah:

- **Ask first.** Check with the nurse about visiting rules, food and anything you plan to bring.
- **Keep it short and warm.** A few songs, a Chanukah card, or a drawing from the children.
- **Bring a little of the holiday.** A photo of the family's menorah at home.
- **Listen.** Sometimes the best gift is time. See [bikur cholim, visiting the sick](/learn/bikur-cholim).

## Common questions

### Can I light a Chanukah menorah in a hospital room?
No, hospitals do not allow open flames, and safety comes first. You can use a battery menorah if the hospital allows it, light at home, and sing and say the story with the patient.

### Do I say the blessings over an electric menorah in the hospital?
No. Chabad.org teaches that the blessings are not said over electric or battery lights. They still bring the joy of the holiday into the room.

### Can a spouse light at home for a patient in the hospital?
A family member can light at home, and the patient shares in the joy. Whether it counts as the patient's own lighting depends on the situation, so please ask the Rabbi.

### What if I am the patient and cannot light at all?
You are not alone and you are not failing a mitzvah. Join by watching a family member light, singing the songs or listening. Call the Rabbi for guidance on your own situation.

### Can I bring latkes or donuts to a hospital patient?
Only if the patient's diet allows it and the hospital approves. Check with the nurse first.
