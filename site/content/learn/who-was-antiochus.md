Antiochus IV, also called Antiochus Epiphanes, was the Seleucid or Syrian-Greek king who ruled the Land of Israel in the second century BCE and issued the harsh decrees against Judaism that led to the Chanukah story. He is the villain of the holiday, the king the Maccabees rose up against.

Here is who he was, what he did, and why he matters to a Jewish family in Lafayette, Moraga or Orinda today.

## A king of a divided empire

When Alexander the Great died, his empire split into several kingdoms. One of them, the Seleucid Empire, was centered in Syria and stretched from Asia Minor in the west to Persia in the east. Its capital was Antioch, in what is today southern Turkey; Jews lived there in Talmudic times.

"Antiochus" was a family name. More than a dozen Seleucid rulers carried it over some 250 years, so when we say "Antiochus" in the Chanukah story we mean the fourth one. He reigned roughly in the middle of the 420 years the Second Temple stood. For the wider period see [The Second Temple Period](/learn/second-temple-period).

## What kind of king was he?

Antiochus took the title "Epiphanes," a Greek title usually understood as "illustrious" or "the one who appears." Some of his own contemporaries gave him a different nickname, "the Mad." Jewish tradition describes him as harsh, cruel, rash and impulsive.

He wanted one culture for his whole empire, the Greek one. Some Jews, called Hellenists, welcomed this and adopted Greek ways. Others, among them the priestly family of Matityahu, refused to assimilate.

## What he did to the Jewish people

Encouraged by the Hellenists, Antiochus decided to wipe out Jewish practice.

- He banned Shabbat, Rosh Chodesh and brit milah (read why in [The Greek Decrees](/learn/decrees-of-the-greeks)).
- He banned the study of Torah.
- He had a pig sacrificed in the Holy Temple, and the Temple's oils were made impure.
- He looted the Temple. The tradition records that its treasures were taken.
- Thousands of Jews were tortured and killed for refusing to give up their faith. One of the most painful stories is that of [Chana and her seven sons](/learn/chana-and-her-seven-sons).

## How the story ended for him

Matityahu the priest and his son Judah the Maccabee led a small, poorly armed band against the royal armies and won, which our Sages credit to a miracle. After the Temple was rededicated, the wars went on for about 25 more years, most of them after Antiochus himself had died. His successors kept sending armies and kept losing.

Accounts of Antiochus's own end differ. One source says he died in Persia. Megillat Antiochus, an ancient scroll telling the Chanukah story, says that he fled and drowned himself in the sea. Since we do not have one agreed account, we leave it there.

## Megillat Antiochus

This scroll, also called the Scroll of the Hasmoneans, was written in Aramaic and retells the story of the king, the decrees and the victory. Some attribute it to the five sons of Matityahu; others to a writer of the period of the Mishnah. It was never included in the 24 books of the Tanach. Some communities, including Yemenite Jews, still read it at Chanukah, without a blessing.

## Why he matters today

The lesson is not about one man. Kings like Antiochus come and go, and the Chanukah lights are still burning about 2,200 years later. A decree can ban Jewish practice, but it cannot extinguish the Jewish soul. It is worth reading about him so that the villain of the story is not a cartoon, and so the courage of the Maccabees is understood. ## Common questions

### Who was Antiochus in the Chanukah story?
Antiochus IV was the Syrian-Greek king who ruled the Land of Israel and banned Shabbat, Rosh Chodesh, brit milah and Torah study. He also defiled the Temple.

### Was Antiochus a real historical person?
Yes. Antiochus IV Epiphanes was a Seleucid king in the second century BCE, and the Chanukah story tells of what he did to the Jews of his time.

### What does "Antiochus Epiphanes" mean?
Epiphanes is a Greek title usually understood as "illustrious." Some of his contemporaries mocked him as "the Mad."

### What happened to Antiochus?
Accounts differ: one says he died in Persia, and Megillat Antiochus says he fled and drowned himself. The Chanukah story's main point is that his decrees failed.

### Is Antiochus the same as Haman?
No. Haman's plan in the Purim story was to destroy the Jewish people physically, while Antiochus wanted Jews to give up the Torah. They lived in different periods and under different empires.
