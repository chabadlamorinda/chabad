Keeping kosher at home is one thing: you control the kitchen. Eating out is where the questions begin. The good news is that you can keep kosher and still have a full social life. Here is how.

## What makes a restaurant kosher

A kosher restaurant is not one that serves Jewish food or avoids pork. It is one where every ingredient is kosher, meat and dairy are kept apart, the kitchen and its equipment are kosher, and a trained supervisor (*mashgiach*) checks all of it under the responsibility of a rabbi or kosher agency. The restaurant displays a certificate, which should be current and name the agency; a sign that says "kosher-style" means the food is not kosher.

Why does supervision matter so much? Because the problems are the ones you cannot see: a vegetable soup made with chicken stock, a grill shared with non-kosher meat, cheese made with animal rennet, wine that is not kosher, lettuce full of insects, cooking by someone who is not Jewish in a way Jewish law regulates. See [kosher certification](/learn/kosher-certification) and [kosher symbols](/learn/kosher-symbols).

## Vegetarian and dairy restaurants are not automatically kosher

This is the most common misunderstanding. A vegetarian or vegan restaurant has no meat, so there is no mixing of [meat and milk](/learn/meat-and-milk), but it is still not kosher without supervision. Its cheeses, wines, vinegars and oils may not be kosher; its vegetables are not checked for insects (see [checking for insects](/learn/checking-for-insects)); its equipment was never made kosher; and Jewish law has rules about food cooked by a non-Jew that apply even to vegetables. A vegetarian menu is a good sign, not a kosher certificate. When no kosher restaurant is available, ask the Rabbi what, if anything, can be ordered in such a place.

## Eating at friends' homes

An invitation from friends who do not keep kosher is a kindness, and you can answer it kindly. Most people appreciate honesty more than they mind it: "We keep kosher, so we cannot eat food cooked in your kitchen, but we would love to come." Then make it easy: bring a kosher dish or dessert that everyone can share, eat fresh whole fruit, or drink from sealed bottles with a kosher symbol. See [Jewish friendship](/learn/jewish-friendship).

## Business meals

Suggest a kosher restaurant when you can; colleagues are usually happy to try somewhere new. If the meeting is elsewhere, eat before, and at the table order a sealed drink, a whole fruit, or nothing at all with a smile; nobody minds, and many respect it. Keeping kosher at work openly and without apology is itself a quiet [Kiddush Hashem](/learn/kiddush-hashem).

## Travel and airline meals

Most airlines offer kosher meals on longer flights if you order well in advance, usually when you book or at least a day or two before. They arrive sealed with a certification label; check that the seal is intact and open it yourself. On the road, pack basics, look for kosher symbols in ordinary supermarkets, and find the Chabad center wherever you land; there is almost always a Shabbat meal waiting. See [kosher when traveling](/learn/kosher-when-traveling) and [kosher on campus](/learn/kosher-on-campus) for students.

## Common questions

### Can I eat a salad at a non-kosher restaurant?
Not simply. Dressings, cheeses, croutons, cooked ingredients and unchecked lettuce all raise questions, and the plates and utensils are not kosher. Ask the Rabbi what can be done in your situation; in general, a sealed drink or a whole fruit is the safe choice.

### Is sushi kosher?
Fish with fins and scales, such as salmon and tuna, are kosher, but sushi in an ordinary restaurant is not: the rice, vinegar, sauces, imitation crab and the knives and boards that also cut non-kosher fish all matter. Kosher sushi exists in kosher restaurants and stores.

### Can I drink coffee at a cafe?
Plain black coffee from a cafe is widely allowed, and many people also take it with milk in a paper cup; flavored syrups and pastries need a kosher symbol. Ask the Rabbi about the practice he recommends.
