After Yom Kippur ends at nightfall, we hear Havdalah and then sit down to a festive meal, which is meant to be a true celebration and not just a way to stop being hungry. Jewish tradition says a heavenly voice calls out, "Go, eat your bread with joy," and many families start building their sukkah that very night.

## Why the break-fast is a celebration

The Torah's verse in Ecclesiastes (9:7), "Go, eat your bread with joy and drink your wine with a glad heart," is understood by our sages to be said on the night after Yom Kippur, according to Chabad.org. After the long fast, we trust that we were forgiven, so the meal is joyful. The shofar blast at the end of [Neilah](/learn/neilah) announces it.

Chassidic teaching adds that this meal channels the spiritual gains of Yom Kippur into material blessings for the year ahead. In other words, ordinary eating becomes holy.

## Step by step: the first hour

1. **Havdalah.** After nightfall we hear the Havdalah blessing, as at the end of any Shabbat, to mark the end of the holy day. See [Havdalah](/learn/havdalah). Ask the Rabbi about the exact order.
2. **Wash and have bread.** It is customary to wash hands for bread and dip it in honey, as on [Rosh Hashanah](/learn/rosh-hashanah-for-beginners).
3. **Greet each other.** People wish each other a good year, as on festivals and Shabbat.
4. **Enjoy the meal.** There are no special food rules, but the food should be tasty and festive.
5. **Talk about the sukkah.** Ideally we begin building it that night, or at least discuss it, moving straight from one mitzvah to the next.

## What to serve

Chabad.org says there are no special food rules. For your family, here are practical ideas:

- **Start gently.** After a day without food or water, begin with water, juice or tea, something light, and some bread before heavier dishes.
- **Dairy or meat?** Both are fine. Families follow their own custom; if you keep kosher, the usual rules apply, and the Rabbi can help.
- **Keep it simple.** A meal you can prepare ahead of time, so nobody is busy in the kitchen at the end of the day, is the best break-fast.
- **Consider guests.** A break-fast is a wonderful time to invite neighbors or someone who would otherwise eat alone. See [welcoming guests](/learn/hachnasat-orchim) if you want to learn more.

Please do not worry about the "right" menu. If you are not sure whether a food is kosher, ask the Rabbi; we do not certify any brand.

## Moving straight to Sukkot

The days between Yom Kippur and [Sukkot](/learn/sukkot-for-beginners) are only four. In a family with children, this is a good moment: hammer in the first nails, hang the first decoration, or plan where the [sukkah](/learn/sukkah) will go. It turns the quiet end of Yom Kippur into the energy of the next holiday.

## Common questions

### What do you eat to break the fast on Yom Kippur?
There are no special food rules. Many families start with water or juice, then bread dipped in honey, then a festive meal of dairy or meat. Start with lighter foods if you are not feeling well.

### Do you say Havdalah before eating after Yom Kippur?
Yes, Havdalah is said first, after nightfall, as at the end of Shabbat. Ask the Rabbi for the order and the details.

### Is the meal after Yom Kippur a celebration?
Yes. Tradition says a heavenly voice calls, "Go, eat your bread with joy," and the meal is festive, not merely practical.

### Why do we dip bread in honey after Yom Kippur?
It is the custom, as on Rosh Hashanah, for a sweet year. Chabad.org notes it is customary to wash and dip bread in honey.

### Should we start the sukkah after Yom Kippur?
Ideally yes: start building it that night, or at least discuss it, to go from one mitzvah to the next.
