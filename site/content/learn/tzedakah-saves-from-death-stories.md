"Charity delivers from death" is a teaching of the Sages, and the Talmud tells two stories about it: the daughter of Rabbi Akiva, saved from a snake on her wedding day after she gave her food to a poor man, and Binyamin the Righteous, who was granted more years of life. These are stories the Sages tell, not guarantees for any one person's life.

## The first story: Rabbi Akiva's daughter (Shabbat 156b)

The story is told this way. Astrologers warned Rabbi Akiva that his daughter would be bitten by a snake on the day she entered the bridal chamber. On her wedding night she pushed a pin into the wall. When she pulled it out the next morning, a poisonous snake came out with it, its eye pierced.

Her father asked if she had done anything unusual. She said that in the evening a poor man had come to the door, when everyone was busy with the wedding feast and no one was paying attention to him. She had given him her own portion of the food.

Rabbi Akiva said, "Charity delivers from death." He also taught that charity saves not only from an unnatural death but from death itself.

## The second story: Binyamin HaTzaddik (Bava Batra 11a)

Binyamin the Righteous was in charge of the community's charity fund. A Questions and Answers piece on parshat Re'eh relates that in a year of scarcity, a woman with seven children came for help. He told her the fund was empty. She said that without help she and her children would die. He paid her from his own pocket.

Later Binyamin became gravely ill. In the Talmud's telling, the angels pleaded for him, and his sentence was torn up and twenty-two years were added to his life. There are two explanations: one connects it to the blessings he earned for giving and for comforting her with kind words, and the other links the twenty-two years to the Hebrew word for "hungry," *ra'eiv* (רָעֵב).

## How to read these stories

The Sages teach what giving means in the eyes of Heaven. They are stories of two people who gave when it mattered, not formulas. If someone who gives is still ill, or loses a loved one, no one is to conclude that they did something wrong. For anything personal, call the Rabbi.

Note what both stories share: the giving was ordinary and personal. A portion of food at a wedding. A few coins from one's own pocket, with a kind word. See also [Segulot](/learn/segulot) and [Tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah).

## What to take from them

- **Notice the person at the door.** The Talmud notes that no one was attending to him.
- **Give a kind word.** Binyamin's comfort is part of why he is remembered.
- **Give even when you are told there is nothing to give.** He gave from his own pocket.

## Common questions

### What does "charity saves from death" mean?
It is a teaching from the Talmud, spoken by Rabbi Akiva after his daughter's rescue. It shows the great spiritual power of tzedakah; it is not a promise about any one person's health.

### What is the story of Rabbi Akiva's daughter and the snake?
Astrologers said she would be bitten by a snake on her wedding day. She gave her portion of food to a poor man, and the next morning a snake was found dead, pierced by her pin. Rabbi Akiva taught that charity delivers from death.

### Who was Binyamin HaTzaddik?
A Talmudic figure who supervised a community charity fund. He helped a mother of seven children from his own pocket and, the Talmud says, was granted twenty-two more years of life.

### Does giving charity guarantee protection?
These stories do not say that. For personal questions, ask the Rabbi.
