In the middle of the spring, between Passover and Shavuot, comes a day of bonfires, music, picnics and children marching with flags. It is called Lag BaOmer, and for many families it is the happiest outdoor day of the Jewish year.

## What the name means

Between Passover and Shavuot we [count the Omer](/learn/counting-the-omer), forty-nine days from the Exodus to the giving of the Torah. The Hebrew letters *lamed* and *gimmel* have the numerical value of thirty-three, so "Lag BaOmer" simply means "the 33rd day of the Omer." It falls on the 18th of the Hebrew month of Iyar. See [this year's date](/holidays/lag-baomer).

## Why the Omer is a quiet time, and why this day is different

The Talmud tells that in the weeks after Passover, 24,000 students of the great Rabbi Akiva died in a plague, because they did not treat one another with proper respect. For this reason the Omer period is kept as a time of semi-mourning: no weddings, no music, no haircuts. On Lag BaOmer the dying stopped. The mourning lifts, and the day is one of open joy. Many weddings are held on Lag BaOmer, and children who have been waiting get their haircuts.

The lesson behind the sadness is the lesson of the day: Jews who love Torah must love one another too. Rabbi Akiva himself taught that "Love your fellow as yourself" is the great principle of the Torah.

## Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai and the inner Torah

Lag BaOmer is also the yahrzeit, the day of passing, of Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai, one of Rabbi Akiva's five remaining students. Rabbi Shimon hid from the Romans in a cave for thirteen years, and he is the great teacher of the inner, mystical dimension of the Torah that was later written down in the Zohar. On the day he left this world he revealed deep secrets to his students and asked that the day be remembered with joy rather than sorrow.

The bonfires are for him. Light is the symbol of the hidden Torah that he brought into the open, and of the light a single soul can bring into the world. Around the world, Jewish communities gather around a fire, sing and tell his story. See [Kabbalah](/learn/kabbalah) and [Chassidus](/learn/chassidus), which carry his teachings into everyday life.

## Children at the center

The Rebbe placed children at the heart of Lag BaOmer. He encouraged Chabad communities everywhere to hold Lag BaOmer parades for Jewish children: marching with banners, singing, hearing the Torah's twelve verses, and feeling proud to be Jewish in public. The idea is simple. A child who marches with other Jewish children, celebrating the unity of the Jewish people on the very day that unity is the theme, carries that pride for a lifetime.

Lag BaOmer is also a favorite day for the [upsherin](/learn/upsherin), a boy's first haircut at age three, often celebrated with a bonfire and a crowd of friends.

## How to celebrate

- **Come to a community celebration**: a bonfire or barbecue, music, games for the kids, and the story of Rabbi Shimon.
- **Go outdoors**: a picnic, a hike, bows and arrows for the children (a tradition recalling the rainbow, which never appeared in Rabbi Shimon's lifetime because his merit protected the world).
- **Learn something**: a teaching of Rabbi Shimon or a page of the Zohar in translation.
- **Do a mitzvah of unity**: a kind word, a call to someone who is alone, a gift of [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah).

## Common questions

### Can I have a haircut or a wedding on Lag BaOmer?
Yes. Lag BaOmer is the day the mourning of the Omer lifts, and it is a traditional day for haircuts and weddings. Customs differ about the days after it, so ask the Rabbi.

### Is Lag BaOmer a holiday with work restrictions?
No. It is a day of joy and celebration, but work, driving and cooking are all permitted. The regular weekday prayers are said, without the Tachanun supplications.

### Why do children play with bows and arrows?
The Talmud says that in Rabbi Shimon's lifetime no rainbow was seen, because his merit was enough to protect the generation. The bow reminds us of that, and children have a wonderful time.

### Is there a special food for Lag BaOmer?
There is no required food, but carob is traditional, because Rabbi Shimon and his son lived on carob and water during their years in the cave. Most families simply enjoy a barbecue or picnic.
