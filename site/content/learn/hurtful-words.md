Ona'at devarim (אונאת דברים) is the Torah's commandment not to hurt, shame or distress another person with words. It comes from the verse, "You shall not wrong one another, and you shall fear your G-d" (Leviticus 25:17), and it is different from lashon hara (gossip), which is speaking badly about someone to others. Here the words are said to the person, and they wound.

Whether in a family kitchen in Lafayette, a group chat in Moraga or a school hallway in Orinda, words can hurt more than we mean them to. Judaism takes that seriously.

## What the Torah forbids

Chabad.org, in its explanation of this commandment, says it prohibits verbally distressing or humiliating another person. It adds that the Sages considered hurtful words a graver sin than cheating someone of money, because money can be returned, but words cannot be taken back.

## Everyday examples

Chabad.org gives these examples:

- **Reminding someone of their past.** Do not remind a person who has changed of what they used to do, even in a hint, or by saying how thankful they should be to have been rescued from it.
- **Blaming the sick.** Do not suggest that a person's suffering means they did something wrong. Job's friends are the example.
- **Misleading a buyer.** Do not send someone to a seller who has nothing for them.
- **Pretending to shop.** Do not ask the price of an item when you have no intention of buying.

Chabad.org also mentions that the Torah is especially strict about words toward the vulnerable, such as a widow, orphan or convert, and says we must be sensitive and avoid anything that might shame, embarrass or hurt. It also states that threatening children with frightening things is not the Jewish way to raise them.

## Shaming in public

Ethics of the Fathers (3:11) teaches that one who embarrasses a friend in public loses a share in the World to Come. That is a very strong statement, and it shows how seriously Judaism takes dignity. A private word, said gently, is very different from a public correction. See [giving criticism kindly](/learn/giving-criticism-kindly).

## What this means today

Our words travel further than ever, and so do the hurts. The principle is the same, and Jewish tradition would apply it to the new settings as well (if you are unsure, ask the Rabbi):

1. **Texts and posts.** There is no tone of voice in a message. Reread it, and if it might sting, rewrite it.
2. **"Just joking."** A joke at someone's expense, especially in front of others, is still hurtful.
3. **Family.** Words to a spouse, a parent or a child sink deepest. See [being a mensch](/learn/being-a-mensch).
4. **School and the workplace.** Teasing and exclusion can be cruel. See [the Jewish view of bullying](/learn/bullying-jewish-view).
5. **Before you speak,** ask: Is it true? Is it kind? Is it necessary? That is a rule of thumb, not an official text.

## How to repair it

If you said something that hurt:

1. **Name it.** "What I said was hurtful."
2. **Apologize plainly,** without "but" or "if you were offended."
3. **Ask what would help,** and do it.
4. **Change the habit.** See [teshuvah](/learn/teshuvah) for the Jewish path of repair.

If the person has died or cannot be found, or you do not know how to approach the situation, ask the Rabbi. We will help you think it through.

## Common questions

### What is ona'at devarim?
It is the Torah commandment not to hurt, shame or distress someone with words, based on Leviticus 25:17.

### How is ona'at devarim different from lashon hara?
Lashon hara is speaking badly about someone to others. Ona'at devarim is hurting someone with words said to them.

### Is it really worse than stealing?
Chabad.org says the Sages considered hurtful words a graver sin than cheating someone of money, since money can be returned but words cannot.

### Is a joke allowed if no one means harm?
Not if it hurts someone. Intent matters, but so does the effect on the person.

### Does this apply to text messages and social media?
Words are words, wherever they are written. If you are unsure about a specific case, ask the Rabbi.

### How do I make it right if I hurt someone?
Name it, apologize sincerely, and change. It is a good idea to do so soon.
