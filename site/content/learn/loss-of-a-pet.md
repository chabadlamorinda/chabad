Losing a pet can be truly painful, and Judaism takes that pain seriously. While the formal mourning rituals, such as shiva and Kaddish, are reserved for human relatives, your grief is real, and there are meaningful Jewish ways to remember an animal you loved.

## Your feelings are real

A dog, cat or other pet is often part of the family. It greets you every day, keeps you company and loves without conditions. When it dies, the house feels empty. Judaism does not ask you to hide that sadness. King David wrote, "G-d's mercy is upon all His creatures" (Psalms 145:9). Feeling compassion for an animal is a reflection of that Divine mercy.

## Kindness to animals in Jewish law

The Torah cares deeply about animals. *Tza'ar ba'alei chayim*, causing needless suffering to animals, is forbidden. Some examples:

- Animals rest on Shabbat too.
- You must feed your animals before you sit down to eat yourself.
- You must help unload an animal struggling under its burden, even if it belongs to someone you dislike.

Caring for your pet throughout its life was a mitzvah, a form of [chesed](/learn/chesed).

## Why there is no shiva or Kaddish for a pet

The laws of mourning, like [shiva](/learn/shiva) and [Kaddish](/learn/kaddish), are about the human soul, created in the image of G-d, and the relationships between people. They are not used for animals, so as to keep their special meaning. This is not a judgment about how much you loved your pet. It is simply that these rituals belong to a different kind of loss.

## Meaningful ways to remember a pet

- **Bury it with respect.** You may bury your pet in a pet cemetery or your yard, where local rules permit. A Jewish cemetery is not used for animals.
- **Say a chapter of Psalms.** Reading [Tehillim](/tehillim) can bring comfort to you and your family. Psalm 104 praises G-d for caring for all creatures.
- **Give tzedakah.** A donation to help others, or to an animal shelter, turns sadness into kindness.
- **Do a kind deed.** Help a neighbor with their animal, or volunteer.
- **Share memories** with family. Telling stories and looking at photos helps everyone heal.

## Helping children through the loss

For many children, the death of a pet is their first experience of loss. Be honest and gentle: tell them the pet died, avoid saying it "went to sleep," and let them cry and ask questions. Let them help with a small goodbye, draw a picture or put a coin in a [pushka](/learn/pushka) for tzedakah. Reassure them that sadness is normal and that it will get easier.

## Common questions

### Do animals have souls in Judaism?
Yes. Jewish mystical teaching says every creature has a G-dly spark that gives it life. An animal's soul is different from a human soul, which has free choice and the ability to know G-d. Read more about [the soul](/learn/the-soul).

### Is putting a sick pet to sleep allowed?
Jewish law forbids causing animals needless suffering, and many authorities permit ending the life of a suffering animal through a humane process. Each situation is different, so ask the Rabbi.

### Can I light a candle for my pet?
A memorial candle is a custom for human loved ones, so it is not used for pets. Giving tzedakah or doing a good deed in your pet's memory is a beautiful alternative.

### Is it wrong to get another pet soon?
No. There is no set waiting period. Some people want a new companion right away and some need time. Do what feels right for your family.
