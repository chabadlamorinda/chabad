Hillel and Shammai were two great sages who lived in the first century before the common era and led the Sanhedrin, the highest Jewish court. Their two schools, the Houses of Hillel and Shammai, argued over countless questions of Jewish law, and their disagreements are held up as the model of a debate "for the sake of Heaven."

## Two teachers, two styles

Hillel came from Babylonia to the Land of Israel as a very poor, brilliant student. Chabad.org describes him as extremely humble and patient. Shammai is described as strict and with a quick temper, who generally took the stricter view of the law, while Hillel more often took the lenient view. The Mishnah records hundreds of disagreements between their schools, and in almost every case the practice follows the House of Hillel.

## "What is hateful to you"

The Talmud (Shabbat 31a) tells of a non-Jew who said he would convert if someone could teach him the entire Torah while he stood on one foot. Shammai was insulted and sent him away. The man came to Hillel, who said: "What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor. That is the whole Torah; the rest is commentary. Go and learn it." The story teaches that the heart of Torah can be said simply, but must still be learned in practice. It also shows Hillel's patience with sincere seekers, and his goal, drawn from [Aaron](/learn/aaron-the-high-priest), to "love peace and pursue peace" and bring people close to Torah. See [love your fellow Jew](/learn/ahavat-yisrael).

## Sayings in Pirkei Avot

Both sages are quoted in [Pirkei Avot](/learn/pirkei-avot), the "Ethics of the Fathers."

- **Hillel:** "If I am not for myself, who will be for me? And when I am for myself alone, what am I? And if not now, when?" Take responsibility, think of others and do not delay.
- **Hillel:** "Be of the disciples of Aaron, loving peace and pursuing peace, loving people and bringing them close to Torah."
- **Shammai:** "Make your Torah study a fixed practice; say little and do much; greet every person with a cheerful face."

## Why the law follows Hillel

The Talmud (Eruvin 13b) says that a heavenly voice declared that both schools' words are "the words of the living G-d," but the halacha follows Hillel because they were kind and humble, studied the opinions of Shammai along with their own, and even mentioned Shammai's opinion first. The lesson: teaching begins with listening, and [humility](/learn/humility) wins. The Rabbi can explain which customs follow which opinion; if you have a practical question, ask.

## Arguing for the sake of Heaven

The Mishnah teaches that a dispute for the sake of Heaven will last, like that of Hillel and Shammai. The Rebbe's teaching, cited in Sichos in English, says these disputes continued for the sake of Heaven. In practice that means: argue to find the truth, not to win; listen to the other side; and remain friends. The Talmud notes that the two Houses still married into each other's families.

## What we can do

1. Before an argument, ask: "Am I trying to understand or to win?"
2. Tell the other side's view fairly, as Hillel's students did.
3. Greet the person cheerfully afterward.
4. Study together, even with those who think differently. [The Talmud](/learn/talmud) is the record of exactly this kind of dialogue.

## Common questions

### Who were Hillel and Shammai?
They were two leading sages of the first century BCE, heads of schools that debated Jewish law in the time of the Second Temple.

### What did Hillel say to the man who asked to learn the Torah on one foot?
He said, "What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor; that is the whole Torah, the rest is explanation; go and learn."

### Why does Jewish law follow Hillel more often than Shammai?
The Talmud says it is because the House of Hillel was kind and humble and taught Shammai's opinions too.

### What does "if I am not for myself, who will be for me" mean?
It is Hillel's saying in Pirkei Avot that you must take responsibility for your life, care about others, and act now.

### What does arguing for the sake of Heaven mean?
It means a disagreement whose purpose is to find the truth, not to win, and which leaves people friends.
