Bikur cholim means "visiting the sick," and it is one of the great mitzvahs of kindness in Judaism. A visit, a call or a prayer lifts a sick person's spirit, eases their burden and, as the Sages teach, brings them closer to healing.

## Why visiting the sick matters

The Talmud teaches that G-d Himself visited Abraham as he recovered from his circumcision, and that we are to walk in G-d's ways: just as He visits the sick, so should we. The Sages say that a visitor takes away a small part of the patient's suffering. Bikur cholim is one of the mitzvahs whose reward is enjoyed in this world while the main reward remains for the World to Come.

It applies to everyone: family, friends, neighbors and people we barely know. A great person visiting someone simple is not lowering himself; he is doing what G-d does.

## The three goals of a visit

Jewish teaching describes a visit as having three purposes:

1. **Practical help.** Find out what the person or family needs: a meal, a ride, a prescription picked up, help with the kids.
2. **Encouragement.** A warm face and a listening ear can change a whole day.
3. **Prayer.** Seeing someone's situation moves us to pray for them with real feeling.

## How to visit well, step by step

1. **Check first.** Call or text before you come. Ask the family about the best time and whether a visit is welcome.
2. **Keep it short.** A sick person tires easily. Fifteen to twenty minutes is often right, unless they want you to stay.
3. **Sit at their level.** Tradition encourages sitting rather than standing over the bed, so the patient feels you are with them.
4. **Listen more than you talk.** Let them lead. Avoid medical advice and stories about other people's illnesses.
5. **Bring something light.** A book, a small treat (check that it is allowed and kosher), a drawing from your kids.
6. **Offer a blessing.** Before leaving, wish them a full recovery. A traditional wish is "HaMakom yerachem alecha b'toch she'ar cholei Yisrael," "May G-d have mercy on you among all the sick of Israel."
7. **Follow up.** One visit is good. Regular calls are even better.

## When you cannot visit

A phone call, video chat, card or message is also a form of bikur cholim, especially when a visit is not possible or would tire the person. Sending a meal or helping a caregiver counts too.

## Praying for someone who is sick

You can pray for someone in your own words, in any language, at any time. Traditional ways include:

- **Saying Tehillim (Psalms).** Many people divide the book of Psalms among friends. See our [Tehillim page](/tehillim).
- **A Mi Sheberach.** A special prayer for healing is said when the Torah is read. Read about the [Mi Sheberach](/learn/mi-sheberach).
- **Giving tzedakah** in the person's merit, and taking on an extra mitzvah for their recovery.

When praying for someone, we use their Hebrew name and their mother's Hebrew name.

## Common questions

### Is it a mitzvah to visit someone who is not Jewish?
Yes. The Talmud teaches that we visit the sick of all communities. Kindness to every human being is a Jewish value.

### What if I do not know what to say?
You do not need perfect words. Saying "I've been thinking about you" and then listening is enough. Your presence is the gift.

### Should I visit someone in the hospital who is very ill?
Ask the family first. Sometimes they welcome visitors, and sometimes they need quiet. If a visit is not right, a call to the family or a meal for them is a wonderful alternative.
