To set a Shabbat table, put the candles and candlesticks on it, two whole challahs on a board under a cover, a Kiddush cup with wine or grape juice, a dish of salt, and a festive, ideally white, tablecloth. Each of these items carries a meaning that turns an ordinary dinner into Shabbat.

For families in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda, setting the table early on Friday is a lovely way to make the house feel like Shabbat is coming. Here is what goes where, and why.

## The table, step by step

1. **A festive tablecloth.** Ideally, use a white tablecloth. Use your best one. Set the table with the finest dishes you have; this is how we honor Shabbat as a queen.
2. **The candles.** Arrange the candlesticks on the table before Shabbat, with the candles in place. It is also a good idea to place a challah or a prayer book beside them, because of the laws of muktzeh (objects that may not be handled on Shabbat); ask the Rabbi how this applies to your table. See [Shabbat candles](/learn/shabbat-candles) and [muktzeh](/learn/muktzeh).
3. **Two whole challahs.** Place two uncut loaves on a challah board or tray. Buy three loaves so that two uncut ones are on the table for each meal.
4. **A cover for the challah.** Drape a cloth or challah cover over the loaves.
5. **The Kiddush cup.** Set a cup, with kosher wine or grape juice, near the head of the table. See [Kiddush](/learn/kiddush).
6. **Salt.** Put salt in a small bowl, so the challah can be dipped in it.
7. **A washing cup** by the sink for handwashing before the bread. See [netilat yadayim](/learn/netilat-yadayim).
8. **Prayer books or printed blessings** for the Grace after Meals, so guests can follow.

If you are hosting guests, it helps to have printed blessings and a translation of the Grace after Meals.

## Why two challahs?

The two loaves are called *lechem mishneh* (לֶחֶם מִשְׁנֶה), a "double portion" of bread. They recall the manna in the desert: before Shabbat, a double portion fell on Friday so that no one needed to gather food on Shabbat. See [challah](/learn/challah) and [Hamotzi](/learn/hamotzi) for how to say the blessing.

## Why cover the challah?

The covered loaves recall the manna, which was protected by a layer of dew. The tablecloth beneath and the cover above echo the dew below and above. The challah is also covered during Kiddush, since the Kiddush is said first, before the bread. This is a custom, not a strict law, so if you forget the cover, nothing is lost.

## Why salt?

The table at which we eat is like the holy altar in the Temple, on which every offering was salted. So we put salt on the table and dip the challah in it. It is a reminder that every Jewish home can be a small sanctuary.

## Make it comfortable for guests

The hosting tips are simple: have enough seats, soft lighting and a comfortable temperature. Put phones away, and have blessings printed. If guests are coming before Shabbat, you may invite the women and girls to light candles with you. See [Shabbat guest guide](/learn/shabbat-guest-guide) and [Friday night dinner, step by step](/learn/friday-night-dinner-step-by-step).

## Common questions

### How do you set a Shabbat table?
Place candlesticks, two whole challahs on a board with a cover, a Kiddush cup, salt, a washing cup and a festive tablecloth, and put your best dishes on the table.

### Why do we have two challahs on Shabbat?
They recall the double portion of manna that fell on Friday. This is called *lechem mishneh*.

### Why do we cover the challah?
The cover recalls the layer of dew that protected the manna. It is also covered while Kiddush is said first.

### Do I need a challah cover and a white tablecloth?
It is a custom, not a requirement. Use what you have, and add nicer things over time.

### Do you put salt on the Shabbat table?
Yes. Salt on the table recalls the altar in the Temple, and it is used for dipping the challah.

### When should I set the table?
You can set it early on Friday, as long as the candles are in place before Shabbat. Check [this week's candle-lighting time](/shabbat-times) and finish well before.
