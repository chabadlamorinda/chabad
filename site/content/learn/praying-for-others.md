Someone you love is in the hospital. A friend is going through a divorce. A relative in Israel is in danger. You want to do something, and the first thing that comes to mind is: pray for them. Judaism says that instinct is exactly right, and gives us simple ways to act on it.

## Why we pray for one another

The Torah's first recorded prayer for another person is Abraham praying for Avimelech and his household, and he was answered. Moses prayed for his sister Miriam in five words: "Please, G-d, heal her, please." Praying for others is woven into Jewish prayer from the beginning.

The Talmud adds a promise: "One who prays for mercy for another, and needs the same thing himself, is answered first" (Bava Kamma 92a). When we set our own worries aside to ask for someone else, Heaven treats us with the same generosity. Chassidic teaching explains that praying for another Jew also connects us to them at the level of the soul, where all of us are one.

## Praying for the sick

**Mi Sheberach.** On days the Torah is read, a special prayer for healing is said for the sick by name. In many communities anyone can ask for a name to be added. You can text the Rabbi the name before Shabbat. See [Mi Sheberach](/learn/mi-sheberach).

**In your own Amidah.** The eighth blessing of the weekday [Amidah](/learn/amidah), *Refaenu* ("Heal us"), has a place to add a personal prayer. Many people pause there and say the name of the person they are praying for, in Hebrew or in their own language. See [talking to G-d in your own words](/learn/talking-to-g-d-in-your-own-words).

**Tehillim.** Saying Psalms for a sick person is one of the oldest Jewish responses to illness. Psalm 20, 121 and 130 are often said, and some people say the verses of Psalm 119 that spell the person's name. You can add a name to our community prayer list at [Tehillim](/tehillim). See the guide to [Tehillim](/learn/tehillim).

**A mitzvah in their merit.** Give [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah), light Shabbat candles a few minutes early, or take on a small mitzvah "in the merit of a complete recovery for ...". A good deed in someone's merit is itself a form of prayer.

## The mother's name

When we pray for someone who is ill, the custom is to use their Hebrew name followed by their mother's name: "Chaim ben (son of) Sarah," "Leah bat (daughter of) Rivkah." For most other purposes, such as being called to the Torah, we use the father's name. The Zohar connects prayers for mercy with the mother, and the sages see a hint in King David's words, "Save the son of Your handmaid" (Psalm 116). If you do not know the mother's Hebrew name, use her English name, or simply pray for the person as you know them. G-d knows who you mean. See [Hebrew names](/learn/hebrew-names).

## Praying for family

You do not need a crisis to pray for the people you love. Many parents say a few words for each child every morning or at candle lighting on Friday night, a moment the Jewish tradition treasures for exactly this. See [blessing your children](/learn/blessing-your-children) and [Shabbat candles](/learn/shabbat-candles). A spouse's name, a parent's health, a brother looking for work, a daughter looking for a match: all of it belongs in the Amidah's blessings for healing, livelihood and peace.

## Praying for the world

Almost every Jewish prayer is in the plural: "Heal *us*," "Bless *us*," "Grant *us* peace." Even alone, we pray as part of the whole Jewish people, and for all of humanity. In times of war or disaster, communities gather to say Tehillim and the Rebbe encouraged adding good deeds, because every mitzvah tips the whole world toward good. When the news is heavy and you feel helpless, open a book of Psalms. It is not nothing; it is one of the most Jewish things you can do.

## Common questions

### Can I pray for someone who is not Jewish?
Yes. Abraham prayed for Avimelech, and we pray for all of humanity. You can say Tehillim for anyone and add any name to your own Amidah. Ask the Rabbi about adding a name to the community's Mi Sheberach.

### What if I do not know the person's Hebrew name?
Use the name you know, in any language. A prayer said with love for "my friend Michael who is in surgery today" is a real prayer. If you can, ask the family for the Hebrew name for the Mi Sheberach.

### Does my prayer help if I am not religious?
Yes. The Talmud's promise about praying for others does not come with conditions. Every sincere prayer rises, and a prayer for someone else is among the most sincere there are. See [why pray](/learn/why-pray).

### How long do we keep praying for someone?
Until they recover, as long as the illness lasts, or, sadly, until they pass away. Community prayer lists are refreshed from time to time; our [prayer list](/tehillim) lets you renew a name so it stays on.
