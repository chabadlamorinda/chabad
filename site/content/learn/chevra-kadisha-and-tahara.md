Tahara (טָהֳרָה, "purification") is the Jewish ritual of washing and dressing a person who has died, done by the Chevra Kadisha (חֶבְרָה קַדִּישָׁא, the "holy society") before burial. The body is cleansed, dressed in plain white shrouds and placed in a simple casket, as an act of deep respect called a *chesed shel emet*, "a true kindness," because it can never be repaid.

## What the Chevra Kadisha is

The Chevra Kadisha is a society of volunteers who care for the deceased. Chabad.org notes that its members usually serve without pay, men caring for men and women for women out of modesty, and that serving is considered a mark of good character. Societies like it go back to Talmudic times and were often among the first institutions founded in a new Jewish community.

The volunteers handle the body with great care, uncovering only what is needed, and they ask the deceased for forgiveness if anything is done that might be disrespectful.

## What happens in a tahara

Chabad.org describes it in simple terms: a gentle cleansing, a ritual washing, dressing the body in shrouds, and the recitation of special prayers asking G-d to lift the soul to eternal rest. It is done in private and with great dignity, and family members are not asked to take part.

## The shrouds and casket

- **Tachrichim (תַּכְרִיכִים), shrouds.** Plain white garments, handmade from linen or muslin. Everyone is dressed alike, rich and poor, which spares families from feeling embarrassed or competitive. They also reflect that the person stands before G-d in purity.
- **A tallit.** A man is also buried in his tallit (prayer shawl). If you have his tallit, give it to the Chevra Kadisha ahead of time. If he has none, the funeral home can usually provide one.
- **A plain casket (aron).** Traditionally simple, unadorned and made of material that returns to the earth, such as wood. Metal caskets are discouraged. Outside Israel, the Chevra Kadisha places earth from the Land of Israel in the casket.

## The shomer: not leaving the body alone

A *shomer* (שׁוֹמֵר, "guard") stays with the body from death until burial, out of respect for the person. It may be a family member, a friend, a Chevra Kadisha member, or a hired person, and people can take shifts. Many shomrim recite Psalms (Tehillim).

## What to do right away

1. **Call Rabbi Labkowski** at 925-384-7242.
2. **Tell the hospital or hospice** that the Chevra Kadisha will care for the body, and ask them to wait for instructions. A Jewish person should accompany the body if it is moved.
3. **Tell the funeral director** you need tahara, a shomer, tachrichim and a plain kosher casket. Chabad.org notes that some funeral homes do not offer tahara unless the family asks.
4. **Avoid embalming, cremation and autopsy** unless the law requires an autopsy; if so, ask the Rabbi at once. Embalming and cremation are against Jewish law.
5. **Plan the funeral soon.** Burial is traditionally as soon as possible, generally within 24 hours, with delay allowed only for the honor of the deceased.

## Even if the family is not observant

Many Jews who do not keep other mitzvahs ask for tahara, and families are right to. It is a gift to the person and a comfort to the family. You do not have to be observant. Please ask, and ask the Rabbi if you are not sure how. See [cremation, a Jewish view](/learn/cremation-jewish-view) for another question families often have.

## Common questions

### What is tahara?
Tahara is the ritual washing and dressing in white shrouds of a person who has died, performed by the Chevra Kadisha before burial. It is a final act of respect.

### What does Chevra Kadisha mean?
Chevra Kadisha means the "holy society," the group of volunteers who prepare a Jewish person for burial. They have existed since Talmudic times.

### What are tachrichim?
Tachrichim are the plain white burial garments, handmade from linen or muslin, in which a Jewish person is buried. Everyone is dressed alike.

### What is a shomer?
A shomer is a "guard," a person who stays with the body from death until burial, so the person is never left alone. It can be a family member, a friend or a hired person.

### Should we ask for a tahara even if we are not religious?
Yes. Tahara is a gift to the person who died and a comfort to the family, and it does not require any previous observance. Call the Rabbi at 925-384-7242 and we will help.

### Can I help with this work?
It is considered an honor to serve, and the volunteers are trained. Ask Rabbi Labkowski at 925-384-7242 about how people learn about it.
