When someone we love is near the end of their life, it can feel like there is nothing left to do. Judaism teaches the opposite: these days are precious, and there is a great deal of love, dignity and holiness we can still give. You do not need to know everything. Call the Rabbi, and we will walk with you.

## Being present is the main thing

The most important gift is your presence. Sit with your loved one, hold their hand, talk to them softly. Hearing is often the last sense to fade, so speak as if they hear every word: tell them you love them, thank them, remember good times together, and ask forgiveness for anything unresolved. Many families take turns so that the person is never alone.

Jewish tradition treats the last hours of life with great respect. Every moment of life has value, until the very end. Speak gently in the room, avoid arguments, and keep the atmosphere calm.

## Saying the Shema and Vidui

Two short prayers are traditionally said near the end of life:

- **Vidui**, a short confession and prayer, asking G-d for forgiveness and for healing, and placing the soul in His hands. It is said by the person if they are able, or by someone at their side. It does not mean giving up hope: the words themselves ask for recovery.
- **The Shema**, "Hear O Israel, the L-rd is our G-d, the L-rd is One." Jews have said these words at the end of life for thousands of years. Saying them with your loved one, or for them, is a beautiful last connection to their people and their soul.

The Rabbi can come and say these prayers with you, or guide you over the phone. If your loved one is not observant or you are not sure what they would want, that is fine: these words belong to every Jew.

## Tehillim and tzedakah

Many families say Tehillim (Psalms) at the bedside, especially chapters 20, 23, 91 and 121, and give tzedakah in the merit of their loved one. See [Tehillim for every occasion](/tehillim).

## Practical matters, ahead of time

If you can, think about these before the last day, so you are not deciding in a rush:

- **A Jewish funeral and burial.** Jewish tradition calls for burial, not cremation, as soon as possible after passing. Let the Rabbi know early, and he will help you arrange a Chevra Kadisha (the sacred burial society) and the funeral.
- **Medical decisions.** Questions about life support, feeding tubes or pain medication can be hard. Jewish law has clear and caring guidance, and every case is different, so ask the Rabbi. A [halachic will or advance directive](/learn/halachic-will) can make your wishes clear.
- **Hospice and hospital staff.** Tell them your family is Jewish and would like the Rabbi called at the end.

## At the moment of passing

When the soul leaves the body, those present may say "Baruch Dayan HaEmet," "Blessed is the true Judge." Close the eyes gently, cover the body with a sheet, and, if possible, open a window. The custom is to stay with the body and not leave it alone, saying Tehillim, until the Chevra Kadisha arrives. Then call the Rabbi: he will help with every next step. See [the Jewish funeral](/learn/jewish-funeral) and [someone passed away](/help/someone-passed-away).

## Take care of yourself too

Watching someone you love decline is exhausting. Eat, rest, accept help from friends, and let yourself cry. You are doing a great mitzvah by being there. The Rebbetzin and the Rabbi are here to talk, any time you need.

## Common questions

### Is it wrong to pray for a peaceful passing?
Jewish tradition always prays for life and healing. At the same time, when a person is suffering, it is permitted to pray for G-d's mercy, without asking for death directly. Every situation is personal, so speak with the Rabbi.

### My loved one is not religious. Should I still say the Shema?
Yes. The Shema belongs to every Jew. You can say it quietly, holding their hand, even if they cannot respond.
