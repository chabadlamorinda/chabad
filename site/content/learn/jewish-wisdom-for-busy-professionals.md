Jewish tradition answers the question of work-life balance with Shabbat: one day each week when we stop working, step back and remember why we work in the first place. Chabad teaching adds that we should do our work with full effort and also with trust in G-d, and that work itself can be a mission, not just a grind.

## Shabbat: the weekly reset

Rivkah Slonim, writing on Chabad.org, describes how for about twenty-five hours each week, from Friday sunset to Saturday night, she does not write, use electricity or the phone, or cook. She explains that the commandment of six days of work and one of rest is a single idea: the workdays give Shabbat its meaning, and Shabbat gives the workdays their purpose. She also writes that being absorbed in work can make it hard to remember our source and our purpose, and that Shabbat is a regular pause to reconnect with our inner selves. Her description of lighting candles and putting the phone away is a familiar picture to many people in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda who feel always "on." Read more in [Shabbat for beginners](/learn/shabbat-for-beginners) and [Shabbat for Lamorinda commuters](/learn/shabbat-for-lamorinda-commuters).

## Prepare during the week

A Chabad.org article for a working professional who wanted to keep Shabbat but could not leave her job early on Fridays makes one main point: prepare for Shabbat during the week. Its suggestions include:

- Baking challah early in the week, or cooking ahead and freezing.
- Using a slow cooker so that a hot meal is ready when you get home.
- Setting the Shabbat table on Thursday night.
- Starting with one or two guests, and keeping it simple.
- Talking to your employer: many people who keep Shabbat arrange to come in earlier, stay later on other days, or make up hours, especially in winter when Shabbat starts early.

## Effort with trust

Chabad.org presents a classic text on trust in G-d which teaches that trust does not mean passivity. A person must still make honest, reasonable effort at livelihood and daily affairs, and rely on G-d for the outcome. For a professional this is a relief: you can give your best, without carrying the idea that everything depends on you alone. For worry that will not let go, read [anxiety and worry](/learn/anxiety-and-worry) and [bitachon](/learn/bitachon).

## Work as a mission

A chapter on making a living, drawn from the Rebbe's teachings, says that money and work are not separate from G-d but a place where hidden spiritual value can be found. In that view, your job is not a distraction from a spiritual life; it can be part of it. Dealing honestly with clients, paying people on time and treating colleagues kindly are all ways of doing this. See [business ethics](/learn/business-ethics) and [parnassah, work and livelihood](/learn/parnassah-work-and-livelihood).

## Common questions

### What does Judaism say about work-life balance?
Judaism builds balance into the week with Shabbat, a day to stop working and reconnect. Chabad teaching adds that we should work with full effort and trust G-d for the result.

### How can I keep Shabbat with a demanding job?
Prepare during the week, set the table ahead and talk with your employer about your schedule. See [telling your boss you keep Shabbat](/learn/telling-your-boss-you-keep-shabbat).

### I cannot do all of Shabbat yet. Is it still worth starting?
Yes. Every mitzvah counts on its own. Lighting candles is a real step. Read [Shabbat candles](/learn/shabbat-candles).

### Is it wrong to work hard?
No. The Torah says to work six days. Chabad teaching sees work as a partnership with G-d in improving the world, as long as it is honest and does not crowd out Shabbat, family and prayer.
