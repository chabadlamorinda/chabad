In December, the stores, the radio and the neighborhood all seem to celebrate someone else's holiday. Many Jewish families feel it, especially families with children. Here is a Jewish way to think about the season, and practical ideas for making it your own.

## Your own light

Chanukah is not "the Jewish Christmas." It has its own meaning, older and different: a small group of Jews refused to give up their faith when a powerful empire pressured them to blend in. The menorah in the window says the same thing today: we are proud of who we are.

That is why the Lubavitcher Rebbe encouraged **public menorah lightings**, in parks, city halls and shopping centers around the world, and why the menorah is placed where people can see it. Chanukah is meant to be shared.

## Ideas for the whole family

- **Make Chanukah big at home.** Light every night together, with every child holding a menorah of their own. Sing, tell the story, play dreidel and give gelt. See [Chanukah for kids](/learn/chanukah-for-kids).
- **Decorate Jewishly.** Put the menorah in the window, hang children's Chanukah art, and add blue and white.
- **Give with meaning.** Connect gifts and gelt to tzedakah and learning; see our [Chanukah gelt](/learn/chanukah-gelt) guide.
- **Come to a public lighting.** Seeing a big menorah in their own town shows children that being Jewish is something to celebrate in public.

## At school and at work

- **Talk to the teacher.** Many teachers welcome a parent who comes in to share Chanukah: a short story, a dreidel game and gelt for the class.
- **Bring Chanukah to the office.** Bring sufganiyot or latkes, or put a menorah on your desk (an electric one where flames are not allowed, as a reminder). See [Chanukah at work and college](/learn/chanukah-at-work-and-college).
- **Answer questions simply.** "We celebrate Chanukah: a holiday about religious freedom and light."

## Respect for our neighbors

Being proud of Chanukah does not mean looking down on anyone. Wishing a neighbor well, thanking a colleague for an invitation and taking part in a community's kindness are good manners. At the same time, Jewish families keep their own traditions and do not take part in another religion's rituals. If you are unsure about a particular invitation, ask the Rabbi.

## Common questions

### Should Jewish children have a Christmas tree?
A tree is a symbol of another religion's holiday, so Jewish homes do not have one. Put that energy into making Chanukah bright and fun. For family situations, talk with the Rabbi.

### How do I explain to my kids why we don't celebrate Christmas?
Keep it positive: "Every family has its own special days. Ours are Shabbat, Chanukah, Passover... and they are wonderful." Then make sure they are.

### Is it okay to say "Merry Christmas" to a neighbor?
Wishing a neighbor well is a kindness. Many people prefer "Happy holidays" or "Wishing you and your family a wonderful season."

### What if Chanukah is early or late, not near Christmas?
Celebrate it whenever it comes. The Jewish calendar follows the moon, so Chanukah can begin anywhere from late November to late December.
