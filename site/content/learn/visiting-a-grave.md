Visiting the grave of a loved one is a meaningful Jewish custom, especially on a yahrzeit and in the weeks before the High Holidays. At the grave we pray, say Psalms, give charity in their memory and leave a small stone as a sign that we were there.

## Why we visit graves

Jewish tradition teaches that the soul has a connection to the place where the body rests. Visiting the grave honors the person who passed away, and the prayers and good deeds we do in their memory bring their soul to higher levels. It also gives the living a quiet place to remember, reflect and feel connected.

Jews have prayed at the graves of the righteous since Biblical times. Caleb is said to have prayed at the Cave of the Patriarchs in Hebron, and people still travel to pray at the resting places of great tzaddikim.

## When to visit

Common times to visit include:

- **The [yahrzeit](/learn/yahrzeit),** the anniversary of the passing on the Hebrew calendar.
- **The end of shiva or [sheloshim](/learn/sheloshim),** according to family custom.
- **The month of Elul** and the days before Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur.
- **An [unveiling](/learn/unveiling),** when the headstone is dedicated.
- **Fast days,** according to some customs.

It is customary not to visit cemeteries on Shabbat, Jewish holidays or Rosh Chodesh (the first day of a Jewish month). Many also avoid visiting the same grave too often, so that grief does not take over daily life.

## What to do at the grave

1. **Approach with respect.** Walk carefully and avoid stepping on graves.
2. **Say Psalms.** It is customary to recite chapters of [Tehillim](/learn/tehillim). A traditional selection includes chapters 33, 16, 17, 72, 91, 104 and 130. Many also say the verses of Psalm 119 that spell out the person's Hebrew name and the word *neshamah* (soul).
3. **Speak from the heart.** Talk to your loved one or to G-d in your own words.
4. **Say Kel Malei Rachamim,** the memorial prayer, and if a minyan is present, [Kaddish](/learn/kaddish).
5. **Give tzedakah** in their memory, before or after the visit.
6. **Place a small stone** on the headstone before leaving.
7. **Wash your hands** after leaving the cemetery. Many pour water over each hand from a cup and do not dry them with a towel.

## Why leave a stone?

Flowers fade, but a stone lasts. Placing a stone shows that someone visited and remembered. It also recalls the ancient practice of marking graves with stones and adds to the monument, in a sense, as a symbol of lasting love.

## Customs at the cemetery

- Men who wear tzitzit usually tuck them in out of respect for the dead, who can no longer do mitzvot.
- Avoid eating, drinking or acting casually in the cemetery.
- Kohanim (descendants of the priestly family) traditionally do not enter a cemetery, except for the funeral of a close relative. Ask the Rabbi if this applies to you.

## Common questions

### What if I cannot travel to the grave?
You can still say Tehillim, give tzedakah and study Torah in your loved one's memory wherever you are. These bring the same merit to the soul.

### Can children come to the cemetery?
Yes, many families bring children. Prepare them for what they will see and keep the visit simple. Some families have different customs, so ask if unsure.

### Do I need a minyan to visit a grave?
No. You can visit alone. A minyan is needed only to say Kaddish.

### Is there a special prayer to say on a yahrzeit visit?
Psalms, the Kel Malei Rachamim prayer and your own words are all fitting. The Rabbi can give you a printed order of prayers.
