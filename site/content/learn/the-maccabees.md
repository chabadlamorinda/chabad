Every Chanukah we thank G-d for "the battles" as well as the miracle of the oil. Who fought them? The Maccabees were a family of priests from a small town who refused to let Judaism be erased, and whose courage, against all odds, gave us the holiday we still celebrate about 2,200 years later.

## The world they lived in

After Alexander the Great, the Land of Israel came under Greek rule, eventually under the Syrian-Greek kings. For a long time Jews could live and worship in peace. Then King Antiochus IV decided to unite his kingdom under one culture, and Judaism stood in his way. He outlawed the three mitzvot that make Jewish time and Jewish life: Shabbat, the sanctifying of the new month, and circumcision. Torah study was banned, Torah scrolls were burned, and an idol was set up in the Holy Temple in Jerusalem.

## Mattityahu and his sons

In the town of Modi'in lived Mattityahu, an elderly Kohen (priest) of the Hasmonean family, with his five sons: Yochanan, Shimon, Yehudah, Elazar and Yonatan. When the king's officers came to Modi'in and ordered the Jews to sacrifice to an idol, Mattityahu refused. A Jew stepped forward to comply, and Mattityahu struck him down together with the king's officer, then called out: "Whoever is for G-d, follow me!" The family fled to the hills, and a revolt began.

## Judah the Maccabee

Mattityahu died within about a year, and leadership passed to his son Yehudah, known as the Maccabee. The name is usually explained as "hammer," for the way he struck the enemy, or as the initials of the verse "Who is like You among the mighty, O G-d." With a few thousand untrained fighters he defeated army after army of trained soldiers, many times their size.

## The rededication of the Temple

After about three years of fighting, the Maccabees retook Jerusalem. They cleared the Temple of idols, built a new altar, and on the 25th of Kislev lit the menorah again. Only one small jar of pure olive oil with the High Priest's seal could be found, enough for one day, and it burned for eight. The word *Chanukah* means "dedication," and the holiday was set for eight days of praise and thanksgiving. ## Yehudit, the heroine of Chanukah

Jewish tradition also remembers a woman. Yehudit, a widow from a besieged town, went to the camp of the enemy general, fed him salty cheese and plenty of wine, and when he fell into a deep sleep, cut off his head. Leaderless, the army fled. Because of her, women have a special place on Chanukah: the custom to rest from work while the candles burn, and the custom of eating dairy foods. See [Chanukah foods](/learn/chanukah-foods) and [Jewish women](/learn/jewish-women).

## What their courage means today

The Maccabees were not fighting for land or power at the start; they were fighting for the right to be Jews. Chassidic teaching points out that the Greeks did not want to destroy the Jews, only Judaism's holiness; they would have been happy with a Torah studied as literature and a Temple without G-d. The Maccabees' answer was the small, pure jar of oil: the part of every Jew that cannot be made impure.

The Rebbe saw in the Maccabees a lesson for every generation. A Jew should never hide a menorah or a mitzvah because the surrounding culture is stronger or more numerous; that is why Chabad lights menorahs in public squares around the world. ## Common questions

### Were the Maccabees religious fanatics?
No. They were ordinary Jews, priests and farmers, pushed to the wall by decrees that made Jewish life a crime. They fought only when the alternative was the end of Judaism.

### Why is the Chanukah story not in the Torah?
The events happened long after the Torah and the Prophets were written. The story is preserved in the Talmud, in the prayers (Al Hanissim) and in the Books of the Maccabees, which were not included in the Hebrew Bible. See [Al Hanissim and Hallel](/learn/al-hanissim-and-hallel).

### How do I tell this story to children?
Keep it simple: the bad king said "no Shabbat, no Torah"; a brave family said "we are Jews"; G-d helped them win; and the little jar of oil lasted eight days. See [Chanukah for kids](/learn/chanukah-for-kids) and [the kids' corner](/kids).
