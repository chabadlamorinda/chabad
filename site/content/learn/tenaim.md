Tenaim (תנאים, "conditions") is the traditional Jewish engagement agreement, in which the two families commit to the wedding date and to sharing the costs of starting the new home. It ends with the mothers of the bride and groom breaking a plate and everyone calling out "Mazal tov!"

## What are tenaim?

An engagement has no legal weight in Jewish law on its own. The tenaim give the couple's promise a formal shape. The document sets a wedding date and the shared costs of the wedding and the household, and may mention gifts the groom will give the bride. It is usually a standard printed form with blanks for the names of the bride, groom, two guarantors (one for each side) and the witnesses.

The agreement is made on behalf of the parents, who take on the financial responsibility. It is signed before two kosher witnesses. In Chabad circles, the text expresses a longing for the Redemption, by saying that the wedding will take place in the rebuilt Jerusalem.

Why have an engagement period at all? It shows that marriage is not an impulsive decision, which is why the Sages required one.

## How are tenaim made?

1. **Writing.** The document is written, ideally under a rabbi's supervision.
2. **The kinyan.** To make the commitments binding, a "handkerchief transaction" (kinyan sudar) is done: one person holds up a garment or cloth, often a kippah, as a token of the commitment. The bride, groom, parents and guarantors each take part in the standard version.
3. **Reading.** The document is traditionally read aloud, and in many communities the rabbi is honored with the reading.
4. **Signing.** Two witnesses sign, and copies are given to the sides.
5. **The plate.** A plate wrapped in a cloth is broken.

Often the groom shares a Torah thought at the celebration, which makes the gathering a *seudat mitzvah*, a festive meal of a mitzvah.

## Why break a plate?

Typically the mothers of the bride and groom break the plate together. There are two explanations:

- **Finality.** Like broken crockery, the agreement cannot be repaired. It marks the commitment as final.
- **Remembering Jerusalem.** It tempers the joy of the moment by recalling the destruction of Jerusalem, just as the groom will break a glass at the end of the wedding.

## When are tenaim done today?

There are two customs. In one, the document is completed and signed at an engagement party, sometimes called a "Tenaim" or "Vort." In the other, only a symbolic kinyan is done at the engagement, and the document is signed on the wedding day, usually at the reception before the chuppah. In that case the plate may be broken twice, once after the kinyan and once at the wedding.

Not every community writes tenaim: In Moroccan Jewish tradition, for example, no engagement contract is written and the commitments are included in the ketubah. For the wedding checklist, tenaim are needed only if you follow the custom. Ask the Rabbi what he recommends for your family.

## Common questions

### What are tenaim in Judaism?

Tenaim are the engagement agreement between the families, setting the wedding date and shared responsibilities, and sealed with a kinyan and a broken plate.

### Who breaks the plate at the tenaim?

Usually the mothers of the bride and groom break the wrapped plate together, to cries of "Mazal tov!"

### Why is a plate broken at an engagement?

It shows that the commitment is final and tempers the joy by recalling Jerusalem's destruction.

### Are tenaim required for a Jewish wedding?

No. They are a custom, and not every community follows it. Ask the Rabbi whether to include them.

### What is the kinyan at the tenaim?

A symbolic exchange, usually holding a kippah or handkerchief, that makes the promises binding.

### Is a plate also broken at the wedding?

At the wedding the groom breaks a glass. If the tenaim are signed on the wedding day, a plate may also be broken at the reception before the chuppah.
