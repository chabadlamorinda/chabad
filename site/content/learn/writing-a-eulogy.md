A eulogy, in Hebrew a *hesped*, is one of the oldest Jewish customs. When Sarah passed away, the Torah says Abraham came "to eulogize Sarah and to weep for her." Three thousand years later we still do the same: before a Jew is laid to rest, someone stands up and says who they were. If that someone is you, this guide will help.

## What a eulogy is for

The sages give a hesped two purposes. The first is honor for the one who passed away (*kavod hamet*): to tell the truth about a life, so that the goodness in it is seen and remembered. The second is to move the people listening: to open their hearts, to let them cry, and to leave them wanting to be a little better because of the person they are burying. A eulogy that does both has done its job, however simple the words.

Chassidic teaching adds a third layer. The soul is present at its own funeral, and the good that is said and the good deeds that people take on in its memory bring it real joy and real elevation. You are not only speaking about the person; in a sense you are speaking for them and to them.

## What to include

- **True stories.** One specific story says more than a list of adjectives. The Shabbat table, the phone calls, the way they treated a waiter, what they did when nobody was watching.
- **Their values.** What did they care about most? Family, honesty, hard work, generosity, laughter, Israel, learning?
- **Their Jewish life,** in whatever form it took: the candles their mother lit, the Seder they ran, the tzedakah they gave quietly, the Yiddish words they used, the pride they felt. Every Jew has a Jewish story; find theirs and tell it.
- **Their name.** Say their Hebrew name if you know it. At a Jewish funeral it matters.
- **A lesson to take home.** End with one thing the people listening can do because of them: call their parents, light Shabbat candles, give to a cause they loved.

The Talmud warns against exaggerating in a eulogy; praise that nobody recognizes helps no one. But it also says to speak of the good generously. Tell the truth, and tell it kindly.

## Length and tone

Three to five minutes is plenty; written out, that is about one page. If several people are speaking, keep yours shorter. The tone is honest and warm: it is fine to smile and even to laugh, and it is fine to cry. Pause when you need to. Nobody at a funeral is judging the delivery.

Write it down and read from the page. Grief makes even confident speakers lose their place, and a written text means your words will be there when you are not sure you can be.

## Who speaks

Usually the Rabbi, who weaves the family's memories into Jewish teaching, and then one or a few family members or close friends. There is no rule that a child must speak; some find it is a comfort, others find it too much, and both are right. A eulogy can also be written by one person and read by another. Jewish custom limits eulogies on certain joyous days of the calendar; the Rabbi will tell you if that applies.

## Common questions

### I am not a good speaker. Can I still give a eulogy?
Yes. A eulogy is not a performance. Write one page of true memories, read it slowly, and stop when you need to. If you truly cannot, write it and ask the Rabbi or a relative to read it for you.

### Should a eulogy be funny?
It can be, if that was who the person was. A loving, funny story that makes people nod and smile through tears honors them well. Just keep the dignity of the moment, and leave out anything that would embarrass them or the family.

### Can I mention their faults?
Lightly and lovingly, if at all. Jewish custom is to focus on the good; a eulogy is not the place to settle accounts. "He was stubborn, and that stubbornness kept this family together" is a eulogy; a list of grievances is not.

### Do eulogies happen at the graveside or in a chapel?
Either, or both. Many funerals have the eulogies at the chapel or synagogue and a shorter service at the grave; some are entirely at the cemetery. The Rabbi will arrange the order with the family.
