The Torah forbids one Jew to lend money to another Jew with interest. This law is called **ribbit** (or "neshech"). It surprises many people, because interest is a normal part of modern finance. But it reflects a beautiful idea: between Jews, a loan is meant to be an act of kindness, not a way to make money from someone's need.

## What the Torah says

The Torah repeats this mitzvah in several places. "If you lend money to My people, to the poor person with you... do not charge him interest" (Exodus 22:24). "Do not take from him interest or increase; fear your G-d, and let your brother live with you" (Leviticus 25:36). The Torah also teaches that lending itself is a mitzvah: helping someone through a hard time with a loan is a high form of [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah).

## Who the prohibition covers

Ribbit applies to both sides of a loan between Jews:

- **The lender** may not charge interest.
- **The borrower** may not pay it, even willingly.
- **Others who help arrange it**, such as a guarantor, a witness or the person writing the loan document, are also involved in the prohibition.

It covers more than a stated interest rate. Our Sages also forbade subtler forms, such as a borrower giving the lender a gift or a special favor because of the loan. These details are many, so ask the Rabbi when a question comes up.

## Why

The Sages explain that ribbit can turn a brother's need into a source of profit for someone else. The Torah wants Jews to see each other as family: when your brother is struggling, you help him stand on his feet, and you do not ask for more back than you gave. Living this way builds trust and caring in a community.

## Heter iska: the business arrangement

Today, Jews often do business with each other, and businesses need capital. For this, the Sages developed the **heter iska** ("business permit"). Instead of a loan with interest, the money is structured as an investment partnership: the investor shares in the profits of the business, under agreed conditions. Many Jewish-owned banks and businesses, including banks in Israel, use a heter iska.

A heter iska must be set up properly, with the right document and terms, and the business arrangement must be real. It is not a formality that fixes any loan after the fact.

**Before lending or borrowing with interest between Jews, including family members, business partners and Jewish-owned companies, speak with a rabbi.** Questions about mortgages, business loans, credit cards and bank accounts can also involve ribbit; ask the Rabbi about your situation.

## The ideal: the free loan

The best way to lend is with no interest at all. A **gemach** (short for gemilut chasadim, "acts of kindness") is a free-loan fund. Jewish communities around the world run gemachs for money, and also for things like wedding dresses, baby equipment and folding chairs. The Rambam lists helping a person become self-reliant, including with a loan, as the highest level of tzedakah.

## Common questions

### Can a Jew charge interest to a non-Jew?
The Torah's prohibition of ribbit applies to loans between Jews. For any specific case, ask the Rabbi.

### Can I lend money to my brother with interest?
Not directly. Family members are included in the prohibition. If there is a business reason, a properly made heter iska may be possible; speak with a rabbi first.

### What is a heter iska?
A business arrangement in which money is invested as a partnership instead of lent with interest. It must be set up correctly; ask a rabbi.

### What is a gemach?
A free-loan fund that lends money or items to people who need them, without interest. It is one of the most beloved forms of kindness in Jewish life.
