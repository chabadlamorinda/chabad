When a child dies, Jewish tradition does not offer quick answers; it offers company. A child is one of the seven relatives for whom Jewish law formally prescribes mourning, so parents are surrounded by the community, by [shiva](/learn/shiva), by the prayers of [Kaddish](/learn/kaddish), and by people who simply come and sit with you.

If this is your loss, you do not have to read this page now. Call or text Rabbi Labkowski at 925-384-7242 or [talk privately with the Rabbi or the Rebbetzin](/help/talk-to-the-rabbi). They know that no words are enough, and they will not try to find them.

## You are allowed to grieve, and to be angry

Chabad.org teaches that crying is needed, and sometimes anger is too. Judaism has never asked a bereaved parent to pretend. We mourn because we cherish life; grief is the measure of how much love there was. There is no schedule you must keep, and no "right" way to feel.

A well-known Midrash tells of Bruriah and Rabbi Meir, whose two sons died suddenly on a Shabbat. Bruriah gently compared them to a treasure lent to them that was now asked back. It is not offered as an explanation, only as one way a grieving mother found to hold her loss. Some parents find comfort in such words, and some are not ready for them. Both are fine.

## What the Jewish laws are

When a child (of any age) passes away, the parents and the child's brothers and sisters are mourners.

- **Before the funeral** the parents are *onenim*, free of other obligations so they can be helped to arrange the burial ([aninut](/learn/aninut)).
- **The funeral** is held as soon as possible, and mourners tear a garment ([kriah](/learn/kriah)).
- **Shiva**, seven days at home, when friends and family come to you. You do not host; you only sit.
- **Sheloshim**, the first thirty days, with some restrictions easing ([sheloshim](/learn/sheloshim)).
- **Kaddish:** For relatives other than parents, Kaddish is traditionally said for thirty days. Who says it and how is a question to bring to the Rabbi, since practices differ and every case is different.

Babies who died very young, stillbirth and miscarriage have their own specific customs. Please call the Rabbi rather than rely on a web page; see also [pregnancy loss](/learn/pregnancy-loss).

## Brothers and sisters

Siblings grieve too, often silently, while everyone worries about the parents. Children under bar or bat mitzvah age are not obligated in formal mourning, but they should not be left out. Tell them the truth in simple words, let them see you cry, keep their routines gentle, and invite them to light a candle or say a Psalm ([Tehillim](/learn/tehillim)) with you. Ask the Rabbi or Rebbetzin how to speak with a child of a particular age.

## How friends can help

- Come, and say very little. "I'm so sorry. I'm here" is enough. See [comforting mourners](/learn/comforting-mourners).
- Bring meals, and keep bringing them after the first week.
- Say the child's name. Parents often fear that the world will forget.
- Remember the dates: the end of sheloshim, the birthday, the [yahrzeit](/learn/yahrzeit).
- Avoid explaining why it happened, or saying "at least..." or "you can have another."

## The year ahead

Grief does not end at the end of shiva. The holidays, the child's birthday and ordinary Tuesdays will each be hard. See [the year of mourning](/learn/year-of-mourning) and [grief during the holidays](/learn/grief-during-the-holidays). Parents who are struggling to carry on, or couples under strain, should not do it alone: ask the Rabbi about support, and speak to your doctor if you need medical or mental health care.

## Common questions

### Do parents sit shiva for a child?
Yes. A child is one of the seven relatives for whom Jewish law prescribes shiva, so the parents sit for seven days. Ask the Rabbi about a very young baby, since special rules apply.

### Do parents say Kaddish for a child?
Kaddish is traditionally said for thirty days for a child, but who says it and how depends on circumstances and custom, so ask the Rabbi to guide you personally.

### What do you say to parents who lost a child?
Say little and sincerely: "I am so sorry. I am here." Do not try to explain or console with reasons. Say the child's name, and keep showing up in the months afterward.

### Why would G-d let a child die?
Judaism does not claim to have the answer, and honest questions, even anger, are welcome. Rabbi Labkowski will not offer easy explanations; he will listen.

### How can I help a grieving sibling?
Be honest in simple words, keep routines gentle, include them in remembering, and let them see that feelings are safe. The Rabbi and Rebbetzin can advise on children of different ages.
