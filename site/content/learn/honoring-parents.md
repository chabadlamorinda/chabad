Honoring your father and mother is one of the Ten Commandments, and Jewish law spells it out in two parts: honor, which means caring for parents, and reverence, which means treating them with respect. The Torah promises long life as its reward.

## Where the mitzvah comes from

The Fifth Commandment says, "Honor your father and your mother, so that your days may be lengthened" (Exodus 20:12). Another verse says, "Every person shall revere his mother and his father" (Leviticus 19:3).

The Talmud teaches that there are three partners in creating a person: G-d, the father and the mother. When we honor our parents, G-d considers it as if we honored Him. That is why this mitzvah is on the first of the two tablets, alongside our duties to G-d. See our guide to the [Ten Commandments](/learn/ten-commandments).

## Honor: kibbud

Honor means actively caring for parents' needs. The Talmud gives examples:

- Providing food, drink and clothing when they need it.
- Helping them get around.
- Doing things for them with a pleasant face and good attitude.

How you help matters as much as what you do. Serving a parent with resentment misses the point.

## Reverence: mora

Reverence means treating parents with dignity. Traditionally, a child should not:

- Sit in a parent's regular seat.
- Contradict a parent in a way that is disrespectful.
- Call a parent by their first name.
- Interrupt them rudely.

## Practical ways to honor parents

1. **Call and visit regularly.**
2. **Speak respectfully**, even when you disagree.
3. **Stand up** when a parent enters the room, as a sign of respect.
4. **Thank them** for what they have done for you.
5. **Ask their advice**, which shows you value them.
6. **Help with tasks** they find difficult.

## Caring for aging parents

As parents age, honoring them can become both more meaningful and more demanding. Jewish law teaches that a child should care for a parent personally when possible, while also recognizing that sometimes professional care is the most loving choice. Caregivers also need to look after their own health.

## After a parent passes away

The mitzvah continues after a parent's lifetime. Children honor them by saying [Kaddish](/learn/kaddish), marking the [yahrzeit](/learn/yahrzeit), giving tzedakah in their memory, and living in a way that brings honor to their name.

## When it is hard

Not every relationship with a parent is simple. Jewish law does not require a person to accept abuse or put themselves in danger. Honoring a parent also never requires breaking a mitzvah. These are sensitive situations, and the Rabbi is here to talk privately.

## Common questions

### Do I have to obey my parents as an adult?
The mitzvah is about honor and respect, not total obedience in all of life's decisions. For example, choosing a spouse or a place to live is generally your own decision. Speak with the Rabbi about specific situations.

### What if my parent asks me to break a mitzvah?
Parents and children are both obligated to G-d, so a parent's request does not override a mitzvah. Respond respectfully and gently.

### Is this mitzvah only for children?
It applies at every age, to sons and daughters alike. A married woman's obligations to her own home may affect what she can do, but she still honors her parents.

### How do I honor a parent who was not a good parent?
This is painful and personal. Some level of respect still applies, but there are limits. Please speak with the Rabbi confidentially.
