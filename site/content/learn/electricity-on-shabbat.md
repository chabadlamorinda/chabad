On Shabbat we do not turn electrical things on or off. Lights stay as they were set before Shabbat, phones and screens are put away, and timers do the switching for us. It sounds like a lot, but most homes need only a few simple steps on Friday afternoon.

## Why electricity is not used on Shabbat

The Torah forbids 39 kinds of creative work on Shabbat, called [melachot](/learn/thirty-nine-melachot), including kindling a fire, cooking and building. Great halachic authorities of the last century discussed exactly which melacha electricity involves; a glowing bulb or heating element is like fire, and completing a circuit is compared to building or finishing something. Whatever the reason in a given case, the accepted practice in every observant community is the same: we do not switch electrical devices on or off on Shabbat.

There is also a deeper side. Shabbat is a day when we stop changing the world and simply enjoy it. Leaving the switches alone is a big part of what makes the day feel calm.

## Lights

- **Decide before Shabbat** which lights stay on: kitchen, hallway, bathroom.
- **Timers set before Shabbat** may turn lights on and off during Shabbat. Many families put bedroom and living room lights on timers.
- **Do not adjust a timer on Shabbat.** If a timer setting goes wrong, ask the Rabbi before touching it.
- **Motion-sensor lights** outside should be turned off or covered before Shabbat.

## The refrigerator

You may open the fridge, but not if a light goes on. Before Shabbat, unscrew the bulb, tape the switch, or use the fridge's Sabbath mode. Ice makers and water dispensers on the door should also be turned off.

## Hot water and heating

- **An urn** filled and plugged in before Shabbat gives hot water for tea and coffee all day.
- **Hot water from the tap** can cause the water heater to heat new water. Many people avoid it on Shabbat; ask the Rabbi about your system.
- **Heating and air conditioning** can run on a thermostat or timer set before Shabbat.

## Shabbat-mode appliances

Many ovens, fridges and warming drawers have a "Sabbath mode." These modes differ from model to model, and rabbis evaluate each one differently. Some are fine for certain uses and some are not. Before relying on one, ask the Rabbi about your specific appliance.

## Phones and screens

Phones, computers and TVs are not used on Shabbat. Many people switch them off, or put them in a drawer, before candle lighting (see [muktzeh](/learn/muktzeh)). For many families this turns out to be the best part of Shabbat. In a real emergency that may endanger life, call for help: saving a life overrides Shabbat ([pikuach nefesh](/learn/pikuach-nefesh)).

## Keeping it simple for beginners

If you are new to Shabbat, you do not need to do everything at once. A good start is to light [Shabbat candles](/learn/shabbat-candles), put the phone away for Friday dinner, and leave the lights you need on. Then add one step at a time. See [Shabbat for beginners](/learn/shabbat-for-beginners).

## Common questions

### Can I leave the lights on all Shabbat?
Yes. Leaving lights on, or setting timers before Shabbat, is the normal practice. What we avoid is switching them on or off during Shabbat.

### Can I use a timer on Shabbat?
A timer set before Shabbat may switch lights and appliances during Shabbat. Changing the timer during Shabbat is a different question; ask the Rabbi.

### Is Sabbath mode on my oven okay?
It depends on the model and how you plan to use it. Ask the Rabbi before relying on it.

### What if a light was left off by mistake?
Leave it off. Work around it, and next week add it to your Friday checklist. For a safety concern, such as a dark staircase, ask the Rabbi.
