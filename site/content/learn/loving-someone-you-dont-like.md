You can love a person and still dislike what they do, because the Tanya says the two feelings aim at different things. Dislike is aimed at the wrong in the deed; love is aimed at the hidden G-dly spark in the person, which is the same as yours.

Everyone has someone like this: a relative, a coworker, a neighbor, a person in the community. Chapter 32 and Chapter 30 of the [Tanya](/learn/tanya) give a clear way to think about it.

## Love and dislike can sit side by side

Chabad.org's Tanya class on Chapter 32 begins with the puzzle. The Torah commands us to love every Jew, yet the Talmud speaks of hating a sinner. Can a person do both?

The Alter Rebbe, Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi, says yes, because the two feelings have different objects. What we reject is the evil. What we love is the divine spark inside the person, the G-dly soul that gives them life. Both are real, and neither cancels the other.

## The "hatred" in the Talmud is very narrow

The chapter is careful here. It says the duty to hate applies only in a rare case: a true equal in Torah and mitzvot, who sinned deliberately, was already warned, and did not repent. For everyone else, including those who are far from Torah or never learned, the Tanya says the opposite: draw them close with love, as Hillel advised, being a disciple of Aaron who loved peace and loved people.

Even in that rare case the love remains. The chapter adds that compassion for the soul, trapped inside a person who is behaving badly, softens the hatred and awakens love.

So if you simply find someone annoying, rude or hard to be around, the Tanya is not asking you to feel dislike as a duty. It asks you to look under the surface.

## "Do not judge until you stand in his place"

Chapter 30 of the Tanya builds on the saying of the Sages in Pirkei Avot 2:4: "Do not judge your fellow until you have stood in his place." The Alter Rebbe uses it to explain how a person can honestly regard himself as lower than others. He says a person's surroundings and nature shape the pull of temptation, and you cannot know how strong someone else's struggle is. The chapter suggests that each of us measure our own effort against the struggle another person faces.

That is a humble way to look at someone who disappoints us. We do not know all they carry. See [judging others favorably](/learn/judging-others-favorably).

## Ways to put this into practice

These are questions that follow from the Tanya's teaching, not extra rules:

1. **What is the soul in this person doing?** Think of one good quality or one hard thing they are going through.
2. **What would it be like to stand in their place?** Even a minute of this changes the tone of a conversation.
3. **What can I say that is kind and true?** Avoid [lashon hara](/learn/lashon-hara), speech that harms.
4. **Can I forgive?** See [Tanya on forgiving others](/learn/tanya-on-forgiving-others).

## Love does not mean staying in harm's way

Loving someone's soul never means accepting abuse, or staying near a person who hurts you or your children. It is right to keep your distance and to protect yourself. ## Common questions

### How do I love someone I don't like in Judaism?
Look past the behavior to the G-dly soul, which Tanya says all Jews share, and judge them as you would want to be judged. You do not have to like what they do.

### Can you love and hate the same person?
Tanya chapter 32 says yes, because love is for the divine spark and dislike is for the wrong deed. It also says the hatred it speaks of applies only in a narrow case.

### Does Judaism say I must love people who hurt me?
Judaism asks you to see the soul and to avoid revenge, not to stay in danger. For painful situations, ask the Rabbi.

### What does "stand in his place" mean in Tanya?
It comes from Pirkei Avot 2:4. Chapter 30 uses it to remind us we cannot know another person's struggle.

### Is this only about Jews?
Chapter 32 is about love among Jews. Judaism also teaches kindness toward all people; see [chesed](/learn/chesed).
