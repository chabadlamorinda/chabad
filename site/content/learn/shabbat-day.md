Friday night gets most of the attention: candles, challah, a family dinner. But Shabbat continues for a whole day, and the day has a beauty of its own: unhurried prayer, time with family and friends, a long lunch, a walk, a nap, a good book. Here is how a Shabbat day unfolds.

## Morning: prayers and the Torah reading

Shabbat morning prayers are longer and more festive than on weekdays, with songs and the special Shabbat Amidah. The highlight is the **Torah reading**: the Torah scroll is carried around the synagogue, the weekly portion is read from the scroll, and people are called up for an *aliyah*. A short reading from the Prophets, the *haftarah*, follows. See [the Torah reading](/learn/torah-reading) and [this week's Torah portion](/learn/weekly-parsha).

Many synagogues add a short talk on the portion and a special time for children. You do not need to know the prayers to come; just follow along, stand when others stand, and enjoy.

## The Kiddush

After services, the community gathers for **Kiddush**: the blessing over wine, then food and conversation. In many communities, including ours, it is a real meal. It is the friendliest moment of the week: newcomers meet old-timers, children run around, and people catch up. See [Kiddush](/learn/kiddush) and [sponsoring a Kiddush](/learn/sponsoring-a-kiddush).

## The Shabbat lunch

At home, the second Shabbat meal begins with Kiddush over wine and Hamotzi over two challahs, just like Friday night. Many families serve a hot dish kept warm since Friday, like [cholent](/learn/cholent). Sing a few [zemirot](/learn/zemirot), share a thought from the Torah portion, and linger. There is nowhere else to be.

## The afternoon: rest, learning and family

The Shabbat afternoon is a gift of time. Take a walk, visit friends, play a board game with the children, read, or take a Shabbat nap, which the sages call a delight. Many people study a little Torah or read [Pirkei Avot](/learn/pirkei-avot), which is traditionally read on summer Shabbat afternoons. Since we refrain from creative work on Shabbat, the day is free from screens, errands and email. See [the 39 categories of work](/learn/thirty-nine-melachot).

## Mincha and the third meal

Late in the afternoon comes Mincha, the afternoon prayer, with a short Torah reading from next week's portion. Then the **third meal**, *seudah shlishit*: often lighter, with bread, songs and words of Torah as the sun goes down. Chassidic teaching calls it a time of special closeness to G-d, as Shabbat gently takes its leave.

## Havdalah: saying goodbye

When three stars appear, Shabbat ends with **Havdalah**: a blessing over wine, fragrant spices and a braided candle, separating the holy day from the week ahead. See [Havdalah](/learn/havdalah) and the exact time on [Shabbat times](/shabbat-times). Some continue with a Saturday night meal, the [Melave Malka](/learn/melave-malka), to escort Shabbat out.

## Common questions

### Do I have to come to services for the whole morning?
No. Come for whatever part you can; many people come for the Torah reading and stay for the Kiddush. You are always welcome.

### What should I wear to synagogue on Shabbat?
Something a little nicer than weekday clothes, as for any special occasion. Men usually wear a kippah, which the synagogue has for you. See [your first time at synagogue](/learn/first-time-at-synagogue).

### What can I do on Shabbat afternoon with children?
Walks, visits, board games, puzzles, stories, a picnic in the backyard and a Shabbat nap. See [Shabbat with kids](/learn/shabbat-with-kids).

### When does Shabbat end?
About 40 to 50 minutes after sunset, when three stars appear. The exact time for Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda is on our [Shabbat times](/shabbat-times) page.
