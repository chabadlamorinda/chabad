An ethical will is a letter in which you pass on your values, wisdom, blessings and hopes to your children and loved ones. A legal will passes on property; an ethical will passes on who you are.

## What is an ethical will?

In Hebrew it is called a *tzava'ah*, meaning "instruction" or "testament." It is not a legal document. It is a personal message, written in your own words, telling the people you love what matters most to you and what you hope they will carry forward.

People often write one later in life, but there is no need to wait. Many write one when a child is born, before a major surgery, at a milestone birthday, or simply when they feel moved.

## Roots in the Torah

The tradition is as old as the Jewish people:

- **Jacob** gathered his twelve sons before he passed away and gave each one a blessing and guidance (Genesis 49).
- **Moses** blessed each of the tribes of Israel at the end of his life (Deuteronomy 33).
- **King David** gave his son Solomon instructions to walk in G-d's ways (I Kings 2).

Through the centuries, rabbis and ordinary parents wrote letters to their children urging them to study Torah, act with kindness, keep peace in the family and stay loyal to their faith. Many of these letters are still treasured today.

## Why write one

- **Your values outlive you.** Your children will remember your words long after possessions are gone.
- **It brings clarity.** Writing forces you to think about what really matters.
- **It can heal.** It is a chance to express love, gratitude and forgiveness that may be hard to say aloud.
- **It connects generations.** Grandchildren and great-grandchildren can come to know you.

## How to write an ethical will, step by step

1. **Reflect.** Think about the lessons life has taught you, the people who shaped you, and the moments you are most grateful for.
2. **Share your story.** Tell your family where you came from: your parents, grandparents and Jewish roots.
3. **Name your values.** Explain what you hope they will hold onto, such as honesty, kindness, family, Shabbat, [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah) or Torah study, and why.
4. **Give blessings.** Write a personal blessing for each child or grandchild.
5. **Express love and forgiveness.** Say thank you, and if needed, ask for or offer forgiveness.
6. **Make requests gently.** For example, ask that Kaddish be said for you, that your [yahrzeit](/learn/yahrzeit) be marked, or that tzedakah be given in your memory. Stating that you want a traditional Jewish burial helps your family honor your wishes.
7. **Keep it and share it.** Store it with your important papers. Some share it while they are alive; others leave it to be read later.

## Your legal will and Jewish law

Jewish law has its own rules about inheritance. Many families want their legal will to respect both civil law and Jewish law. Speak with the Rabbi and a qualified attorney to make sure your wishes are clear and properly arranged.

## Common questions

### Does an ethical will need to be long?
No. A single heartfelt page is enough. Write in the language and style that feels natural to you.

### Can I write one even if I am not very observant?
Absolutely. Your values, your story and your love are worth passing on, whatever your level of observance.

### Can I update my ethical will?
Yes. Many people revise it every few years as life changes. Date each version.

### Should I include funeral wishes?
It helps a great deal. Writing that you want a Jewish funeral and burial spares your family difficult decisions. See our page on [planning a Jewish funeral](/help/planning-a-jewish-funeral).
