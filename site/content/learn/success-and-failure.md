We live in a culture that celebrates winning and hides losing. Judaism takes both seriously, and sees both differently. Success is a gift and a responsibility. Failure is not the end of the story; often it is where the real story starts.

## Do your part, and trust G-d with the results

The Torah promises, "G-d will bless you in all that you do." The Sages read it closely: in all that *you do*. We are asked to work hard, plan well and give our best, and to remember that the outcome is in G-d's hands. That frees us from two traps: thinking every success is only our own doing, and thinking every failure means we are worthless. See [bitachon](/learn/bitachon), trust in G-d.

## When things go well

The Torah warns about a person who prospers and says, "My strength and the might of my hand made me this wealth." The Jewish response to success is [gratitude](/learn/hakarat-hatov) and generosity: thanking G-d, giving [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah) from what we earned, and using our success to help others. [Humility](/learn/humility) is not pretending we did nothing; it is knowing where our talents came from.

## When things fall apart

- **"A righteous person falls seven times and rises"** (Proverbs 24:16). The verse does not describe someone who never falls. It describes someone who keeps getting up, and perhaps becomes righteous through it.
- **A descent for the sake of an ascent.** Chassidic teaching explains that many setbacks are a step down that makes a higher step possible, like a person crouching before a jump.
- **Every failure can be repaired.** [Teshuvah](/learn/teshuvah) means returning: whatever went wrong, a person can learn, make amends and begin again. "Nothing stands in the way of teshuvah."
- **Your worth is not your results.** In G-d's eyes, a person is measured by effort and intention. The Sages said, "According to the effort is the reward."

## Measuring a life well

At the end of life, the Talmud says, a person is asked: Were you honest in business? Did you set times for Torah? Did you hope for redemption? It does not ask how much you earned or how many times you won. A Jewish life is measured by goodness, learning and faith, and those are open to everyone.

## Common questions

### Is it Jewish to be ambitious?
Yes, when ambition is used for good: providing for a family, building a business honestly, growing in learning and kindness. Ambition only becomes a problem when it pushes out what matters most.

### How do I start again after a big failure?
Take one small step. Talk it through with someone you trust, take a lesson from it, and do one good thing today. The Rabbi is glad to help you think it through.

### Is a setback a punishment?
Not necessarily, and we should never assume it about someone else. Judaism encourages asking "What can I learn from this?" rather than "Why is this happening to me?" See [why bad things happen](/learn/why-bad-things-happen).
