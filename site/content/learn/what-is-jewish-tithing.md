Jewish tithing is called maaser kesafim (מעשר כספים), setting aside a tenth of your income for tzedakah, charity. In Jewish practice the common custom is to give 10 percent of what you earn, and it is a gift of gratitude that many families in Lafayette, Moraga, Orinda and across the East Bay keep every month.

## What does "maaser" mean?

Maaser (מעשר) comes from the Hebrew word for ten. Maaser kesafim means "the tenth of money." Chabad.org describes it as a structured system: a set part of your income, usually ten percent, that you separate and later give out.

This is different from tzedakah in general. The Torah commands tzedakah with no fixed percentage; you give when asked, within reasonable limits. Maaser gives that giving a steady shape. You decide the amount ahead of time, and then you look for the best places to give it. To read more about tzedakah itself, see [Tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah) and [Maaser: Giving a Tenth to Charity](/learn/maaser).

## Where does Jewish tithing come from?

According to Chabad.org, tithing first appears in the Torah as something our forefathers did. Abraham gave a tenth of the spoils after a battle, and Jacob vowed at Beth El that of all that G-d would give him, he would give a tenth. The Midrash also says that Isaac tithed his produce.

Later, the Torah set out several tithes of the harvest in the Land of Israel, such as the tithe for the Levites and the tithe for the poor. Those laws are tied to the Land and its farming.

Maaser kesafim, the tenth of money, grew out of this. Chabad.org explains that its exact source is debated. A few authorities see it as coming from the Torah. Most see it as a rabbinic rule or a long-standing custom, and a custom that is widely accepted can become as binding as a vow. Most people follow that view. Everyone agrees on one point: every Jew should give at least some tzedakah.

## Who gives maaser?

The simple answer is: everyone who earns income and can afford it. Chabad.org notes that because most people do not separate tithes from produce, everyone should tithe their earnings.

Your own basic needs come first. If you cannot cover food, clothing and shelter, you are not obligated to give maaser, though you should still give what you can. Chabad.org adds that the Rebbe encouraged giving a partial amount where a full tenth is not possible. There is more in [Giving When Money Is Tight](/learn/giving-when-money-is-tight).

## Why a tenth, and why more is praised

A tenth is the standard measure. Chabad.org quotes Maimonides: the most desirable way to give is a fifth of one's resources, a tenth is an ordinary measure, and giving less shows stinginess. Giving up to a fifth (chomesh) is praised if you can afford it, and halacha generally sets a fifth as the upper limit for giving.

## Why many Jews keep it

Many people find that maaser brings clarity. Money is no longer a daily argument ("Should I give? How much?"). The decision is made once, and the giving is calm and regular. Jewish teaching also sees our money as a trust from G-d, and tithing is a way of saying thank you. Chabad teaches that maaser is Hashem's promise of success: the Talmud reads the Torah's words as "give a tenth so that you will become rich," and the Rabbi teaches that this is a promise about maaser. Read more in [The Blessing of Giving Maaser](/learn/the-blessing-of-giving-maaser).

## Common questions

### What is maaser kesafim in simple words?
It is the Jewish practice of setting aside ten percent of your income and giving it to tzedakah. Maaser means "tenth" and kesafim means "money."

### Is Jewish tithing required?
Tzedakah is a Torah obligation. Chabad.org explains that most authorities see the tenth of money as a rabbinic rule or a binding custom, and most Jews who keep it do so as a firm practice. For your personal situation, ask the Rabbi.

### Where does the idea of giving a tenth come from?
From Abraham, who gave a tenth of the spoils, and from Jacob, who vowed to give a tenth of all that G-d gave him. The Torah also sets tithes on the harvest in Israel.

### Do I give a tenth of everything I receive?
In general maaser applies to income, as explained in [How to Calculate Maaser](/learn/how-to-calculate-maaser). Details such as taxes, gifts and bonuses are covered there, and for your own case, ask the Rabbi.

### Can I give more than ten percent?
Yes. Giving up to a fifth is considered commendable if you can afford it. Giving beyond a fifth is generally not advised.

### Is it only for wealthy people?
No. Chabad.org notes that the question of who must give depends on ability, and that everyone gives something. Start with what is realistic for you.
