Chabad's menorah campaign began on the 19th and 20th of Kislev in the winter of 1973, when the Lubavitcher Rebbe, Rabbi Menachem M. Schneerson, asked that every Jewish home have a menorah to light on all eight nights, and that menorahs be given to those who had none. Within a few years, large menorahs were rising in city squares across the world, and today they stand in public places from Berlin to Buenos Aires.

Before that winter, Chanukah was mostly a private, indoor holiday. Here is how it changed.

## The Rebbe's call

At a gathering (a *farbrengen*) in December 1973, the Rebbe called for a new effort: every Jewish home should light the menorah on all eight nights, menorahs should be given to people who lack one, and children should receive Chanukah gelt and a menorah of their own. Chabad.org places the call on the 20th of Kislev, December 15, 1973, and says it was first announced the day before.

The reasoning is simple. The menorah is meant to shine out toward the street, so that passersby see it. The Rebbe asked that it be placed where it can be seen. Learn more about the Rebbe and his campaigns in [The Rebbe](/learn/the-rebbe) and [Ten Mitzvah Campaigns](/learn/ten-mitzvah-campaigns). The Rebbe also asked that menorahs be lit at Israeli army bases in the days after the 1973 war.

## Tin menorahs and two-by-fours

Within two days, the Lubavitch Youth Organization found a metal stamper to make an initial run of 10,000 tin menorahs. The stamper, Tibor Kupferstein, a Holocaust survivor from Budapest, stopped his work on a military contract and ran his factory around the clock. By the end of that Chanukah about 60,000 menorahs had been made, and all were given away. Volunteers handed them out on streets and in stores, and some were shipped to Los Angeles and Miami.

In Midtown Manhattan, Rabbi Shmuel Lipsker and student friends stood at Fifth Avenue and 47th Street. They built a simple menorah from two-by-fours on a cinder-block base and lit it at 5 p.m. A large crowd gathered. Public menorah lightings were not yet a common idea.

## From a street corner to the world

- **1974:** A Chabad rabbi lit a public menorah outside Independence Hall in Philadelphia.
- **1975:** Chabad of San Francisco and a music promoter, Bill Graham, built a 25-foot mahogany menorah for Union Square. It is called the Bill Graham Menorah.
- **1979:** The President of the United States lit Chabad's giant menorah outside the White House. It later became known as the National Menorah.
- **Today:** Chabad raises giant menorahs in public squares around the world, and each year it sends out more than 700,000 menorah kits and 2.5 million Chanukah guides, in person and by mail.

Some accounts name different "firsts" among these early lightings, so we call them among the first. If you want to dig into the details, ask the Rabbi.

## Why the street?

The idea is rooted in the mitzvah itself: to "publicize the miracle." A public menorah turns the holiday outward, so that a Jew who has never lit one, or who passes by on the way home, feels a spark. The Chabad-style menorah has straight arms, like the one in the Temple. See our guide to [public menorah lightings](/learn/public-menorah-lighting) for the reasons and customs.

## Common questions

### When did Chabad's menorah campaign start?
It began in December 1973, when the Rebbe, Rabbi Menachem M. Schneerson, asked that every Jewish home light a menorah and that menorahs be given to those without one.

### Where was the first Chabad public menorah?
Chabad.org tells of a simple wooden menorah on Fifth Avenue in Manhattan in 1973, a public menorah at Independence Hall in Philadelphia in 1974, and a giant one in San Francisco in 1975. Accounts differ on which was first, so ask the Rabbi if you would like more.

### Why does Chabad give out menorahs?
So that every Jewish family can light the menorah on all eight nights, and so the miracle of Chanukah is publicized. The Rebbe asked that menorahs be given to those who lack one.

### Who lit the menorah at the White House?
Chabad.org records that in 1979 President Jimmy Carter lit Chabad's giant menorah outside the White House.

### Why is a Chabad menorah built with straight arms?
Chabad-style menorahs follow the shape of the Temple's menorah, with straight arms. For the full explanation, read our guide to the [Chanukah menorah](/learn/chanukah-menorah).
