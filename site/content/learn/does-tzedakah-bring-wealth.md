Judaism teaches that giving tzedakah, charity, does not cause poverty, and that those who faithfully give a tenth are promised blessing in return. Chabad.org explains that this is a teaching about trust in G-d, not a business deal and not a guarantee for any one person's bank account.

## What the Torah and the Talmud say

Chabad.org notes that the Torah promises that giving tzedakah will not leave a person poorer, and that one who faithfully gives *maaser* (מַעֲשֵׂר, a tenth of income) is assured of being repaid. It adds that Solomon taught that giving to the poor is like lending to G-d, who repays in this world with added returns.

This idea is unusual. The Torah normally forbids testing G-d (Deuteronomy 6:16). But with the tithe, the Talmud (Taanit 9a), as Chabad.org presents it, says it is permitted: give the tenth, and see whether blessing follows. The prophet Malachi (3:10) says it in G-d's own voice, "Try it and see."

## What this does not mean

Read carefully, none of these sources says that giving is a way to get rich. Here is what to keep in mind:

- **It is not a transaction.** The word tzedakah means "justice," not "charity." Our money is entrusted to us by G-d so that we can share it (see [Tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah)). We give because it is right, and the blessing is a gift on top.
- **Blessing is not only money.** Chabad.org lists protection, blessings, life and the Divine Presence among what giving brings. Blessing can mean health, peace, family and the sense of enough.
- **It is never a promise.** If someone is struggling financially, or ill, it is not because they gave too little. Judaism does not claim to predict how blessing will unfold for each person. For a personal question, speak with the Rabbi.

## Giving when money is tight

Chabad.org explains that basic needs come first: food, clothing and shelter. Someone who cannot cover them is not required to give maaser. Even then, a person should give what they can in tzedakah. The Rebbe encouraged giving a partial amount, such as five percent, and keeping a record of what is still owed, to be made up later. More ideas are in [Giving when money is tight](/learn/giving-when-money-is-tight).

## Common questions

### Does giving charity make you rich in Judaism?
No. Judaism teaches that giving does not cause poverty and brings blessing, but it never promises wealth. Blessing is understood broadly, and giving remains a mitzvah whether or not we see it.

### What does "test Me" in Malachi mean?
In Malachi 3:10, G-d invites the Jewish people to bring the tithe and see whether He will pour out blessing. Chabad.org teaches that this is the one place where the Torah permits putting a promise of G-d to the test.

### Is maaser, giving ten percent, required by Jewish law?
Chabad.org explains that authorities differ on whether maaser kesafim is a Torah law, a rabbinic law or a custom held as strongly as a vow. Most follow the latter view. Ask the Rabbi how it applies to you.

### Should I give with the hope of becoming wealthy?
Give because it is the right thing to do and because the money was entrusted to you. Blessing may come, but the mitzvah stands on its own. For more on this, read [What giving does for the giver](/learn/what-giving-does-for-the-giver).

### What if I cannot afford to give a tenth?
Give what you can, even a little, and keep a record of what remains. Basic needs come first. The Rabbi can help you think it through, confidentially.

### Can I give maaser to a synagogue or Jewish organization?
Chabad.org lists communal institutions such as the synagogue among the permitted uses of maaser. Ask the Rabbi about specific cases, such as honors or dinners.
