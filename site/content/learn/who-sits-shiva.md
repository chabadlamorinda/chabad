In Jewish law, seven relatives sit shiva: a father, a mother, a husband or wife, a son, a daughter, a brother and a sister. Half siblings count too. Grandchildren, in-laws and stepfamily are not obligated, but there are meaningful ways for them to take part.

## The seven mourners

The list comes from the Torah (Leviticus 21:1 to 3) and is explained on Chabad.org. You mourn formally for:

1. Your father
2. Your mother
3. Your husband or wife
4. Your son
5. Your daughter (married or not)
6. Your brother
7. Your sister

Half-brothers and half-sisters, whether through the father or the mother, are included. If you are one of the seven, you are a mourner and may sit [shiva](/learn/shiva), tear [kriah](/learn/kriah), and observe [sheloshim](/learn/sheloshim) and [the year of mourning](/learn/year-of-mourning) where they apply.

## What about everyone else?

**Grandchildren, grandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins.** They are not among the seven, so they are not formal mourners. They are welcome to visit the shiva house, say Psalms, give tzedakah, study Torah in the person's memory, and light a [yahrzeit candle](/learn/yahrzeit-candle). Many grandchildren feel the loss deeply, and these are real ways to honor it. If you wish to do more, ask the Rabbi.

**In-laws.** A husband or wife does not formally mourn for a spouse's parents, but many sit with their spouse and share the house of mourning. Ask the Rabbi about any custom you want to take on.

**Stepparents and stepchildren.** They are not in the list of seven. Many people loved a stepparent as a parent, and the feeling of loss is real. Please ask the Rabbi what is appropriate for you, since there are customs of respect and sometimes of Kaddish. See [blended families](/learn/blended-families).

**Adopted relatives.** According to Chabad.org there is no formal obligation to mourn adoptive parents, siblings or children, but "sympathetic mourning," refraining from public rejoicing, is recommended. Ask the Rabbi about your situation.

## Children and minors

Boys under 13 and girls under 12 are not obligated in shiva or other mourning customs. Still, their clothes should be torn, and they should be encouraged to limit their regular activities, especially older minors. Keep them close, and speak to the Rabbi about what suits a child's age.

## Who says Kaddish?

[Kaddish](/learn/kaddish) is traditionally said by those who are mourners under Jewish law, and by others who volunteer to say it in someone's memory. Customs differ, especially for women, for stepchildren and for adoptive children. Ask the Rabbi. For a convert mourning non-Jewish parents, Chabad.org advises that Kaddish may be said if desired, but that a Psalm or learning Torah in their honor is preferable.

## The outer circle of support

You do not have to be one of the seven to matter. Friends, neighbors and colleagues form the circle that holds a mourner: visiting ([comforting mourners](/learn/comforting-mourners)), bringing meals, and making up a minyan so that Kaddish can be said. ## Common questions

### Do grandchildren sit shiva?
No, grandchildren are not among the seven relatives who sit shiva. They are welcome to visit, learn Torah or say Psalms in memory of the grandparent, and to ask the Rabbi about any added custom.

### Do you sit shiva for a half sibling?
Yes. Chabad.org states that half-brothers and half-sisters are included among those for whom one mourns.

### Do you sit shiva for a stepparent?
A stepparent is not among the seven relatives, but many people observe some mourning customs out of respect. Ask the Rabbi what is right in your case.

### Do you sit shiva for a spouse's parent?
Not formally, but you may sit with your husband or wife and support them. Ask the Rabbi about specific customs.

### Do children sit shiva?
Boys under 13 and girls under 12 are not obligated, though clothing is torn and they are guided gently. Ask the Rabbi about each child.

### Who says Kaddish?
Mourners among the seven relatives traditionally say Kaddish, and others may say it in memory. Customs vary, so ask the Rabbi.
