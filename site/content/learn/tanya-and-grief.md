When a loved one dies, the Tanya's answer is not an argument but a letter: the Alter Rebbe wrote words of condolence to people in mourning, teaching that the spiritual life of a righteous person does not end, and that what he gave to those close to him lives on in them. Tanya's guidance is spiritual comfort. It does not replace the time grief needs.

## The Tanya has letters of comfort

The Tanya, written by Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi, the Alter Rebbe, founder of Chabad, has three parts. The third, the Iggeret HaKodesh ("holy letter"), is a collection of letters he sent to his followers and friends, and two of them are letters of condolence. See [what is the Tanya](/learn/tanya) for the book as a whole.

- **Epistle 27** was written to his Chassidim after the death of his mentor, Rabbi Menachem Mendel of Vitebsk.
- **Epistle 28** was written to Rabbi Levi Yitzchak of Berditchev after the death of his son, Rabbi Meir.

We tell what the letters say, in our own words. Both letters are about the passing of a tzaddik, a righteous person. They are not a general book on grief, and they do not say that mourners should feel better quickly.

## What Epistle 27 teaches

The Alter Rebbe explains the saying of the Sages that a tzaddik "left life for all the living." A tzaddik's real life is spiritual: faith, awe and love of G-d. When he passes, he leaves these qualities as a legacy to those connected with him, and they can receive even more than while he was alive. His soul rises to higher levels, yet something of it remains close, and his teachings go on producing good deeds and growth in later generations.

The letter ends with a call to give tzedakah, and especially to support Rabbi Menachem Mendel's family. That is a Jewish instinct many mourners know: to turn loss into giving. See [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah).

## What Epistle 28 teaches

To a father who lost his son, the Alter Rebbe writes that the passing of a righteous person draws down a special light into the world, comparing it to the Red Heifer, which purified. The tzaddik's hidden work in life, such as his prayer and his Torah study, is described as revealed in its full effect after he passes. The loss is not made smaller; the letter finds in it a spiritual gift for the whole generation.

If these ideas feel far from your own grief, that is fine. Take what helps. The letters speak of a tzaddik, yet many mourners find comfort in their idea that what a person gave goes on in those he touched. Ask the Rabbi how this speaks to your own loss.

## Ways to carry their memory

These are well-known Jewish ways to honor a loved one, not teachings from the two letters. Ask the Rabbi what fits your family.

- **Say Kaddish.** Chabad of Lamorinda can arrange Kaddish for a loved one. See [Kaddish](/learn/kaddish) and the form at /kaddish.
- **Give in their memory.** Epistle 27 closes with tzedakah. See [yahrzeit](/learn/yahrzeit) for the yearly remembrance.
- **Learn something in their merit,** a page of Tanya, a Psalm or a mitzvah.
- **Tell their story** to the next generation.

Grief takes time. Tanya's guidance is spiritual, and counseling and grief support are also right and good when you need them. Ask the Rabbi about your own situation.

## Common questions

### What does the Tanya say about death and grief?
The Tanya has letters of condolence (Epistles 27 and 28 of the Iggeret HaKodesh). They teach that a righteous person's spiritual life continues, that their teachings and example live on, and that we should respond with faith and good deeds. They are not a manual for the stages of grief.

### Does Tanya say we should not cry?
No such instruction appears in the pages we read. Jewish mourning is full of crying and sitting with loss. See [shiva](/learn/shiva) and [coping with grief](/learn/coping-with-grief-jewish-wisdom).

### What happens to the soul after death in Chassidic teaching?
Epistle 27 describes the soul of a tzaddik rising to higher levels while something of it remains close and continues to influence this world. For the wider Jewish teaching, see [the afterlife](/learn/afterlife) and [the soul](/learn/the-soul).

### Is it right to do something in memory of the person who died?
Yes. Say Kaddish, give tzedakah and learn in their merit. Epistle 27 itself closes with a call to tzedakah. Call Rabbi Labkowski at 925-384-7242 to ask how Chabad of Lamorinda can help.

### Can the Tanya help if I also need professional support?
Yes, the two go together. Tanya's guidance is spiritual, and grief counseling is also right when needed. Ask the Rabbi if you would like help finding support.
