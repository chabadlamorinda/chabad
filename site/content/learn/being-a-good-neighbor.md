In Pirkei Avot, Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai asked his students to find the best path in life. One said "a good friend," another "a good neighbor." A good neighbor makes daily life sweeter, and being one is a mitzvah we can practice every day, right outside our door.

## Kindness close to home

"Love your fellow as yourself" begins with the people closest to us. Bring a meal when a neighbor has a new baby, an illness or a loss. Take in the mail when they travel. Shovel the walk or carry groceries for an older neighbor. Small things build trust and warmth.

## Welcome the new family

Abraham kept his tent open on all sides to welcome guests. When someone moves in, knock with a plate of cookies or a challah, share your number, and tell them about the neighborhood. If they are Jewish, invite them for Shabbat. See [welcoming guests](/learn/hachnasat-orchim).

## Respect and boundaries

Jewish law cares about the ways we affect each other: noise late at night, trees that hang over, where water runs, privacy and windows. The Talmud discusses all of these, teaching that what we do on our own property must not harm a neighbor. Being thoughtful avoids most problems before they begin.

## Keeping the peace

When a problem does come up, talk face to face, calmly and early, before it grows. Assume good intentions. If it becomes a real dispute, see [neighbor disputes](/learn/neighbor-disputes), or talk with the Rabbi.

## Being a light

How we treat our neighbors, Jewish or not, is how they come to know Judaism. Every kindness is a [kiddush Hashem](/learn/kiddush-hashem), making G-d's name beloved. A menorah in the window and a warm hello go a long way.

## Common questions

### Should I invite my non-Jewish neighbors to a Jewish celebration?
Yes, warmly. Many neighbors love joining a Chanukah party or a Sukkot visit. It builds friendship and understanding.

### My neighbor is difficult. What does Judaism say?
Stay kind, set clear and polite boundaries, and look for ways to make peace. Talk with the Rabbi if it becomes hard.
