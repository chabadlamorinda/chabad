Unetaneh Tokef (וּנְתַנֶּה תֹּקֶף, "Let us speak of the power of this day's holiness") is a solemn prayer of Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur that says our fate for the coming year is written on Rosh Hashanah and sealed on Yom Kippur. It lists the ways a year may unfold, including "who will live and who will die", and ends with the hopeful line that repentance, prayer and charity can soften the decree.

## What the prayer says

The prayer describes G-d as a shepherd counting His flock as the sheep pass beneath His staff, one by one. Every person, rich or poor, young or old, passes before Him. It then lists what the year might hold: who will live and who will die, who will be at peace and who will be unsettled, who will be rich and who will be poor.

Many people find this frightening on first reading. Chassidic teaching invites a gentler way to hear it. The Day of Judgment is also a day when G-d is close, and the judge is a loving Father. The list is real, but it is not the end of the prayer.

## The line that changes everything

After the list comes the answer, in three words: *teshuvah, tefillah, utzedakah*, repentance, prayer and charity, "avert the severity of the decree."

These three are things we can actually do:

1. **[Teshuvah](/learn/teshuvah)** (תשובה, "return"): honestly turn back to who we are meant to be, and decide to do better.
2. **Tefillah** (תפילה, prayer): speak to G-d directly, in any language, and ask.
3. **[Tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah)** (צדקה, charity and justice): give generously to others, and in doing so, open the way for G-d to give to us.

So the prayer is really a call to action. The year is not fixed in stone; what we do now matters.

## The story of Rabbi Amnon

The tradition tells of Rabbi Amnon of Mainz (Mayence), a respected scholar whom the local duke admired and consulted. When courtiers pressured the duke to make Amnon give up his faith, Amnon refused. According to the story, after he was severely punished he asked to be brought to the synagogue on Rosh Hashanah, and there he recited this prayer, and died as he said the words "He is our G-d, and there is no other."

The story is recorded in the *Ohr Zarua*, a book written in the thirteenth century. Chabad.org notes that some details are questioned, for example that the prayer seems to be older than Rabbi Amnon. The story may well be true at its core, but not every detail can be confirmed. Whatever its origin, it has moved Jews for centuries. If you have questions about the history, ask the Rabbi.

## How to experience it if you read little Hebrew

You do not need to follow every word. Here are some ways in:

- Read the English translation in your machzor (holiday prayer book) beforehand, even for five minutes.
- Listen to the melody. Many communities sing this prayer with a famous tune that carries the emotion without words.
- Pause at the line about teshuvah, tefillah and tzedakah, and silently choose one thing to do this year.
- Bring the children. A child hearing the congregation hush and then sing together will remember it.

## Common questions

### What is Unetaneh Tokef?
Unetaneh Tokef is a prayer in the Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur services that describes G-d judging every person and ends by saying that repentance, prayer and charity avert the harsh decree. It is recited during the repetition of the Musaf prayer.

### What does "who will live and who will die" mean?
It means that Rosh Hashanah is a day of judgment on which the year ahead is decided. Jewish teaching stresses that it is a day of hope, because G-d is a merciful Father and our actions can change the outcome. Ask the Rabbi if this is on your mind.

### Who was Rabbi Amnon of Mainz?
He is the scholar in the traditional story said to have recited Unetaneh Tokef as he died, refusing to give up his faith. The account comes from the thirteenth-century *Ohr Zarua*; some of its details are debated.

### Who wrote Unetaneh Tokef?
Tradition connects it to Rabbi Amnon, but historians note that the prayer appears to be older than the story. The author is therefore not certain.

### What are teshuvah, tefillah and tzedakah?
They are repentance, prayer and charity, the three steps the prayer says can change a harsh decree. See our guides on [teshuvah](/learn/teshuvah) and [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah).
