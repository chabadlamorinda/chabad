A kosher school lunch is simple when you decide what kind it is: a meat lunch, a dairy lunch, or a pareve lunch (neither meat nor dairy). Keep meat and dairy apart, use foods with a reliable kosher symbol, and teach your child to say a blessing before eating.

## Pick one: meat, dairy or pareve

As the guide on [meat and milk](/learn/meat-and-milk) explains, every kosher food is meat, dairy or pareve. Pareve foods are neither, such as fruits, vegetables, grains, eggs and fish, and they can be eaten with either (fish, though, is not eaten together with meat). For a lunchbox, the easiest rule is to make the whole lunch fit one category.

- **A pareve lunch** is the simplest: fruit, vegetables, bread or crackers with a pareve spread, eggs.
- **A dairy lunch** might include cheese, yogurt or milk, alongside bread, fruit and vegetables.
- **A meat lunch** might include chicken or meat from a kosher source (see [kosher meat](/learn/kosher-meat)).

These are ideas, not rulings. For a particular product, the question is whether it carries a reliable symbol. Learn the symbols in [kosher symbols](/learn/kosher-symbols), and watch for the "D" or "dairy" mark next to the symbol.

## Waiting between meat and dairy

Jewish law teaches that very young children do not need to wait between meat and milk, but it is best to feed them something else in between. For children from age three to five, one hour is enough. Children age six and older should generally wait six hours, and up to age nine one may be lenient and wait one hour if necessary. Every child is different: ask the Rabbi about yours.

## School rules and allergies

Your school may have its own rules about what can be brought, especially about allergens. Follow the school's rules, and let the school know about your child's needs. If an allergy or health need conflicts with a kosher question, ask the Rabbi. Also see [pikuach nefesh](/learn/pikuach-nefesh).

## Save money

A lunchbox of fruit, vegetables and dry goods is inexpensive. For tips, see [keeping kosher on a budget](/learn/kosher-on-a-budget).

## Blessings

Before your child eats, they say a blessing. Children can learn to say these blessings from a young age. A simple blessings card in the lunchbox can help. See [teaching kids the blessings on food](/learn/teaching-kids-blessings-on-food).

## Common questions

### What is a kosher lunch for a child?
It is a lunch made from kosher foods that does not mix meat and dairy. The easiest way is to make the whole lunch meat, dairy or pareve.

### How long should my child wait between meat and milk?
Jewish law says: very young children need no wait but should be given something in between, ages three to five wait one hour, and age six and older generally wait six hours (with leniency to one hour up to age nine if necessary). Ask the Rabbi about your child.

### Can my child eat cheese after a chicken sandwich?
Not right away. The waiting time depends on age, as above.

### Are fruits and vegetables kosher in a lunchbox?
Yes, fresh fruits and vegetables are kosher, but some need checking for insects. See [checking for insects](/learn/checking-for-insects).

### What if my child's school provides lunch?
School meals are a different question. Ask the Rabbi what to do for your family.

### Should my child say a blessing at school?
Yes, a brief blessing before eating is a beautiful habit. The guide on blessings explains which blessing to say.
