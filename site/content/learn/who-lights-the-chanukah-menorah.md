Both men and women are obligated in the mitzvah of lighting the Chanukah menorah, and every Jewish household should light. How the family divides the lighting differs from home to home, so if you are unsure, ask the Rabbi.

## Everyone is part of the mitzvah

Jewish law says that men and women alike are obligated in Chanukah lights, because women too were part of the miracle. Everyone in the home takes part, either by lighting or by being present and answering "Amen" to the blessings. See [women and Chanukah](/learn/women-and-chanukah) for more.

## Two common ways families do it

Chabad.org describes two patterns:

- **One person lights for the household.** The head of the household lights while the others listen to the blessings and answer Amen.
- **Each person lights their own menorah.** Ashkenazi families commonly have a menorah for each person, and some Sephardic communities use one per household.

Both are valid, and your family's custom is what counts. If you are starting out, a simple way is for each child and adult to light their own, which brings out the joy of the holiday.

## Married women

Chabad.org notes that many married women do not light their own menorah but take part in their husband's lighting. A woman who lives on her own lights her own menorah. Women also have the custom of not working while the lights burn. Details of this custom are in [women and Chanukah](/learn/women-and-chanukah). If you are unsure about your situation, ask the Rabbi.

## Children

In many families children light their own menorahs, and it is important that everyone be present and involved. Children love this mitzvah, and it is one of the best ways for them to connect to Judaism. For ideas, see [Chanukah for kids](/learn/chanukah-for-kids) and the [kids' corner](/kids). Customs on how young children take part, and whether girls light their own, differ, so ask the Rabbi what your family should do.

## Students, guests and travelers

Light wherever you will sleep that night. A student in a dorm or apartment lights there. A guest can be included in the host's lighting, or light their own, depending on the family's custom. Read [Chanukah away from home](/learn/chanukah-away-from-home).

## Lighting in synagogue and at public events

At the synagogue the cantor lights with the blessings, and congregants still light at home. The same goes for a public lighting: you take part, and then light at home as well. A public lighting is a joyful way to be with others; see [public menorah lighting](/learn/public-menorah-lighting).

## Common questions

### Who should light the Chanukah menorah?
Every Jewish man and woman is obligated, either by lighting or by being included in a household lighting. How it is done depends on your family's custom.

### Does every family member light a menorah?
In many families yes, especially in Ashkenazi homes, and in others one person lights for everyone. Both are accepted. Ask the Rabbi what fits your home.

### Does a wife light her own menorah?
Many married women take part in their husband's lighting rather than lighting separately, while a woman living alone lights her own. Ask the Rabbi about your family's custom.

### Should children light their own menorah?
In many families children do, and it is a wonderful way to involve them. Customs differ on the details, so ask the Rabbi.

### What if I am a student away from home?
Light in your own room or apartment where you sleep. Read [Chanukah away from home](/learn/chanukah-away-from-home), and call us if you are near Lamorinda.

### What if I light at synagogue or a public event?
You still light at home, since the mitzvah is to light in the home.
