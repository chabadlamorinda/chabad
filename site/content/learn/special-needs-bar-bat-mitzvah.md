Every Jewish child becomes a bar mitzvah at 13 (a boy) or a bat mitzvah at 12 (a girl), including a child with autism, Down syndrome or other needs. The milestone is a change of status, not a performance: the child now counts in the community and is responsible for mitzvos according to their ability.

A day can and should be shaped to fit your child. Rabbi Labkowski will plan it with you privately, and nothing here is a rule you must meet.

## What a bar or bat mitzvah really is

"Bar mitzvah" means "son of the commandment" and "bat mitzvah" "daughter of the commandment." A child reaches this age on their Jewish birthday whether or not there is a party ([bar mitzvah](/learn/bar-mitzvah), [bat mitzvah](/learn/bat-mitzvah)). No test or speech is required. The ceremony and celebration come from love and joy.

## Adapting the day

Here are the kinds of adaptations families have used, according to stories on Chabad.org and to what the Rabbi can arrange:

- **A short, meaningful goal.** A girl with Down syndrome in Peabody, Massachusetts, learned the Shema and lit Shabbat candles with the blessing, and said the blessing over bread at a Kiddush the next day. The family and community described it as sweet, intimate and joyful.
- **Tefillin** for a boy, put on with help, if comfortable ([tefillin](/learn/tefillin)).
- **An aliyah** for the child, or the father called up, with the words said line by line.
- **A familiar time and place,** a quieter room, and a break if needed.
- **A sensory-friendly party:** fewer people, less noise, and a space for rest.

Chabad.org also shows other stories, such as an autistic boy in Munich, whose Chabad community celebrated him. Every child is different, so the Rabbi will start with your child's strengths.

## How to start

1. Call the Rabbi and tell him about your child. Everything is private.
2. Decide what feels meaningful and possible: a blessing, a word of Torah, a mitzvah project.
3. Practice a little at a time, with patience, at home.
4. Choose a date and a calm setting. See [the timeline](/learn/bar-bat-mitzvah-timeline) and [mitzvah projects](/learn/bat-mitzvah-projects).
5. Invite those who love your child.

## For parents and siblings

Parents often carry worry and, sometimes, grief. You are not alone. Ask the Rabbi or Rebbetzin to talk and, if you wish, to arrange support from other families. Siblings may want a role too, such as reading a blessing.

## Common questions

### Can a child with autism have a bar mitzvah?
Yes. A Jewish child becomes bar or bat mitzvah at the age of 13 or 12 regardless of ability, and the celebration can be adapted to the child.

### Does a child with special needs have to read from the Torah?
No. Nothing in Jewish law requires a particular performance. A Shema, a blessing or an aliyah can be enough, and the Rabbi can advise.

### Can a nonverbal child have a bar mitzvah?
Yes. Others can say words with the child, and the child can take part with gestures, music or silence. Ask the Rabbi to plan it together.

### What about a child with Down syndrome and a bat mitzvah?
A bat mitzvah is just as meaningful, and Chabad.org shares a story of one celebrated with the Shema, candle lighting and Hamotzi.

### Who is the day for?
For the child, and for the family and community who love them.
