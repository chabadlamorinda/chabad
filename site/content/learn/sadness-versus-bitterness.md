In Tanya, sadness (atzvut, עַצְבוּת) is a heavy, stuck feeling that drains energy, while bitterness (merirut, מְרִירוּת) is honest, active regret over a mistake that breaks the heart open at a set time and leads to change and then to joy. Tanya wants us to avoid the first and to use the second carefully and briefly.

This is one of the most practical ideas in the book. It answers a question many people ask: "Is it healthy to feel bad about what I did?"

## The same feeling, two very different results

Everyone who has done something wrong knows the pull to feel awful. Tanya, in Chapter 26, says that sadness itself is a hindrance. A person weighed down by it becomes sluggish and finds it harder to do anything good.

But the chapter also says there is a regret that is useful. It speaks of "genuine bitterness" and a broken heart, at suitable times. Chassidic teaching contrasts this active remorse with passive depression. The first moves a person to act; the second leaves him stuck.

## What makes regret healthy

From Chapter 26, a healthy regret has these marks:

- **It has a set time.** It is not allowed to take over the whole day, and it is not for the middle of business or other activity.
- **It is honest.** It looks directly at what went wrong, in the light of G-d's greatness, and does not make excuses.
- **It ends.** After the set time, Tanya says to put the sadness away completely.
- **It leads to joy.** The chapter says that the joy after remorse is deeper, like light that comes out of darkness.

## Trusting that you are forgiven

The last step is the key. Tanya teaches that G-d pardons abundantly, including when a person has made the same mistake more than once, since G-d forgives far more readily than people do. Believing that you can start again is the joy that follows regret. This is the heart of [teshuvah](/learn/teshuvah), the Jewish way of returning and beginning anew.

If the hardest person to forgive is yourself, see [forgiving yourself](/learn/forgiving-yourself).

## A simple way to practice

Tanya does not give a bedtime schedule in this chapter, but it does say to choose calm, fixed times. Many people find it natural to set aside a few quiet minutes at a time of their own choosing, ask "Where did I fall short?", say sincerely that they are sorry and what they will do differently, and then end it. Our guide to [Jewish self-accounting](/learn/cheshbon-hanefesh) describes this practice.

## Not a substitute for care

Tanya's guidance is spiritual. Persistent guilt, hopelessness or heaviness that does not lift can be signs of a condition that needs professional help, and seeking it is also right when needed. Ask the Rabbi about a personal situation. See [mental health in Judaism](/learn/mental-health-jewish-view).

## Common questions

### What is the difference between sadness and bitterness in Tanya?
Sadness (atzvut) is a heavy, stuck mood that Tanya wants us to avoid. Bitterness (merirut) is active, honest regret at a fixed time, which leads to change and then to joy.

### Is it healthy to feel guilty in Judaism?
A brief, honest regret that leads to change is welcome. Staying in guilt is not, because it keeps a person from moving forward. Teshuvah is meant to end in a fresh start.

### How long should regret last?
Tanya speaks of set times, not of a number of minutes. After the time you set, put the sadness away. If it will not leave, ask the Rabbi and consider professional help as well.

### How do I know G-d forgives me?
Tanya says G-d pardons abundantly, even for repeated mistakes. Teshuvah, with real regret and a decision to change, is always open. Ask the Rabbi if you want to talk it through.

### What if I feel bad all the time, not just about one thing?
That is not the focused regret Tanya describes. It may need professional care, and seeking it is right. Tanya's guidance is spiritual and goes alongside such help.
