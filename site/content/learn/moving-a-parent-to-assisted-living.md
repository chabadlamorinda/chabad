Moving a parent to assisted living is not automatically a failure to honor them: in Jewish law the mitzvah of honoring parents (kibbud av va'em, כִּבּוּד אָב וָאֵם) is about how you serve and respect them, and Chabad.org notes that when personal caregiving becomes too hard, a child may hire help. Because every family's situation is different, ask the Rabbi about your own case, and bring your parent's needs, your health and your family's limits to that conversation.

## Why it feels so painful

Many adult children in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda feel guilt when a parent can no longer live safely alone. You may worry that you are "giving up" on someone who gave you everything. That feeling shows how much you care. It is also worth knowing that the Torah's commandment to honor parents is one of the Ten Commandments, and Jewish teaching takes it very seriously. So it is right to ask a rabbi, and not just to decide alone.

## What honoring parents means in practice

According to a Chabad.org article on the subject, the mitzvah has two parts: honor and respect. Honor is active: serving and helping parents however possible and whenever necessary, including things like feeding, dressing and getting them where they need to go. If parents can afford these needs themselves, the child is not required to pay, and the article adds that parents usually feel better supporting themselves. Respect is about how we speak and act: not contradicting a parent to their face, and disagreeing privately and politely. The article also says that a parent may waive their own honor, and that if caregiving becomes too hard, a child may hire help.

The article does not address assisted living by name. So whether a move is right, and how to handle questions that come up, is a matter for the Rabbi, who can think it through with you. Read more in [honoring parents](/learn/honoring-parents) and [caring for aging parents](/learn/caring-for-aging-parents).

## Visiting, not just paying

The Lubavitcher Rebbe taught that society wastes the wisdom of older people, and he criticized children who make short, dutiful visits and think a yearly gift settles their duty. Instead, he urged children to honor their parents now and to seek their advice, since the Fifth Commandment links honoring parents with lengthened days. A move to assisted living can be a chance to put this into practice: visit, call, ask for your parent's opinion and stories, and keep them part of the family's decisions and celebrations.

## Common questions

### Is it a sin to put a parent in assisted living?
No one can answer this for every case. Jewish law asks us to serve and respect our parents, and Chabad.org says a child may hire help when caregiving becomes too hard. Please ask the Rabbi about your particular situation.

### Do I have to take care of my parent myself?
The mitzvah of honor means making sure a parent is cared for, including feeding, dressing and transportation. A child may hire help when personal caregiving is too hard. Ask the Rabbi how this applies to your family.

### Who should pay for my parent's care?
According to Chabad.org, a child is not obligated to pay for what a parent can afford themselves. Questions about money and care are specific to each family, so ask the Rabbi.

### How can I feel less guilty?
Staying close helps: visit often, listen, and include your parent in family life. Speaking with the Rabbi can also bring peace of mind. Talking with a counselor is a good idea too.

### Can a parent still keep Shabbat and holidays after a move?
Often yes, in small ways. See [Jewish life in senior living](/learn/jewish-life-in-senior-living-lamorinda), and ask the Rabbi about candles and kosher food.
