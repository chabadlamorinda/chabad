The Cave of Machpelah (Me'arat HaMachpelah, מְעָרַת הַמַּכְפֵּלָה), also called the Cave of the Patriarchs, is the ancient burial place in Hebron where, according to the Torah, Abraham and Sarah, Isaac and Rebecca, and Jacob and Leah are buried. Jewish tradition adds that Adam and Eve rest there too. It is the first piece of land in the Land of Israel that Abraham acquired.

## Why "Machpelah"?

Machpelah means "doubled." There are two explanations: either the cave had two levels, or it is the resting place of four couples. The Torah also calls Hebron Kiryat Arba, "the city of four," and one traditional explanation of that name is the four couples buried there.

## How Abraham bought the cave

When Sarah passed away at the age of 127, Abraham came to Hebron to mourn her and to find a place to bury her. The Torah tells the story at length in the portion called *Chayei Sarah*. Ephron, the owner of the cave, offered it to Abraham as a gift. Abraham insisted on paying, and bought the cave and its field for 400 shekels of silver. It is called the first plot of land in the Holy Land legally owned by the Jewish people.

Read about Abraham and Sarah in [Abraham and Sarah](/learn/abraham-and-sarah).

## Who is buried there

- **Abraham and Sarah**
- **Isaac and Rebecca**
- **Jacob and Leah**
- **Adam and Eve**, according to the tradition of the Zohar

Rachel is not buried in the cave but on the road to Bethlehem (see [Rachel's Tomb](/learn/rachels-tomb)). Jacob, who passed away in Egypt, asked his son Joseph to bring him back to be buried in Machpelah, and Joseph did. To meet the Matriarchs and what they mean to us, see [The Matriarchs](/learn/the-matriarchs).

## The Zohar's secret: a gateway to Eden

The Zohar teaches that the cave is a gateway to the Garden of Eden. According to this tradition, Adam saw a ray of light coming from the spot, and he and Eve were buried there. A Midrash tells that Abraham, while running after a calf he wanted to serve to his three guests, found the cave and recognized the graves of Adam and Eve.

## A place of prayer through the ages

- **Caleb**, one of the twelve scouts Moses sent to the Land, went to Hebron to pray at the graves of the Patriarchs, according to the Talmud.
- **Herod**, in the first century before the common era, built the large stone enclosure that still stands over the cave.
- **Maimonides** and the traveler Benjamin of Tudela both wrote that they visited and prayed there in the twelfth century.
- **The Chabad tradition.** Rabbi DovBer, the second Chabad Rebbe, taught that although prayers rise through Jerusalem, they first go by way of Hebron on their way to G-d.
- **Shabbat Chayei Sarah.** Each year, when the Torah reading tells of Abraham's purchase, many people come to Hebron for Shabbat.

Access to the cave itself was closed around the 1490s and remains closed; visitors pray in the building above it.

## Visiting today

If you are thinking of visiting Hebron, check current travel guidance from your government and a trusted guide, and plan with care. It is customary to pray at the resting places of the Patriarchs and Matriarchs and to ask them to plead for us in Heaven. Those who cannot go can pray from where they are.

## Common questions

### Who is buried in the Cave of Machpelah?
Abraham and Sarah, Isaac and Rebecca, and Jacob and Leah. Jewish tradition says Adam and Eve are buried there too. Rachel is buried elsewhere, near Bethlehem.

### What does Machpelah mean?
It means "doubled." Either the cave had two levels or four couples are buried there.

### How did Abraham buy the Cave of Machpelah?
He bought it, together with its field, from Ephron the Hittite for 400 shekels of silver, even though Ephron offered it as a gift. The purchase is told in the Torah portion Chayei Sarah.

### Can I pray at the Cave of the Patriarchs?
Yes, Jews have prayed there for many centuries. Check current travel guidance before you go, and ask the Rabbi for a name to remember in your prayers.

### Can I pray for my family from home?
Yes. You can say [Tehillim](/learn/tehillim) wherever you are, and ask the Rabbi about the custom of praying in the merit of our ancestors.
