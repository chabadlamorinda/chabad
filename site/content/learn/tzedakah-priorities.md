There are more good causes than any of us can support. Jewish law does not leave us guessing. It gives a clear, loving order of priorities, and it cares just as much about **how** we give as **to whom**. See [Tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah) for the basics.

## Closest first

The Torah says, "lend to My people, to the poor among you" (Exodus 22:24). The Talmud (Bava Metzia 71a) learns from this an order of priorities, and the Code of Jewish Law (Yoreh De'ah 251) spells it out:

1. **Your own basic needs** and those of your household.
2. **Your family:** parents in need, then children, siblings and other relatives.
3. **Your neighbors and your local community.**
4. **The poor of your city** before the poor of other cities.
5. **The poor of the Land of Israel**, who have a special priority over those of other lands.

This is not selfishness. It is responsibility. If everyone takes care of those closest to them, everyone is cared for. And a priority is not an exclusion: most people give to several circles at once.

Some causes, such as Torah education and saving lives, have special importance too. When choices are hard, ask the Rabbi.

## Giving with dignity

The Rambam teaches that whoever gives tzedakah with a sour face, or makes the poor person feel ashamed, loses the merit of the gift even if they gave a lot. Give with a kind word and a smile. A kind word with no money is also a gift; money given with a harsh word hurts.

## The eight levels of giving

The [Rambam](/learn/rambam) lists eight levels of tzedakah, from highest to lowest (Laws of Gifts to the Poor 10:7-14):

1. **Helping a person become self-sufficient:** a job, a loan or a partnership so they no longer need help.
2. Giving where **neither** the giver nor the receiver knows the other.
3. The giver knows, but the receiver does not know who gave.
4. The receiver knows, but the giver does not know who received.
5. Giving **before** being asked.
6. Giving **after** being asked.
7. Giving less than you should, but pleasantly.
8. Giving grudgingly.

Even the lowest level is still tzedakah. Start wherever you are, and climb.

## Giving to non-Jews

The Talmud teaches that we support the poor of other nations along with the Jewish poor, "for the sake of peace" (**darchei shalom**, Gittin 61a). Kindness to every person is part of Jewish life and a [Kiddush Hashem](/learn/kiddush-hashem).

## How much?

The custom is to give a tenth of one's income, called [maaser](/learn/maaser). The Sages advised not giving more than a fifth, so that a person does not become needy. Many keep a [pushka](/learn/pushka) at home and give a little every weekday.

## Common questions

### Should I give to my own synagogue before a big national charity?
Generally, your local community comes first, because you and your neighbors depend on it. Many people give to both.

### Is it better to give a lot once or a little often?
The Rambam writes that giving many small gifts trains the heart in generosity more than one large gift. Both are good.

### Does supporting a family member count as tzedakah?
Yes. Helping relatives in need is tzedakah, and comes first.
