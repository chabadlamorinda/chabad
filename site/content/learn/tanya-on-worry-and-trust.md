Tanya teaches that worry loosens its grip when we remember that G-d gives us life at every moment and that nothing truly evil comes from Above. In Epistle 11 of the Iggeret HaKodesh, the Alter Rebbe writes that everything is good, even when its goodness is too deep for us to see. This is a way of trusting, not a way of pretending.

## Where this comes from

The Tanya was written by Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi, the Alter Rebbe, the founder of Chabad. Its third section, the Iggeret HaKodesh ("the holy letter"), is a collection of letters he sent to his followers. Epistle 11 is one of them, and it deals with exactly what keeps many of us awake at night: what if things go wrong?

You can read it for yourself in the Tanya, in print or online. Other Tanya guides on this site, such as [what is the Tanya](/learn/tanya), explain the book as a whole.

## What Epistle 11 says, in simple words

Here are the main ideas, in our own words.

- **G-d creates the world anew every moment.** The letter says that creation is not something that happened once and ended. Life flows to us constantly from G-d's wisdom, which is beyond what any mind can grasp.
- **Nothing evil comes from Above.** What looks like suffering seems that way because we cannot see its source. The letter says that in truth everything is good, but its goodness is too lofty to be seen. Sometimes the good is "clothed" in pain until it is revealed.
- **Faith means knowing G-d is everywhere.** The letter describes the purpose of faith as knowing that G-d is present everywhere and that life comes from Him.
- **Sadness shows what we are missing.** The Alter Rebbe explains that grief and complaint come from feeling that some good is lacking, which is why Chassidic teaching works so hard to move us toward joy.
- **The letter is written to each person.** It speaks in the singular. Chassidic teaching notes that this shows the level it describes is meant to be reachable by every Jew, because it relies on a faith hidden in each of us.

The letter also sets a very high ideal, asking us to want even life's basics only for their spiritual purpose. Most of us will not live there all day. Take the direction, not the pressure.

## A true story: reading Epistle 11 through a quarantine

A Chassidic story tells of Reb Asher Grossman of Nikolayev, a shochet (ritual slaughterer) whom the Rebbe Rashab chose to proofread the Tanya for printing errors. His friend Rav Meir Shlomo Yanovsky, the city's rabbi and the Lubavitcher Rebbe's maternal grandfather, caught typhus in an outbreak. The sick were quarantined outside the city, and many lost hope.

Reb Asher came every day and stood beneath his friend's window. For thirty days he read Epistle 11 aloud, not knowing whether Rav Meir Shlomo could hear. Rav Meir Shlomo recovered, and he later credited those readings with keeping him strong and hopeful.

## What to do with worry, step by step

These are suggestions in the spirit of Tanya, not instructions from the text.

1. **Name the worry.** Say it plainly, to yourself or to someone you trust.
2. **Do your part.** Trust does not mean doing nothing. See [bitachon](/learn/bitachon) on the balance of effort and trust.
3. **Remind yourself where life comes from.** A short line such as "G-d is giving me life right now" can steady the mind.
4. **Say Modeh Ani in the morning** or a few words of thanks at night. See [anxiety and worry](/learn/anxiety-and-worry) for more steps.
5. **Reach out.** Call a friend, a counselor or the Rabbi.

## Common questions

### How can I stop worrying from a Jewish point of view?
Judaism teaches us to do our part and then trust that G-d is guiding the result. Tanya adds that G-d gives life at every moment and that nothing evil truly comes from Above. Worry is human, so be patient with yourself. See [bitachon](/learn/bitachon).

### Does Tanya say that everything that happens is good?
In Epistle 11, the Alter Rebbe teaches that nothing evil descends from Above and that all is good, though sometimes too high for us to see. He does not ask us to like pain or to deny it. For a personal situation, ask the Rabbi.

### Is it a sin to worry?
Worry is a normal human reaction. Tanya's guidance is an invitation to a calmer faith, not a reason to feel guilty. If you feel worse for worrying, that is a sign to be gentle with yourself and to talk to someone.

### What if my worry is more than ordinary worry?
Tanya's guidance is spiritual. If anxiety is constant or overwhelming, professional help is also the right thing to seek, and Judaism encourages it. The Rabbi can also help you find the right support.

### Which part of the Tanya is Epistle 11 in?
It is in the Iggeret HaKodesh, the third section of the Tanya. It is a short letter and is available online with an English translation.
