Phones, tablets and computers are part of almost every moment of our lives. Judaism does not ask us to run away from them. It asks a better question: is this tool serving me and my purpose, or am I serving it?

## Technology is a gift that can serve G-d

The Lubavitcher Rebbe taught that every new discovery in the world is meant, in the end, to serve G-d and make the world a better place. The same radio waves and internet that can spread noise can also spread Torah, kindness and connection. The Rebbe himself encouraged using satellite broadcasts, and later the internet, to bring Jewish learning to people everywhere.

Our Sages also teach that we can learn a lesson from everything. Chassidic teachings draw some from technology: the telephone teaches that what we say here is heard there; the camera, that our actions are recorded; the internet, that one small act can reach the whole world in an instant.

So technology is not good or bad on its own. Like fire, it can cook a meal or burn down a house. It all depends on how we use it.

## The real challenges

Most of us already feel the downsides:

- **Distraction.** Our attention is precious. Notifications pull us away from our children, our spouse, our prayers and our own thoughts.
- **Comparison.** Social media shows everyone else's best moments. Judaism teaches us to be happy with our portion (Pirkei Avot 4:1) and to focus on our own mission.
- **Lashon hara online.** Gossip and harsh words spread faster and last longer on a screen. The laws of [lashon hara](/learn/lashon-hara) apply to posts, comments, group chats and forwards just as much as to speech.

## Shabbat: a weekly break from screens

Long before anyone spoke about a "digital detox," Jews have been putting down their tools for 25 hours every week. On Shabbat we do not create or control the world; we enjoy it. Phones, computers and TV are set aside, and something wonderful fills the space.

Families who keep Shabbat often say it is the best part of their week: long meals with real conversation, children who have their parents' full attention, time for a walk, a nap, a book, a visit with friends. Parents finish Shabbat rested; children grow up knowing that people matter more than screens. If a full Shabbat feels like too much at first, start with Friday night dinner without phones and see how it feels. See [Shabbat with kids](/learn/shabbat-with-kids) for ideas.

## Practical ideas for every day

- **Phone-free meals.** Put the phones in a basket during dinner. Say a blessing, eat, talk.
- **Start the day with your soul, not your inbox.** Say [Modeh Ani](/learn/modeh-ani) and a few words of prayer before checking messages.
- **Kids and devices.** Decide together on screen times and places (not in bedrooms, not at the table), and model what you ask for. Children copy what we do far more than what we say.
- **Mindful use.** Before opening an app, ask: why am I opening this? Turn off notifications you do not need.
- **Use it for good.** Listen to a Torah class on your commute, learn the [daily portion](/learn/daily-torah-study), send a kind message to someone who is lonely, share a Jewish holiday reminder, or give tzedakah online.

## Finding your balance

There is no single rule for every family. What matters is that we stay in charge, and that our time, our attention and our tools are used for what really matters. For questions about specific situations, such as children's phones or using technology on holidays, ask the Rabbi.

## Common questions

### Does Judaism say technology is bad?
No. The Rebbe taught that every invention can be used to serve G-d and do good. The question is how we use it, and whether it helps or harms our family, our relationships and our soul.

### Can I use my phone on Shabbat?
Phones, computers and other electronics are not used on Shabbat, except to save a life or in a real emergency. Many people find this weekly break is one of the greatest gifts of Shabbat. For specific situations, ask the Rabbi.

### Does lashon hara apply to social media?
Yes. Posting, commenting, forwarding or "liking" negative information about someone can all be lashon hara, and because it spreads so far, it can be even more harmful online.

### How can I help my kids use screens well?
Set clear times and places for devices, keep meals and Shabbat screen-free, and be a role model. Fill their lives with real friends, Jewish experiences and good activities, so screens are only one small part.
