A Kohen (כֹּהֵן, a male descendant of Aaron the priest) may not come into contact with the dead, but he is required to attend the funeral and burial of his closest relatives: father, mother, son, daughter, wife, brother and unmarried sister. For every other funeral or cemetery, a Kohen keeps a distance, and the details depend on the place, so ask the Rabbi.

## The basic rule

Chabad.org explains that a Kohen is forbidden to become ritually impure through contact with the dead. The impurity comes in three ways: touching, carrying, and being under the same roof or covering as the body. In an open area a Kohen keeps about 6.5 feet (four *amot*) from a grave or body. A solid partition can reduce the distance. The basics of who is a Kohen are in [Kohen, Levi, Yisrael](/learn/kohen-levi-yisrael).

## The seven close relatives

The Torah makes an exception for seven relatives: father, mother, son, daughter, wife, brother and unmarried sister. Chabad.org says a Kohen must attend the burial of these relatives, even though this makes him impure, and it is considered a mitzvah. The exception covers only the funeral and burial, and only for that relative. Details such as half-siblings are given differently, so please ask the Rabbi about your case. A Kohen may not become impure for other relatives, such as a grandfather or stepparent.

If you are a Kohen and mourn someone close, you are not left out: you are expected to take part in the funeral and burial. You may sit [shiva](/learn/shiva) and say [Kaddish](/learn/kaddish) as every mourner does.

## Funerals and cemeteries for other deaths

Chabad.org explains the following for a Kohen:

- He may not attend an indoor service where the body is present. An outdoor service is permitted at the required distance, and he must not stand under anything covering the body.
- He may enter a cemetery only if he can keep the required distance and avoid coverings such as trees or awnings.
- He may stay in a car on cemetery grounds, provided the windows are closed or open no more than three inches.
- A Kohen visiting a hospital should ask a rabbi first, because impurity can spread under a shared roof. In great need, such as visiting a parent, wife or child who would be deeply distressed, a Kohen may rely on a more lenient view.

## Planning the grave of a Kohen's relative

Because a Kohen cannot visit his relatives' graves in a way that makes him impure, Chabad.org notes that graves of Kohen's relatives are often placed near the edge of the cemetery. If a Kohen's parent, spouse or child is to be buried, tell the Rabbi so that the plot and arrangements can be chosen with this in mind. See [buying a plot in a Jewish cemetery](/learn/buying-a-jewish-cemetery-plot) and [visiting a grave](/learn/visiting-a-grave).

## If a Kohen is near and does not know

Chabad.org advises that if a Kohen is unknowingly near the dead, someone should tell him quietly, so that he can step away.

## Common questions

### Can a Kohen go to a funeral?

A Kohen may attend the funeral and burial of his father, mother, son, daughter, wife, brother and unmarried sister. For other funerals, he keeps the distance Jewish law requires, so ask the Rabbi.

### Can a Kohen enter a cemetery?

Only if he can keep about 6.5 feet from graves and avoid anything covering them, such as trees. Ask the Rabbi before you go.

### Can a Kohen attend the funeral of a grandparent?

No. Chabad.org says a Kohen may not become impure for a grandfather, stepparent or relatives outside the seven. It is difficult, and the Rabbi can help you take part at a distance.

### Why do Kohanim stay away from the dead?

It is a commandment in the Torah for the descendants of Aaron the priest to avoid ritual impurity from the dead. See [Kohen, Levi, Yisrael](/learn/kohen-levi-yisrael).

### Can a Kohen visit a relative's grave?

Chabad.org says that after the burial, a Kohen may not visit the grave in a way that makes him impure, which is why the graves are placed near the cemetery's edge. Ask the Rabbi.

### Can a Kohen stay in a hospital room with a dying parent?

Ask the Rabbi right away. Chabad.org says that in great need, such as visiting a parent, a Kohen may rely on a lenient view.
