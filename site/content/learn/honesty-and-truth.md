**Emet**, truth, is at the very center of Judaism. "The seal of the Holy One, blessed be He, is truth" (Talmud, Shabbat 55a). A person who lives honestly carries G-d's seal.

## Keep far from falsehood

The Torah does not just say "do not lie." It says, "Keep far from a false matter" (Exodus 23:7). We stay far away from deception, not only from outright lies. The Sages forbid even **geneivat da'at**, "stealing someone's mind": creating a false impression, such as pretending to do someone a favor you are not really doing. This applies toward every person, Jewish or not.

## Keeping your word

"He shall not break his word; all that comes from his mouth he shall do" (Numbers 30:3). A Jew's word should be as good as a signed contract. Many have the custom to say "bli neder," "without a vow," when promising small things, so as not to make a binding vow, and then to keep the promise anyway.

## A small change for peace

Truth is not a weapon. Judaism teaches that one may alter the truth for the sake of peace (Talmud, Yevamot 65b). When Sarah laughed that she was too old to have a child, saying her husband was old, G-d repeated it to Abraham as "I am old," so as not to hurt Abraham's feelings (Genesis 18:12-13).

Aaron, Moses' brother, was famous for making peace. When two people quarreled, he would go to each one and tell them how much the other regretted the fight, until they embraced (Avot d'Rabbi Natan 12).

This permission is narrow. It is for preserving peace and sparing feelings, such as praising a host's meal or a bride's beauty, never for personal gain or to avoid responsibility. When in doubt, choose truth said gently.

## Honesty in business

The Talmud says that the first question a person is asked in the World to Come is, "Did you deal honestly in business?" (Shabbat 31a). Honest scales, honest advertising, paying on time and full disclosure are part of serving G-d. See [Business Ethics](/learn/business-ethics).

## Honesty with children

The Talmud teaches: do not promise a child something and then not give it, because you teach the child to lie (Sukkah 46b). Children learn honesty from what they see: a parent who returns extra change, keeps promises and says "I was wrong."

## Admitting mistakes

Great people in the Torah admitted when they were wrong. When Judah realized Tamar was right, he said, "She is more righteous than I" (Genesis 38:26). When Moses was corrected by his brother Aaron, he agreed and was not ashamed to say so (Leviticus 10:20, with Rashi). Admitting a mistake is the first step of [teshuvah](/learn/teshuvah) and takes real [humility](/learn/humility).

## Common questions

### Is it a lie to say "I'm fine" when I'm not?
It is a common courtesy, not deception. But it is healthy to have someone you can tell the truth to.

### May I praise a gift I don't like?
Yes. Thanking someone warmly for a gift is for the sake of peace and kindness. Focus on what is true: their thoughtfulness.

### What if the truth will hurt someone?
Ask whether they need to know, and if so, say it kindly and privately. Truth and kindness go together.

### Is honesty toward non-Jews the same?
Yes. Deceiving anyone is forbidden, and honest dealing with everyone is a [Kiddush Hashem](/learn/kiddush-hashem).
