The holidays are built around family: the Seder table, the menorah, the Rosh Hashanah meal, the people who were always there. After a loss, those same moments can hurt the most. The empty chair is easy to see, and the songs bring back a voice we miss. If you are dreading a holiday this year, you are not alone, and nothing is wrong with you.

## What Jewish tradition says

Judaism takes grief seriously. There are set times to mourn ([shiva](/learn/shiva), [sheloshim](/learn/sheloshim), [the year of mourning](/learn/year-of-mourning)), and set times to rejoice. Interestingly, a holiday stops shiva early: the joy of the festival is a mitzvah that the whole community shares, and it gently lifts a mourner forward. This does not mean we pretend we feel fine. It means we are not left alone with our grief; we are carried by the community and by the holiday itself.

The [soul](/learn/the-soul) of the person we lost is not gone. Jewish teaching says a soul is aware of the good we do in its merit, and our holidays, prayers and mitzvot bring it real joy and elevation.

## Practical ideas

- **Keep one tradition, change another.** It is fine to do some things exactly as your loved one did them, and to do others differently because the old way is too painful. There is no wrong choice.
- **Bring them to the table.** Tell a story about them, cook their recipe, use their menorah or their Kiddush cup. Many families give [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah) in their memory before the holiday.
- **Light a candle or learn in their memory.** A [yahrzeit candle](/learn/yahrzeit-candle) is not only for the yahrzeit; it is also lit before Yom Kippur and the holidays when Yizkor is said. Saying [Kaddish](/learn/kaddish) or [Yizkor](/learn/yizkor) on the holidays when it is said is a gift to the soul.
- **Do not spend it alone** unless you truly want to. Accept an invitation, or come to a community holiday event, where it is easier to be quiet and still be surrounded by warmth.
- **Plan an exit.** Know that you can step outside for a few minutes. Tell one person you trust how you are feeling.
- **Take care of yourself**: sleep, eat, walk outside. Grief is tiring.

## Helping someone who is grieving

Invite them, and make it easy to say yes or no. Mention their loved one by name; most mourners want to hear it. Do not try to fix the sadness. A short message ("Thinking of you this Chanukah") means more than you know. See [comforting mourners](/learn/comforting-mourners).

## Common questions

### Should we still celebrate the first year after a death?
Yes. Holidays are kept even in the year of mourning, though a mourner stays away from parties with music and dancing for a time (a year for a parent, thirty days for other close relatives). The Seder, the menorah and the holiday meals are all kept. Ask the Rabbi about a specific event.

### Is it okay to cry on a holiday?
Of course. Tears and holiday joy can sit side by side. Many people find that after the hardest moment passes, they are glad they took part.

### Can I talk to someone?
Yes. The Rabbi and the Rebbetzin are glad to sit with you, by phone or in person, and help you plan how to get through the day.
