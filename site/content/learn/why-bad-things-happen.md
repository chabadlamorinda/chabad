"Why do good people suffer?" is one of the oldest questions there is. Moses asked it, King David asked it in the Psalms, and the whole Book of Job is about it. Judaism does not offer a neat answer that makes the pain go away. It offers a way to live with the question, to keep faith, and above all to help.

## Job and the limits of understanding

Job was a good man who lost his children, his wealth and his health. His friends insisted he must have sinned. G-d later told them they were wrong. When G-d finally answered Job "out of the whirlwind," He did not explain the reason. He showed Job how vast creation is, and how small a human view of it is.

The sages put it simply: "It is not in our hands to explain the tranquility of the wicked or the suffering of the righteous" (Ethics of the Fathers, chapter 4). We see one frame of a very long film.

## Everything is ultimately good

The Talmud tells of Nachum Ish Gamzu, who said about everything that happened to him, "Gam zu l'tovah," "This too is for the good," and of Rabbi Akiva, who taught, "Whatever G-d does is for the good."

Chassidus goes further. It teaches that nothing truly bad comes from G-d; some good is so high that it comes down hidden, looking like the opposite. This is a deep belief about G-d's goodness. It is not a reason to pretend pain does not hurt, and it is not a promise that we will understand.

## We do not explain someone else's pain

A belief that comforts us in our own trouble must never become a lecture to someone else. A famous Chassidic saying, attributed to Rabbi Moshe Leib of Sassov, teaches that even disbelief has one use: when someone comes to you in need, act as if there were no one but you to help, not as if "G-d will take care of it." Our job is not to justify another person's suffering. It is to:

- Visit, call and listen (see [comforting mourners](/learn/comforting-mourners) and [bikur cholim](/learn/bikur-cholim)).
- Bring a meal, drive to an appointment, help with the bills.
- Pray for them by name (see [Mi Sheberach](/learn/mi-sheberach)).

## It is all right to be angry

Abraham argued with G-d: "Shall the Judge of all the earth not do justice?" Moses asked, "Why have You done evil to this people?" These words are in the Torah. Honest prayer, even angry prayer, is still a relationship with G-d. Many people find that saying [Tehillim](/learn/tehillim) gives them words for what they feel.

## "Until when?"

Judaism does not accept suffering as the way things should be. The Rebbe taught that we should cry out to G-d, "Ad matai?" "Until when?" and ask for [Moshiach](/learn/moshiach), when, as Isaiah says, G-d "will wipe away tears from every face." Every act of kindness brings that day closer.

## Common questions

### Is suffering a punishment?
Not necessarily, and we should never say so about someone else. Job's friends made that mistake. Each person can look inward and grow, but the reasons belong to G-d.

### Is it a lack of faith to ask "why?"
No. Moses, King David and Job all asked. Asking shows that you expect the world to make sense, which is itself a kind of faith.

### What can I say to a friend who is suffering?
Often very little. Be there, listen, and offer practical help. Avoid "everything happens for a reason." Ask the Rabbi if you are unsure what to say.

### I am angry at G-d. Can I still pray?
Yes. Tell G-d exactly how you feel. That is prayer too. If you would like to talk it through, call the Rabbi.
