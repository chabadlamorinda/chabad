A Jewish baby boy is named at his brit milah on the eighth day, and a baby girl is traditionally named in the synagogue when her father is called up to the Torah. The name is announced with a special prayer asking G-d to bless the child with health, wisdom and good deeds.

## Why the name matters

Jewish tradition teaches that a name is far more than a label. The Hebrew name expresses something of the person's soul, and Chassidic teachings say that parents receive a small spark of prophecy when choosing it. A Hebrew name is used when a person is called to the Torah, in prayers for their health, on a ketubah (marriage contract), and in memorial prayers.

## When a boy is named

A boy receives his name at the end of his [brit milah](/learn/brit-milah). After the circumcision, a blessing is recited over a cup of wine, and the mohel or Rabbi announces: "...and his name in Israel shall be called..." This is often a very emotional moment, since the name is traditionally kept private until then.

## When a girl is named

A girl is named at the Torah reading in synagogue. The custom is to do this as soon as possible after birth, at the first Torah reading where a minyan is present. The Torah is read on Monday, Thursday and Shabbat mornings, as well as on holidays and Rosh Chodesh.

Here is what typically happens:

1. The father (or another family member, if needed) is called up for an aliyah, a blessing over the [Torah](/learn/torah).
2. After the reading, a special prayer called a Mi Shebeirach is recited for the mother and the baby.
3. The baby's Hebrew name is announced for the first time.
4. Many families sponsor a kiddush afterward so the community can celebrate.

The mother and baby do not need to be present. Many mothers are still recovering at home.

## Choosing a name

Common practices include:

- **Naming after a relative.** Ashkenazi Jews traditionally name after a loved one who has passed away, keeping their memory alive. Many Sephardic families also name after living grandparents, which is an honor in their tradition.
- **Biblical and traditional names.** Names like Sarah, Rivka, Moshe or David connect a child to our heroes.
- **Holiday names.** A child born near a holiday might receive a related name, such as Mordechai or Esther around Purim.

## Common questions

### Can a baby girl be named at home instead of in synagogue?

The traditional custom is to name her at a Torah reading, which requires a Torah scroll and a minyan. If that is hard for your family, talk to us. We can help find a time and place that works.

### What if my child was never given a Hebrew name?

It is never too late. A child, teen or adult can receive a Hebrew name at a Torah reading. Many people do this before a bar or bat mitzvah or a wedding. Ask the Rabbi and we will arrange it.

### Do we need to keep the name secret?

There is a widespread custom not to reveal a boy's name until the bris, and many families also wait until the girl's naming. It is a custom, not a strict law, but it adds to the meaning of the moment.

### Should the Hebrew name match the English name?

It does not have to. Many families choose an English name that shares a first letter or sound with the Hebrew name, but any combination is fine.
