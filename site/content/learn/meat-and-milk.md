Jewish law forbids cooking, eating or benefiting from meat and milk together. In practice this means separate dishes and utensils for meat and dairy, and waiting between eating meat and eating dairy.

## Where it comes from

Three times the Torah says, "Do not cook a kid in its mother's milk" (Exodus 23:19, 34:26 and Deuteronomy 14:21). The Oral Torah explains that the repetition teaches three separate prohibitions:

1. **Cooking** meat and milk together.
2. **Eating** meat and milk cooked together.
3. **Benefiting** from such a mixture, for example selling it or feeding it to a pet.

The Sages added protective rules, such as treating poultry like meat and not eating meat and cheese at the same meal, even cold.

## Meat, dairy and pareve

Every kosher food falls into one of three groups:

- **Meat (fleishig):** beef, lamb, poultry, and anything cooked with them.
- **Dairy (milchig):** milk, butter, cheese, cream, and anything cooked with them.
- **Pareve:** neither, such as fruits, vegetables, grains, eggs and fish. Pareve food can be eaten with either.

Check labels. A product marked "D" or "dairy" next to its kosher symbol contains dairy or was made on dairy equipment.

## How long to wait

**After meat, before dairy:** the widespread custom, followed by Chabad, is to wait six hours. Some communities have other customs, so follow your family tradition or ask the Rabbi.

**After dairy, before meat:** most dairy needs only a short break. Eat something pareve, rinse your mouth, and wash your hands. Hard aged cheeses are the exception. After those, many wait six hours, just as after meat.

## Keeping them apart at home

A kosher kitchen has two sets of everything that touches food:

- Separate pots, pans, dishes, cutlery and serving pieces.
- Separate dish racks, sponges and towels.
- Ideally separate sinks, or careful use of one sink with basins.
- A clear system, like color coding, so everyone in the family knows what is what.

Many people also keep a third set for pareve cooking, which lets pareve food stay usable with either meal. Our [kosher kitchen](/learn/kosher-kitchen) guide explains the full setup.

## When a mistake happens

Mistakes happen in every kosher home: a dairy spoon in a meat pot, or milk splashed on a hot meat dish. Do not throw anything away yet. The answer depends on the temperature, the amounts and other details. Set the items aside and ask the Rabbi. Often the solution is simpler than you expect.

## Common questions

### Why do we wait after meat but not after milk?
The Sages explain that meat leaves a taste and residue that lasts in the mouth for hours, while most dairy does not. Hard cheese is treated like meat for this reason.

### Can I eat fish with cheese?
Yes. Fish is pareve, and fish with dairy is fine. Lox and cream cheese is a classic. Fish is not eaten together with meat, though.

### Is chicken really considered meat?
Yes. By rabbinic law poultry is treated exactly like meat, with the same waiting time and separate dishes.

### Can I use one dishwasher for meat and dairy?
This is a complicated question that depends on the machine. Many people keep one dishwasher for one type only. Ask the Rabbi about your kitchen.
