Sometimes we see a friend, a child, a spouse or a colleague doing something hurtful or wrong. Should we say something? The Torah says yes, and also teaches how. "You shall surely rebuke your fellow," says the Torah, and in the very next breath, "you shall love your fellow as yourself." Correction in Judaism, called tochacha, is meant to be an act of love.

## Why speak up at all

The Torah also says, "Do not hate your brother in your heart." Keeping resentment inside poisons relationships. A caring word, said the right way, can help someone grow, repair a friendship, and free us from carrying anger.

## How to say it so it can be heard

- **Check your motive.** Am I trying to help, or to win? If I am angry, wait until I calm down.
- **Speak privately.** The Sages taught that embarrassing someone in public is a grave wrong. Never correct in front of others.
- **Be gentle and specific.** Talk about the action, not the person: "When you said that, it hurt," not "You always..."
- **Start with what is good.** Mention what you appreciate first.
- **Include yourself.** "We all slip sometimes" goes further than "You did wrong."
- **Listen.** There may be something you did not know.

## When to stay silent

The Talmud says that just as it is a mitzvah to say what will be heard, it is a mitzvah not to say what will not be heard. If you know your words will only cause a fight, wait for a better moment, or find another way. And criticism is never a cover for [lashon hara](/learn/lashon-hara): we speak to the person, not about them.

## Receiving criticism

Pirkei Avot praises the person who loves correction. When someone takes the trouble to tell us something hard, it is a gift. Thank them, think about it, and take what is true.

## Common questions

### Should I correct my adult children?
Gently and rarely, with lots of love and without pressure. Often a good example says more than words. The Rabbi or the Rebbetzin are glad to talk it through with you.

### What if the person gets angry?
Stay calm, do not argue, and leave the door open. You can come back to it another time.

### Is it my place to correct a friend's religious practice?
Judaism teaches love, warmth and example first. Inviting someone to Shabbat does more than any critique. When in doubt, ask the Rabbi.
