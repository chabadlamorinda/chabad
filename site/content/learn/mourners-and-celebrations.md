Jewish mourners generally avoid joyous gatherings, especially parties with music and dancing, for thirty days after the burial, and for twelve months after losing a parent. The details depend on who passed away and on the kind of celebration, so ask the Rabbi about your own case before you decide.

The idea is not to punish a person who is grieving. Mourning is a time of inwardness, and Jewish law gives the heart room to heal before it returns to public joy ([the year of mourning](/learn/year-of-mourning)).

## Three time periods

- **Shiva, the first seven days:** Mourners stay home and avoid anything that brings joy. A mourner does not marry or attend celebrations ([shiva](/learn/shiva)).
- **Sheloshim, the first thirty days:** For all seven relatives, joyous occasions are avoided until the thirty days end ([sheloshim](/learn/sheloshim)).
- **Twelve months:** For those mourning a parent, restraint continues for the year.

Shiva and sheloshim are counted from the burial; the twelve months for a parent are counted from the day of death. Holidays can change the count. See [shiva and holidays](/learn/shiva-and-holidays) and ask the Rabbi to work out the dates.

## Weddings

According to Chabad.org (Rabbi Maurice Lamm), after losing a parent a mourner should generally avoid a wedding hall with music and dancing for twelve months, and for another relative for thirty days. There are more lenient possibilities, such as a wedding with no instrumental music, or if the mourner is needed for the minyan or to help. These are case by case, so do not assume.

If you are the parent of the bride or groom and are mourning your own parent, you often can attend and take part, with some limits during sheloshim. A person mourning a child cannot easily plan a wedding during shiva or sheloshim, but there are narrow exceptions. In all of these, speak to a rabbi.

## Bar and bat mitzvah

A mourner may usually attend the service. The party, the meal and the music are the questions. According to Chabad.org, a parent hosting may prepare after shiva but not sit at the festive meal during sheloshim, and a parent-mourner may attend after sheloshim if the child gives a Torah talk, since that makes it a religious occasion. See [attending a bar or bat mitzvah](/learn/attending-a-bar-bat-mitzvah). If this affects your family, call the Rabbi well before the date.

## Music, parties and everyday life

- **Music:** Dance music and music enjoyed in company are signs of joy and are avoided; many mourners also avoid listening for pleasure. Ask the Rabbi what applies for your relative.
- **Parties and social dinners:** Large parties are avoided, small get-togethers with family and close friends are fine.
- **Work and ordinary life:** After shiva, mourners return to work and normal routines.

## What you can do instead

If you cannot attend a celebration, send a warm note and a gift, call the family, and join a quiet visit before or after. A good family will understand. If you are hosting a simcha and a friend is in mourning, tell them there is no pressure, and see [comforting mourners](/learn/comforting-mourners).

## Common questions

### Can a mourner go to a wedding?
It depends on the relative and on the timing. For a parent, a wedding hall with music is generally avoided for twelve months, and for other relatives for thirty days. Ask the Rabbi for your case.

### Can I listen to music during the year of mourning?
Music that brings joy is generally avoided during mourning, especially for a parent. Ask the Rabbi what is right for you.

### Can a mourner attend a bar mitzvah?
A mourner can usually attend the synagogue service, but the party, meal and music are limited. Chabad.org notes some leniency after thirty days if the celebrant gives a Torah talk. Ask the Rabbi.

### Can I get married during shiva or sheloshim?
Marriage is not held during shiva, and during sheloshim only in narrow circumstances. Always ask the Rabbi first.

### What if I am the host of a simcha and I am mourning?
Speak with the Rabbi early: there are different guidelines for a parent hosting a bar or bat mitzvah, and for a wedding of a child.

### How long is the mourning year?
For a parent, twelve months of restraint from joyous occasions; for other relatives, thirty days. The Rabbi can tell you the exact dates.
