We live in a place of great plenty, and many of us are still restless. There is always a bigger house, a better vacation, a child who got into a better school. Jewish wisdom has a short, famous answer to this restlessness, and it has not aged a day.

## "Who is rich?"

Ben Zoma taught: "Who is rich? One who is happy with his portion, as it says, 'When you eat of the labor of your hands, you are fortunate and it is well with you'" (Ethics of the Fathers 4:1). Notice that he does not say how much the rich person has. Wealth, in the Jewish view, is not a number in a bank account; it is the relationship between what you have and how you feel about it. A person with little who is at peace is rich; a person with much who is always hungry for more is, in the deepest sense, poor.

The word "portion," *chelek*, matters too. Judaism teaches that what each of us has was measured out for us by G-d with love and purpose, like a portion served at a table. See [Divine providence](/learn/hashgacha-pratit).

## Gratitude is a practice, not a mood

A Jew is called *Yehudi*, from a word meaning "to thank." We begin every day with Modeh Ani, thanking G-d simply for being alive, before we have checked a single message. The sages ask us to say a hundred blessings a day: over food, over the body working, over seeing the ocean. A hundred times a day we notice something good that we did not make ourselves. This is not pretending life is perfect; it is training the eye. What you look for, you see. See [gratitude](/learn/hakarat-hatov) and [blessings](/learn/blessings).

## The comparison trap

The last of the Ten Commandments is "do not covet." It is the only one about a feeling, because coveting quietly poisons everything else. Today we carry the neighbor's house, car and vacation in our pocket, polished and filtered, and we compare our ordinary Tuesday to their best moment. No wonder we feel we are falling behind.

The Torah's cure is to look at your own table instead of the neighbor's. Ben Zoma's rich person is eating "the labor of *your* hands": your food, your family, your work, your life. See [jealousy and envy](/learn/jealousy-and-envy) and [screens and technology](/learn/technology-and-screens-jewish-view).

## Content in things, never in growth

There is a well-known Chassidic teaching that turns contentment on its head. In material matters, look at those who have less than you, and you will be grateful. In spiritual matters, look at those who have more than you, and you will be inspired to grow. Most of us do the opposite.

So a Jew is meant to be deeply content with their house and their car, and never content with their kindness, their learning or their prayer. "I have enough" is a holy sentence about things. "I am good enough as I am" is not a Jewish sentence about the soul. And growth itself is one of the great sources of joy. See [joy](/learn/simcha).

## Practical steps

1. **Say Modeh Ani** before you look at your phone, and mean it.
2. **Name three specific blessings** at dinner, out loud, as a family.
3. **Give tzedakah when you feel envy.** It reminds you that you are someone who has enough to give. See [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah).
4. **Celebrate someone else's good news** with a real "mazal tov"; it loosens envy's grip.
5. **Set one spiritual goal** to be "not content" about this month: a class, a mitzvah, a daily Psalm.

## Common questions

### Is it wrong in Judaism to want more money?
No. Judaism blesses honest work and prosperity, and wealth used well is a mitzvah. The problem is not wanting; it is letting wanting rob you of enjoying what you already have. See [the Jewish view on wealth](/learn/jewish-view-on-wealth).

### How can I be content when I am genuinely struggling?
Contentment is not denial. If you are short on money, health or peace, Judaism asks you to act, to ask for help and to pray. Gratitude for what remains gives you strength to do that. If you need support, call Rabbi Labkowski at 925-384-7242 or see [getting through hard times](/help/grief-and-hard-times).

### How do I teach my children to be content?
Children learn it from watching us. Thank G-d out loud, do not compare them to other children, and give them the joy of giving. See [children, money and tzedakah](/learn/children-money-and-tzedakah).
