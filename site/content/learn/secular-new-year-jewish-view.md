Fireworks at midnight, and everyone asks about your resolutions. For a Jew, this raises a quiet question: our new year was months ago, on Rosh Hashanah. So what is January 1st? Not a Jewish holiday, but not nothing either.

## Our new year is Rosh Hashanah

The Jewish year begins on the first of Tishrei, Rosh Hashanah, the anniversary of the creation of Adam and Eve. It is a day of judgment and of coronation: we stand before G-d, hear the shofar, and accept Him as King for another year. The ten days that follow end with Yom Kippur. That is where Jewish taking-stock, forgiveness and new beginnings live. See [Rosh Hashanah](/holidays/rosh-hashanah) and [the month of Elul](/learn/elul). See also [the Jewish calendar](/learn/jewish-calendar).

## What January 1st is, and is not

The civil calendar is the calendar of the world we work and live in, and Judaism has no quarrel with using it. We date our checks, file our taxes and plan the school year by it. Jews have always carried two calendars: the one in our pocket and the one in our soul.

January 1st is not a Jewish holiday. So there is no mitzvah or Jewish celebration connected to it. At the same time, there is no need to be sour about a long weekend, a day off, or a neighbor's good wishes. A friendly "Happy New Year" back is simple good manners; see [Thanksgiving: a Jewish view](/learn/thanksgiving-jewish-view) for a similar approach.

## The difference between a party and a new beginning

Chassidic teaching draws a line between two ways of marking time. One looks backward only to celebrate that the year is over; the other looks backward to grow. Rosh Hashanah is entirely the second kind. If you find yourself on December 31st wanting to take stock, that pull is Jewish, and you do not have to wait until Tishrei to follow it.

Judaism in fact builds taking stock into every day and every month: the [bedtime Shema](/learn/bedtime-shema) looks back on the day, the eve of [Rosh Chodesh](/learn/rosh-chodesh) on the month, and the Rebbe encouraged people to treat their [Hebrew birthday](/learn/hebrew-birthday) as a personal Rosh Hashanah, a day to reflect and take on a good resolution.

## Resolutions, the Jewish way

Most New Year's resolutions are forgotten within weeks, usually because they are too big and too vague. The Jewish approach, a *hachlatah tovah*, a good resolution, works differently:

- **Make it small and concrete.** Not "be more spiritual" but "light Shabbat candles this Friday," "give to tzedakah before I check my email," "learn one line of Torah a day."
- **Say "bli neder,"** "without a vow." We mean it, but we do not turn a good intention into a binding oath.
- **Start now, not on a date.** The Talmud says a person should repent the day before their death; since no one knows that day, every day is the day. See [teshuvah](/learn/teshuvah) and [changing a bad habit](/learn/changing-a-bad-habit).
- **Tie it to something that lasts.** A resolution shared with a friend, a spouse or the Rabbi has a much better chance.

## Living in Jewish time

The deepest Jewish answer to January 1st is not a rule but a rhythm. Every week ends in Shabbat; every month begins with a new moon; every year climbs from Pesach through Shavuot to Rosh Hashanah and back. When that calendar is alive in a home, the secular year passing is noted with a shrug and a smile. ## Common questions

### Is it wrong for a Jew to celebrate New Year's Eve?
There is no Jewish celebration of it, and much of what happens that night, drinking to excess and the like, is not the Jewish way. A quiet evening with family or friends is a different matter. Ask the Rabbi if you have a specific situation in mind, such as a work event.

### Can I wish people a Happy New Year?
Yes. Kindness and courtesy to everyone around us is a Torah value, and wishing a neighbor well costs nothing.

### I missed Rosh Hashanah this year. Is it too late to start fresh?
Never. Teshuvah is open every day of the year. Pick one mitzvah and begin this week, and come say hello at Chabad; nobody will ask where you were in Tishrei. See [not religious? You are welcome](/learn/not-religious-welcome).
