Judaism helps with grief by giving it a path: stage by stage, from the first numb hours through shiva, sheloshim and the year, with a community that comforts you and a faith that the soul continues. Chabad.org teaches that these practices give grief an accepted outlet and validation, which is important to healing.

If you are grieving, you do not have to do it alone, and you do not have to know what comes next.

## Grief has a path

Chabad.org describes the Jewish mourning observances as phases, each with its own purpose:

1. **Aninut: from death until burial.** A numbing, paralyzing grief. The family is so absorbed in arrangements that the Torah exempts them from mitzvot such as prayer and tefillin. The sages advise against offering words of comfort at this stage. Friends should be present and help with practical things. See [aninut](/learn/aninut).
2. **Shiva: seven days.** The mourners step away from daily routines to focus on the memory of the person, to think about how to honor them, and to receive comfort. See [shiva](/learn/shiva).
3. **Sheloshim: thirty days.** A gradual return to everyday life, while still keeping restraint. See [sheloshim](/learn/sheloshim).
4. **The first year, for a parent.** The restraint continues until the first yahrzeit. See [the year of mourning](/learn/year-of-mourning).

The structure helps mourners move step by step, until the loss becomes a constructive force in their lives. The article adds that the practices should be followed carefully but not exceeded.

## You may not feel what the rituals express

Chabad.org says something very honest: a mourner may not feel the intensity of grief that the rituals express, or may not feel ready to move to the next, lighter phase. This is normal. The framework has helped many people through this process. You do not have to force a feeling. For the longer road, read [the mourning journey](/learn/mourning-journey).

## Comfort comes from people

The mitzvah of comforting mourners (nichum aveilim) is a community's work. Visitors may talk about the person's life, join in prayer, give charity or simply be present, and are not expected to make the mourner cheerful. Comfort comes through presence, listening and shared remembrance. The traditional words are: "May G-d comfort you among the mourners of Zion and Jerusalem." For how to be that kind of friend, see [comforting mourners](/learn/comforting-mourners).

## The soul continues

Judaism teaches that death is not the end of the soul. Chabad.org says the memorial candle reflects the idea that the soul is a "candle of G-d," and that the mourning and memorial practices are meant to honor the soul and help it rise. Chabad.org calls it "nothing greater" for a soul that its relatives, friends and children take on good deeds in its merit: learning Torah, giving tzedakah, improving in a mitzvah. See [the afterlife](/learn/afterlife) and [learning in memory of a loved one](/learn/learning-in-memory-of-a-loved-one).

## When to ask for more help

Jewish tradition is not a replacement for professional care. If your grief feels unmanageable, or if you have thoughts of harming yourself, please reach out to a doctor, a counselor or a crisis line as well as to the Rabbi. Chabad.org's article on mourning does not address counseling, so this is our own gentle note, not a quotation. For hope in hard times, see [hope in hard times](/learn/hope-in-hard-times).

## Common questions

### How does Judaism help with grief?
It gives grief an accepted outlet and a step-by-step structure: aninut, shiva, sheloshim and the year, with the support of a community and the belief that the soul continues.

### How long does Jewish mourning last?
Shiva is seven days. Sheloshim is thirty days. For a parent, mourning lasts a full year, and Kaddish is said for eleven months.

### What if I do not feel sad, or I am not ready to move on?
Chabad.org says that mourners may not feel the intensity of grief the rituals express, or may not feel ready for the next phase. This is common. Speak with the Rabbi.

### What should I say to someone who is grieving?
Say the traditional words of comfort and be present. Chabad.org says comfort comes through presence, listening and shared remembrance, not through forced cheerfulness.

### Does Judaism believe the soul lives on?
Yes. Chabad.org teaches that the soul is a "candle of G-d" and that mourning and memorial rituals help it rise to a higher state.
