Jews remember the six million who were murdered in the Holocaust by saying Kaddish, lighting memorial candles, learning Torah and doing mitzvot in their memory. Above all, the Jewish response has been to live proudly as Jews, so that what the victims cherished lives on.

## Why we remember

The Torah tells us to remember what Amalek did and never to forget. Remembering the Holocaust is part of honoring every life that was lost, many of whom left no children and no grave. For their families, and for the whole Jewish people, remembering is a sacred duty.

## Days of remembrance

- **The Tenth of Tevet.** Many victims' dates of death are unknown. Israel's Chief Rabbinate set the fast of the Tenth of Tevet as a general day of Kaddish, called Yom HaKaddish HaKlali, for those whose yahrzeit is not known. Families light a candle and say Kaddish on this day. See [minor fast days](/learn/minor-fast-days).
- **Yom HaShoah.** Holocaust Remembrance Day falls on the 27th of Nissan, shortly after Passover. Communities hold memorial gatherings, read names and light six candles.
- **Yizkor.** In the [Yizkor](/learn/yizkor) memorial prayer on the holidays, many add a prayer for the martyrs of the Holocaust.
- **Tisha B'Av.** On this saddest day of the year, kinot (elegies) are recited for Jewish tragedies through history, and many communities include kinot for the Holocaust.

## Jewish ways to remember

- **Kaddish.** [Kaddish](/learn/kaddish) is said in a minyan in memory of the departed. If you do not know a relative's date of death, you can say Kaddish or arrange for it to be said on the Tenth of Tevet.
- **A memorial candle.** Lighting a 24-hour candle, as on a [yahrzeit](/learn/yahrzeit), recalls the verse "the soul of a person is the candle of G-d."
- **Learning.** Studying Mishnah is a traditional way to honor a soul, because the Hebrew letters of mishnah also spell neshamah, "soul." Learning a chapter, saying [Tehillim](/learn/tehillim), or dedicating a class in memory of a family member brings merit to their soul.
- **Tzedakah and good deeds.** Giving tzedakah or doing a mitzvah in someone's memory is a gift to their soul. Even a small mitzvah, done with them in mind, matters.
- **Names.** Many victims have no one left to remember them. Learning their names, and naming children after relatives who perished, keeps their memory alive.
- **Living Jewishly.** The Rebbe often taught that the strongest answer to those who tried to destroy the Jewish people is to strengthen Jewish life: lighting Shabbat candles, educating children in Torah, and building Jewish homes. Every Jewish child and every Shabbat is a living memorial.

## Speaking with survivors and their families

Listening to survivors' stories, and recording them, is a mitzvah of kindness and of memory. Children and grandchildren of survivors carry these stories forward.

## Common questions

### What if I do not know when my relatives died?
That is common. You can say Kaddish and light a candle on the Tenth of Tevet, the general day of Kaddish. You may also choose another meaningful date. Ask the Rabbi for guidance.

### Can I say Kaddish for someone who is not my parent?
Yes. Kaddish may be said for any Jew, including relatives lost in the Holocaust. A minyan is needed. We can help arrange it.

### How can I teach my children about the Holocaust?
Speak in an age-appropriate way, focusing on Jewish courage, faith and continuity, not only on horror. Sharing family stories and doing a mitzvah in memory of a relative helps children connect in a positive way.

### Why light six candles?
Six candles are often lit at Holocaust memorial gatherings to represent the six million Jews who were murdered.
