Psalm 23 (מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד, "A Psalm of David") is a short song of trust in which King David describes G-d as his shepherd who provides, guides and protects him. Jews turn to it in times of worry, illness and loss, and many sing it at the Shabbat table.

## Who wrote it and when

The Book of Psalms (Tehillim, תְּהִלִּים) is traditionally attributed mainly to King David. Rashi, quoting the Sages, explains that Psalm 23's opening, "of David, a song," tells us something about its source. When a psalm says "a song of David," David played first and then the Divine Presence rested on him. When it says "of David, a song," the Divine Presence came first and then he sang. Psalm 23 begins "A Psalm of David," in the first form, the one in which David first made music. Read it in the Hebrew spirit: it is a person talking to G-d in song. See [Tehillim](/learn/tehillim) and [King David](/learn/king-david).

## The psalm, verse by verse, in plain words

1. **"G-d is my shepherd, I shall not lack."** A shepherd feeds and guards his flock. Rashi notes that even in the dry wilderness David was confident he would lack nothing.
2. **Green pastures and still waters.** G-d gives rest and refreshment. Rashi connects this with David writing it in a barren forest, which G-d made feel like the blessing of the World to Come.
3. **"He restores my soul."** After exhaustion and running for his life, David's spirit is renewed. G-d leads him on straight paths that keep him from falling into his enemies' hands.
4. **The valley of darkness.** Even in the darkest place, "I fear no harm, for You are with me." The rod and staff can mean hardship and trust in G-d's kindness, and both comfort David.
5. **A table before my enemies.** Rashi connects the anointing oil to David having been made king by G-d's command. The overflowing cup means abundance.
6. **"Goodness and kindness will follow me all my days."** The psalm ends with confidence that G-d's goodness walks behind us, and that we will dwell in G-d's house for many years.

## When Jews say Psalm 23

- **Hard times**: Chabad.org lists Psalms as a source of comfort in times of distress. Families say Psalms at the bedside of someone who is ill. See [hope in hard times](/learn/hope-in-hard-times).
- **At Shabbat meals**: in many communities it is sung, often to a melody, at the third Shabbat meal. Customs vary, so ask the Rabbi what your family or community does.
- **In mourning**: Psalms are recited near the time of a death and at a funeral. Which psalms and when depends on community custom; ask the Rabbi.
- **For others**: [praying for others](/learn/praying-for-others) and [Mi Sheberach](/learn/mi-sheberach) often include Psalms.

## The message: trust (bitachon)

The heart of the psalm is **bitachon** (בִּטָּחוֹן), trust in G-d. It does not say life has no dark valleys. It says we do not walk through them alone. For more, see [bitachon](/learn/bitachon). You can say the psalm in Hebrew or in English; G-d hears every language, as explained in [talking to G-d in your own words](/learn/talking-to-g-d-in-your-own-words).

## Common questions

### What is Psalm 23 about?
Psalm 23 is about trust: King David describes G-d as a shepherd who provides, guides and protects him, even in the darkest valley.

### What is Psalm 23 called in Hebrew?
It is called "Mizmor LeDavid, Hashem Ro'i" (מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד ה' רֹעִי), meaning "A Psalm of David, G-d is my shepherd."

### Do Jews read Psalm 23 at funerals?
Jews recite Psalms around a death and at funerals, and Psalm 23 is well known. Which psalms are said varies by community, so ask the Rabbi.

### When do Jews sing Psalm 23?
Many communities sing it at the third Shabbat meal. Customs differ, so ask the Rabbi.

### Can I say Psalm 23 for someone who is sick?
Yes. Saying Psalms for a person who is ill is a beloved Jewish practice. Call Rabbi Labkowski at 925-384-7242 to add a name to our prayers.

### Do I need to read it in Hebrew?
Hebrew is traditional, but you can read it in English and it still counts as heartfelt prayer.
