Jewish parents usually choose a Hebrew name that honors a beloved relative or a great figure from Jewish history, and that carries a meaning they hope their child will grow into. Jewish tradition teaches that parents receive a spark of prophecy when they name a child, so the name is more than a label: it is connected to the child's soul.

## Why the name matters

The Hebrew name is the name a person is called up to the Torah with, prayed for with when sick, and remembered by. Chassidic teaching explains that the letters of a Hebrew name are channels through which life and blessing flow to the soul. Read more in our guide to [Hebrew names](/learn/hebrew-names).

## Naming after relatives

The most common way to choose a name is to name after a family member:

- **Ashkenazi custom** (families from Central and Eastern Europe) is to name after a relative who has passed away, not a living one. This keeps the memory of the person alive and is believed to bring merit to their soul.
- **Sephardic custom** (families from Spain, North Africa and the Middle East) often names after living grandparents, which is considered a great honor.

Many families keep the Hebrew name and choose an English name with the same first letter or a similar sound. For example, a grandfather named Moshe might be honored with a baby named Moshe, called Max in English.

## Other ways to choose

- **A great person from Jewish history**: Avraham, Sarah, Moshe, Miriam, David, Esther.
- **A righteous person or tzaddik**: in Chabad families, names of the Rebbes and their wives are common, such as Menachem Mendel, Shneur Zalman, Levi Yitzchak, Chaya Mushka and Chana.
- **The time of birth**: a child born on Purim may be named Esther or Mordechai, and one born around Chanukah may be named Yehuda.
- **A beautiful meaning**: names that express a prayer or a quality.

## Popular Hebrew names and their meanings

**For boys**
- Avraham: father of many nations
- Yosef: G-d will add
- Shmuel: heard by G-d
- Ari: lion
- Natan: He gave
- Eliyahu: my G-d is the L-rd
- Menachem: comforter

**For girls**
- Sarah: princess
- Rivka: to bind or connect
- Devorah: bee
- Chana: grace
- Batsheva: daughter of the oath
- Shira: song
- Yehudit: praise

## Names to avoid

Tradition avoids naming a child after people known for wickedness in the Torah, such as Pharaoh or Haman. Some families also avoid naming a baby after a relative who died young or tragically. If a name raises a question for your family, ask the Rabbi.

## When the name is given

A boy receives his Hebrew name at his [brit milah](/learn/brit-milah) on the eighth day. A girl is named in the synagogue when her father is called up to the Torah, ideally at the first Torah reading after birth. Our guide to [baby naming](/learn/baby-naming) explains both ceremonies.

## Common questions

### Can we give a baby two Hebrew names?
Yes. Many children receive two names, often to honor two relatives. Both names are used together, for example Chaya Mushka or Yosef Yitzchak.

### Is it bad luck to share the name before the naming?
Many families keep the name private until the bris or the Torah naming, out of custom and to let the name be revealed at its proper moment. It is a tradition rather than a strict rule.

### Can we name a baby after a living parent?
Among Ashkenazim, it is generally avoided. Among Sephardim, naming after a living grandparent is common. Families with mixed backgrounds can ask the Rabbi for guidance.

### What if we want a modern Israeli name?
Many modern Hebrew names, like Noa, Tamar, Ari or Eitan, come straight from the Torah and the Prophets or carry beautiful meanings. Any good Hebrew name can serve as a Jewish name.
