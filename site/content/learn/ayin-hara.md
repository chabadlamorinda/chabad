Many Jewish grandmothers say "kein ayin hara" when they praise a grandchild. The idea of the ayin hara, the "evil eye," has real roots in Jewish sources. It is also surrounded by folk customs. Here is what it means, and what actually protects us.

## Sources in the Talmud and Mishnah

- **Ethics of the Fathers** (chapter 2) teaches that "an evil eye, the evil inclination and hatred of people remove a person from the world." The same chapter praises the opposite, a "good eye": being generous and happy for others.
- **The Talmud** (Bava Metzia 107b) records that the sage Rav saw ayin hara as a serious danger.
- **The Talmud** (Berachot 20a) says the descendants of Joseph are not affected by the evil eye, reading Jacob's blessing of Joseph (Genesis 49:22) this way.
- **Jewish law** says not to stand and stare into a neighbor's field when the crops are full, so as not to harm him with an envious look.

## What it means

Most commentators explain the ayin hara as the effect of envy and resentment. When we flaunt our blessings, we stir jealousy in others, and that jealousy can, as it were, bring our blessings under scrutiny. The lesson is about how we live: quietly, modestly, and with a generous eye toward others.

## Common customs

Customs vary from family to family:

- **"Bli ayin hara"** ("without the evil eye"), said after mentioning good news, such as how many children someone has.
- **Not counting people directly.** The Torah counted the Jewish people with half-shekel coins (Exodus 30:12). For a [minyan](/learn/minyan), many count "not one, not two..." or use a ten-word verse.
- **Not showing off** a new baby, a new home or good fortune.
- **The red string.** Tying a red thread, often one wound at Rachel's Tomb, is a popular folk custom. It is not a mitzvah or a law, and some authorities were critical of it. It is no substitute for the things below.

## The real protection

1. **Mitzvahs.** Every mitzvah is a source of blessing and protection.
2. **A kosher [mezuzah](/learn/mezuzah)** on every doorway, checked regularly. The mezuzah is called a guardian of the home.
3. **Humility.** A person who does not flaunt does not stir envy. See [humility](/learn/humility).
4. **A good eye of your own.** Be happy for others and do not envy them. That removes the problem at its source.
5. **Trust in G-d.** See [bitachon](/learn/bitachon). Worry about the evil eye should never become fear.

## Common questions

### Is the evil eye a real Jewish belief?
Yes, it appears in the Talmud. But its main message is ethical: avoid envy and avoid flaunting. It is not meant to make us anxious.

### Should I wear a red string?
It is a folk custom, not a requirement, and opinions about it differ. If it matters to you, ask the Rabbi. A kosher mezuzah and mitzvahs are the protection Jewish teaching emphasizes.

### Why do people say "bli ayin hara"?
It is a humble way of acknowledging a blessing without boasting, and a small prayer that it should continue.

### I'm worried someone gave me an evil eye. What should I do?
Do not let fear take hold. Give tzedakah, have your mezuzahs and [tefillin](/learn/tefillin) checked, say a chapter of [Tehillim](/learn/tehillim), and call the Rabbi if you would like to talk.
