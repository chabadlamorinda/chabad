To daven (pronounced DAH-ven) is the Yiddish word for praying in the traditional Jewish way, using the set prayers of the siddur, the prayer book. The word is used for all three daily prayers, morning, afternoon and evening, as well as the prayers of Shabbat and holidays.

## The word and its meaning

"Daven" is Yiddish, the language of the Jews of Eastern Europe, and it has no exact English translation. Hebrew calls prayer *tefillah* (תְּפִלָּה), a word that also carries the sense of connecting. Where the Yiddish word comes from is debated, so it is best not to rely on any one explanation. People use it as a verb, "I'm going to daven," and as a noun, "davening."

## What davening includes

According to Chabad.org, prayer takes place three times a day: *Shacharit* in the morning, *Minchah* in the afternoon and *Maariv* in the evening, with additions on Shabbat and holidays. These prayers are organized in the siddur, whose name comes from the word for "order." Davening includes:

- Blessings and psalms of praise
- The [Shema](/learn/shema), the declaration of G-d's oneness
- The [Amidah](/learn/amidah), the silent standing prayer
- Torah reading on certain days
- Aleinu and closing prayers

For a full map, see The Morning Service and [Daily Prayers](/learn/daily-prayers).

## The Chassidic way: prayer as connection

Chabad.org describes concentration as the most important part of prayer. Speaking a little with focus is better than speaking a lot without it, and it frames prayer as standing before a king: "Know before Whom you stand." This is the heart of the Chassidic approach. Chassidic teaching, including Chabad Chassidus, stresses that prayer is more than reciting words. It is a moment of connection with G-d. Chassidic teaching encourages taking time to think about the meaning of the prayers and about the One we are addressing, so that prayer comes from the heart. Chabad.org adds that G-d knows our needs already, and that prayer helps us recognize that everything we have comes from Him. To go deeper, read [Kavanah in Prayer](/learn/kavanah-in-prayer) and Why Pray?.

The prayer book text used by Chassidim is called *Nusach Arizal*, which follows the teachings of Rabbi Yitzchak Luria, the Arizal. The wording is slightly different from other prayer books, and Chabad communities use it.

## What davening looks like

- **Swaying or bowing.** Some people sway while praying. See Shuckling.
- **Quiet and standing.** The Amidah is said silently and standing.
- **Rushing is discouraged.** Chabad.org says to avoid talking during prayer and not to rush.
- **Your own pace.** You do not need to keep up with others.

## How to begin davening

1. **Choose one short prayer,** such as the Shema or Modeh Ani. See Modeh Ani.
2. **Read the meaning first,** in English, so your heart is in it.
3. **Say it slowly,** with attention to whom you are speaking.
4. **Add your own words.** See [Talking to G-d in Your Own Words](/learn/talking-to-g-d-in-your-own-words).
5. **Come to a service** and see how it feels with others.

## Common questions

### What does daven mean?
To daven is to pray in the traditional Jewish way, from the siddur. It is a Yiddish word.

### Is "daven" the same as "pray"?
Almost. "Pray" is general, while "daven" usually means the structured prayers of the siddur, though people also say it more loosely.

### What is the Chassidic way of davening?
It emphasizes concentration and connection, standing before G-d with focus and heartfelt meaning. Chabad.org calls concentration the most important part of prayer.

### Do you have to daven in a synagogue?
No, but Chabad.org says praying in a synagogue is preferable, even without a minyan. See [Praying Alone at Home](/learn/praying-alone-at-home).

### Why do people sway when they daven?
It is a common custom, and people do it in different ways. See Shuckling.
