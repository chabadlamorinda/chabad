In Judaism, eating is more than fuel: when we eat with awareness, say a blessing and use the energy for something good, we elevate the food into holiness. Chassidic teaching says every piece of food contains a spark of G-dliness, and our meals are a chance to lift it.

For families in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda, this turns an ordinary dinner into something meaningful. Here is how.

## Why does Judaism care how we eat?

Eating keeps us alive and gives us pleasure, but Chabad.org teaches that Judaism treats it as a spiritual exercise too. In a recent Chabad.org article on mindful eating, Rabbi Menachem Feldman notes that paying attention while eating is even good for health, while eating distracted can lead to overeating.

Judaism adds a deeper layer: the food on our plate is part of G-d's creation, and what we do with it matters.

## The sparks in food

Kabbalah and Chassidic teaching say every created thing has a soul, a "spark of G-dliness," whether it is a stone, a plant or an animal. Each spark longs to rise and reconnect with its source. Humans, made in G-d's image and with free choice, are the ones who can lift them.

The Tanya (chapter 7) explains how. Chabad.org says that eating purely for personal pleasure lowers both the eater and the food to the food's own level, which is a missed opportunity. Eating for a higher purpose, such as to be healthy and have energy to serve G-d and fulfil your mission, lifts the spark and reunites it with its Divine source.

## The blessing before eating

Before we eat, we say a blessing. Chabad.org explains that when we say a blessing and intend to serve G-d with the food's energy, we elevate the food's physical substance into holiness. These blessings belong to the type called *birkat hanehenin* (blessings of enjoyment): we may not enjoy G-d's world without first acknowledging its Source.

For bread there is the *hamotzi* blessing, and for other foods blessings suited to the kind of food. See the guide to [blessings](/learn/blessings). After a meal with bread we say Grace After Meals. See [blessings after food](/learn/blessings-after-food).

## Use the energy for good

Chabad.org's practical advice for mindful eating has four steps:

1. **Be present.** Notice the colors, smells, flavors and textures.
2. **Recognize the spark.** Be aware of the holiness within the food.
3. **Bless.** Say the proper blessing.
4. **Direct the energy.** Ask yourself how the strength from this meal can fuel a good deed: a phone call to someone alone, a donation, an hour of learning, kindness at work.

## The table as an altar

When the Holy Temple stood, the altar brought atonement. The Talmud (Menachot 97a) teaches that today a person's table does. In the Temple, the daily offerings combined an animal, a grain offering and salt, covering all the kingdoms of creation. Chabad.org explains that today the work of lifting the world's sparks falls to each of us, through what we eat and how we eat it. Read more in [the Holy Temple](/learn/the-holy-temple).

This is why a Jewish table is a place of blessings, words of Torah and guests.

## Kosher, hospitality and Shabbat

The same ideas connect other parts of Jewish life:

- **Kosher.** The laws of [kosher](/learn/kosher) shape what we eat. Chabad.org notes that when someone eats kosher meat and uses the energy for a mitzvah, a spark of divinity is raised.
- **Guests.** Sharing food is a mitzvah. See [hospitality](/learn/hachnasat-orchim).
- **Shabbat meals.** The Friday night and Shabbat day meals are the weekly high point of the Jewish table. See [Shabbat meals](/learn/shabbat-meals).

For kosher questions in your own kitchen, ask the Rabbi.

## Common questions

### What is the Jewish view of eating?
Judaism sees eating as a spiritual practice as well as a physical need. Done with intention, blessings and good purpose, it lifts the food into holiness.

### Why do Jews say a blessing before eating?
We say a blessing to acknowledge that the food comes from G-d. Chabad.org says that with the blessing and the intention to serve G-d, we elevate the food.

### What does it mean to raise the sparks?
It means finding the G-dly spark in food and elevating it by using its energy for a mitzvah and good deeds. This idea comes from Kabbalah and Chassidic teaching.

### What does "the table is an altar" mean?
The Talmud says that where the altar once brought atonement, today a person's table does. A table with blessings, charity and guests carries that holiness.

### Is eating itself a mitzvah?
Eating is not always a mitzvah, but it becomes one when it is done to serve G-d. If you want details for your own situation, ask the Rabbi.
