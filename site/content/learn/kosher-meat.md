Kosher meat comes from a kosher species of animal or bird that was slaughtered by a trained shochet, inspected for health, and then soaked and salted to remove the blood. Every step matters, so meat from a kosher animal is still not kosher unless all of them were done properly.

## Which animals are kosher

The Torah (Leviticus 11 and Deuteronomy 14) gives two signs for kosher mammals: they have **split hooves** and they **chew their cud**. Cows, sheep, goats and deer qualify. Pigs, rabbits, camels and horses do not.

For birds, the Torah lists the non-kosher species instead of giving signs. Because of this, Jews eat only birds with a clear tradition of being kosher, such as chicken, turkey, duck and goose.

## Shechita: kosher slaughter

Shechita is done by a shochet, a G-d-fearing person trained in the detailed laws. He uses a perfectly smooth, extremely sharp knife and makes one swift cut across the throat. This causes an immediate loss of blood flow to the brain. The knife is checked before and after, because even a tiny nick makes the shechita invalid.

## Inspection and glatt

After shechita, the animal is inspected (bedikah) for defects that would make it a treifah, an animal that would not have survived. The lungs get special attention.

**Glatt** means "smooth" in Yiddish. It describes lungs free of adhesions. Many communities, including Chabad, eat only glatt meat for beef and lamb. Today the word is often used loosely to mean a higher standard.

## Removing forbidden parts

Certain fats (chelev) and the sciatic nerve (gid hanasheh) are forbidden. Removing them from the hindquarter is hard work, so in most places outside Israel the hindquarter is simply sold as non-kosher meat. That is why cuts like sirloin are rare in kosher butcher shops.

## Removing the blood (kashering)

The Torah forbids eating blood. To draw it out, raw meat is:

1. **Soaked** in water for about half an hour.
2. **Salted** on all sides with coarse salt and left to drain for one hour.
3. **Rinsed** thoroughly, usually three times.

Liver has so much blood that it must be broiled over an open flame instead of salted. Almost all kosher meat and poultry sold today is already kashered, but check the label.

## Buying kosher meat

Look for a reliable kosher certification on the package (see our guide to [kosher symbols](/learn/kosher-symbols)). Meat should stay sealed until you use it. Once at home, meat is cooked only in meat pots and kept fully separate from dairy. Read more in [meat and milk](/learn/meat-and-milk).

## Common questions

### Is kosher meat healthier?
Kosher laws are G-d's commandments, not a health program. That said, the careful inspection process means many problems are caught. We keep kosher because it brings holiness into eating.

### Is fish considered meat?
No. Fish with fins and scales is pareve, neither meat nor dairy. Still, Jewish custom is not to cook or eat fish together with meat. Rinse your mouth or eat something in between.

### What does "kosher style" mean?
"Kosher style" usually means a menu inspired by Jewish foods, like pastrami or brisket, but not actually kosher. Only food with reliable supervision is kosher.

### Do I need to salt meat myself?
Almost never. Nearly all kosher meat sold in stores is already soaked and salted. If you buy unsalted meat or liver, ask the Rabbi how to prepare it.
