Watching a parent lose their memory is one of the hardest things a family can go through. The person you love is still there, and yet so much seems to slip away. Judaism has deep and gentle teachings for this time, and you do not have to face it alone.

## The broken tablets

When Moses came down from Mount Sinai and saw the golden calf, he broke the first tablets of the Ten Commandments. The Talmud teaches that the broken pieces were not thrown away: they were kept in the Holy Ark, right beside the new, whole tablets. From this, the Sages learned that **an elderly person who has forgotten their learning, through no fault of their own, must still be treated with full honor.** Your parent's dignity is not measured by what they remember.

## Honoring a parent who does not recognize you

The mitzvah of honoring parents continues even when a parent cannot understand it. The Talmud tells of a man whose mother, in her confusion, embarrassed him in public, and he did not answer her harshly. Honor today may look like a calm voice, a familiar song, holding a hand, or making sure they are clean, comfortable and treated kindly by others.

At the same time, Jewish law does not ask a child to do the impossible alone. When care at home becomes too much, arranging good professional care is itself a way of honoring a parent. Ask the Rabbi about your situation.

## Jewish life in memory care

Long-held memories often stay longest. Many people with dementia still respond to:

- **Shabbat candles** (electric candles are fine where flames are not allowed) and a familiar Shabbat song.
- **The Shema**, Modeh Ani, or a blessing they said all their life.
- **Holiday tastes and sounds**: a piece of challah, the shofar, a dreidel, Chanukah lights.
- **Yiddish, Hebrew or Russian** words from childhood.

The Rabbi or the Rebbetzin can visit your parent at home or in a care home, bring Shabbat or a holiday to them, and pray with them.

## Prayer and the caregiver

Add your parent's Hebrew name and their mother's Hebrew name to a [Mi Sheberach](/learn/mi-sheberach), and say Tehillim. And care for yourself: rest, accept help, and talk with someone. See [support for caregivers](/learn/caregiver-support).

## Common questions

### Can a rabbi visit my parent in memory care?
Yes. Call Rabbi Labkowski at 925-384-7242 to arrange a visit.

### Is my parent still obligated in mitzvot?
A person whose understanding is seriously impaired may not be obligated in the same way, but it is still a kindness to help them take part in what they love. Ask the Rabbi about specific questions.

### I feel guilty about moving my parent to a care home. Is that wrong?
No. Making sure your parent is safe and well cared for is a mitzvah. Please talk privately with the Rabbi or the Rebbetzin about the feelings that come with it.
