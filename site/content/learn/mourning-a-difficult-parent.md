If a parent who hurt, neglected or abandoned you has died, it is normal to feel grief, relief, anger, guilt, or nothing at all, sometimes all in one day. Jewish teaching does not ask you to pretend. Whether and how the laws of mourning apply when a parent was abusive is a personal question, so please ask the Rabbi privately before deciding anything.

If you live in Lafayette, Moraga, Orinda or elsewhere in the East Bay, you can call Rabbi Yisroel Labkowski or Rebbetzin Tzipora Labkowski at 925-384-7242. No one will judge you or need you to explain more than you want to.

## Mixed feelings are not a failure

Many people are surprised by how complicated the loss feels. You may be mourning the parent you had, or the parent you never had. Chabad.org, in an answer to a son whose father had abandoned him, says to acknowledge the hurt and the grudge honestly and not blame yourself for them. Once the idealized picture of a parent falls away, it adds, you can be free of guilt.

If you would like support beyond Torah, a counselor or doctor can help too. See [mental health: a Jewish view](/learn/mental-health-jewish-view).

## Does Jewish law require me to mourn?

The usual mourning laws for a parent, described in [losing a parent](/learn/losing-a-parent), are an expression of the mitzvah to [honor your parents](/learn/honoring-parents). The same Chabad.org answer explains that mourning is a way of honoring a parent, and that someone who was truly wicked, it gives the examples of a sexually abusive parent or a severe child-beater, has forfeited that honor. It also says the Torah does not ask you to place unbearable strain on your own well-being in order to honor a parent.

But every case is different. A parent who was absent, who struggled with addiction, or who was cruel in some ways and kind in others is not the same as one who was abusive. We do not decide this from a website. Please speak to the Rabbi, privately, and he will tell you what applies to you.

## What Kaddish does, for the soul and for you

[Kaddish](/learn/kaddish) is often misunderstood as a "prayer for the dead." Chabad.org explains that the mourner leads the congregation in declaring G-d's greatness, and that this elevates the mourner. Jewish mysticism teaches that a parent and child stay spiritually connected, so a child's growth in goodness can bring the parent peace. Some people find that saying Kaddish, even once, on the [yahrzeit](/learn/yahrzeit), is the beginning of their own healing.

You might decide to say Kaddish, to do some other mitzvah in their memory, such as giving charity, or to do nothing outwardly. Chabad.org suggests, for someone who could not mourn in the usual way, making it up with good deeds: giving charity, leading prayers and taking on mitzvot in the parent's merit. Ask the Rabbi what fits.

## Forgiveness without denial

Forgiveness is not the same as saying that what happened was acceptable. Another Chabad.org answer, to a woman whose parents had abused her, comes from a rabbi who hopes she will in time be able to forgive them. He suggests their souls, after death, have been through purification and feel remorse, and that only her forgiveness stands in the way of their full forgiveness. He also encourages her to continue working with her counselor.

That is one rabbi's gentle hope, offered to one person. It is not a demand, and you are not obligated to forgive before you are ready. Read more in [forgiveness and making peace](/learn/forgiveness-and-making-peace) and [difficult relationships with parents](/learn/difficult-relationships-with-parents).

## Common questions

### Do I have to sit shiva for a parent who hurt me?
It depends on the case, and this is a private question for the Rabbi. Chabad.org says that mourning honors a parent and that a truly abusive parent may not require it. Call 925-384-7242 and ask.

### Can I say Kaddish for a parent who was abusive or absent?
You may say Kaddish if you wish. Chabad.org teaches that Kaddish elevates the one who says it and can also bring a parent merit. Whether it is required in your case is for the Rabbi to say.

### Is it wrong to feel relief when a difficult parent dies?
No. Mixed feelings are common and human. You do not have to feel only one thing.

### Do I have to forgive my parent?
No one can force forgiveness. Jewish teaching values it, and some find healing in it over time, but it is yours to give when you are ready, ideally with support.

### What if I find out about a parent's death a long time later?
Chabad.org says the usual seven, thirty and twelve month periods do not apply in the same way, and tearing keriah should be done with a rabbi's guidance. Ask the Rabbi.
