In Judaism, pregnancy is a time of great blessing, when a mother partners with G-d in bringing a new soul into the world. Jewish tradition surrounds this time with prayer, good deeds and simple customs that help both parents prepare with joy and calm.

## A holy partnership

The Talmud teaches that there are three partners in the creation of every child: the father, the mother and G-d, who gives the soul. Pregnancy is therefore not only a physical process but a deeply spiritual one. Many mothers find that it is a natural time to add a little more prayer, kindness and mitzvot to daily life.

## Customs during pregnancy

These are widely kept customs. Every family can choose what fits them, and the Rabbi or Rebbetzin is happy to talk them through.

- **Keeping it private at first.** Many people do not announce a pregnancy until it is well along, often until the end of the first trimester or later, out of a sense of modesty and protection.
- **Checking mezuzahs.** Many families have their [mezuzahs](/learn/mezuzah) checked by a scribe to make sure they are kosher.
- **Tzedakah.** Giving charity regularly, especially on Friday before Shabbat candle lighting, is a time-honored way to bring blessing.
- **Tehillim.** Many mothers say chapters of [Tehillim](/learn/tehillim) (Psalms) for a healthy pregnancy and easy birth. Friends and family often join in.
- **Prayer in your own words.** Speaking to G-d in your own words, in any language, is always heard.
- **Preparing the home.** Some have a custom not to bring baby furniture into the home, or not to set it up, until after the birth. Others are comfortable preparing earlier. Both are acceptable.

## Preparing for birth

Jewish law treats the health of the mother and baby as the highest priority. Saving life, called *pikuach nefesh*, overrides Shabbat and holidays.

1. **Plan ahead for Shabbat.** If labor starts on Shabbat or a holiday, driving to the hospital and calling the doctor are permitted. Prepare your bag and keep things simple so you can focus on getting there safely.
2. **Talk to the Rabbi beforehand** about what a companion may and may not do on Shabbat, so you are not deciding under pressure.
3. **Bring Shir Hamaalot cards.** A Chabad custom is to place cards with Psalm 121 and other protective verses in the room of the mother and newborn, in the hospital and at home.

## Fasting during pregnancy

Pregnant women do not fast on minor fast days. For Yom Kippur and Tisha B'Av, speak with both your doctor and the Rabbi well in advance. Any health concern always comes first.

## After the baby arrives

- **For a boy:** a [brit milah](/learn/brit-milah) takes place on the eighth day, if the baby is healthy. Contact a mohel right away.
- **For a girl:** the father is called to the Torah and the baby receives her Hebrew name. See our [baby naming guide](/learn/baby-naming).
- **Thanking G-d:** many parents give tzedakah and say a prayer of thanks after a safe birth.

## Common questions

### Is there a special prayer for a pregnant woman?
There are many traditional prayers, and Tehillim is the most common. Many women also pray at candle lighting on Friday evening, a time known for being especially receptive to a mother's prayers.

### Can I find out the baby's gender?
Yes. Jewish law does not prohibit it. It is a personal choice for each family.

### Should we choose a Hebrew name before birth?
Many families think about names in advance but keep their choice private until the naming. See our guide to [Hebrew names](/learn/hebrew-names) for ideas.

### Can a pregnant woman go to a funeral or cemetery?
Some have a custom that pregnant women avoid cemeteries, while others do not. If you are unsure, ask the Rabbi.
