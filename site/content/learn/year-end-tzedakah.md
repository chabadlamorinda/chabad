Tzedakah (צדקה) is the Jewish word for giving to people in need, and it literally means "justice" or "righteousness," not "charity." Jewish tradition teaches that giving is a duty, not a favor, and that the right time to give is whenever you are able, not only at the end of the calendar year.

December is when many families in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda sit down and think about their giving. That is a good moment to ask the deeper question: what does Jewish tradition say about how much, to whom and how?

## Why we call it justice

Chabad.org explains that, in Jewish thought, all money ultimately belongs to G-d, who entrusts it to us to share. That is why giving is called tzedakah: we are not being generous with what is ours alone, we are doing what is right with what we were given. The poor person is not a favor to us; we are the ones given a chance to do a mitzvah. See [Tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah) for the basics.

## How much: maaser, a tenth

The sages taught that it is proper to give a tenth of one's earnings to tzedakah. This is called maaser (מעשר), "a tenth." Those who are able are encouraged to strive for a fifth, called chomesh. Read more in [Maaser: Giving a Tenth](/learn/maaser).

Many families find it easiest to keep a simple record all year: set aside a tenth when income arrives, and give from that fund as needs come up. Then December is not a scramble, it is simply a moment to see what is left in the fund and where it should go. Exactly how to calculate maaser in your situation is a personal question. Ask the Rabbi.

There is also a lovely custom of giving in multiples of 18, because the Hebrew word chai, "life," has the numerical value of 18.

## Who comes first

Jewish law gives an order of priorities, like concentric circles:

1. A family member in financial difficulty.
2. The poor and the organizations of your own community, before those far away.
3. Causes in Israel, before other causes in distant places.

This is not meant to shut anyone out. Most people give to several circles at once. For the full list see [Who Comes First in Giving](/learn/tzedakah-priorities) and [Tzedakah in Lamorinda](/learn/local-tzedakah-in-lamorinda).

## The eight levels of giving

The Rambam (Maimonides) lists eight levels of tzedakah. The highest is helping a person become self-supporting, for example through a loan or a job, so they keep their dignity. The lowest is giving unwillingly. Between them are gifts given cheerfully, quietly, before being asked and without the giver or receiver knowing each other. A good year-end question is: can I move one gift up a level?

## Common questions

### What does tzedakah mean?
Tzedakah means "justice" or "righteousness" in Hebrew, and it is the Jewish word for giving to those in need. It is called justice because Jewish tradition sees giving as a duty, not an optional kindness.

### How much tzedakah should a Jew give?
The sages taught that a tenth of earnings, called maaser, is the proper amount, and that those who are able should strive for a fifth. For how to apply this to your own finances, ask the Rabbi.

### Who should I give to first?
Jewish tradition teaches to take care of your own household and family first, then your local community, then Israel, then others farther away. Most people give to several of these at once.

### Do I have to give before December 31?
Jewish tradition does not tie tzedakah to the calendar year. Give whenever you can, and as soon as you are able. If December is when you plan, that is a fine time to plan.

### Is my gift tax deductible?
Ask your tax adviser. This guide is about Jewish giving and does not give tax advice.
