Chapter 32 of the Tanya teaches that you can love every Jew, near or far, because all Jewish souls come from one source, and only bodies are separate. When we look at the soul first, love for another Jew becomes as natural as love between brothers and sisters.

This is the chapter Chabad reaches for when it explains *Ahavat Yisrael* (אהבת ישראל), love of a fellow Jew. For the general guide to the mitzvah, see [Ahavat Yisrael](/learn/ahavat-yisrael). Here we follow the reasoning of the [Tanya](/learn/tanya) itself.

## The joy that comes just before

The chapter before it, Chapter 31, speaks to a person who feels bad about his spiritual state, and says he should find joy in his G-dly soul. Chapter 32 continues on that path: a person who treats his body as secondary and finds his joy in his soul has a direct road to the mitzvah "You shall love your fellow as yourself" (Leviticus 19:18), toward every Jew, great or small.

## The heart of the Tanya

We love ourselves without trying. Love for others usually has to be built. The Alter Rebbe, Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi, explains what changes the picture:

- If you do not put your body first, you will not feel above anyone because of it.
- Souls are not "better" or "worse" in a way we can measure. Their true greatness is known only to G-d.
- All the G-dly souls come from one root, so Jews really are brothers and sisters. Only the bodies are apart.

When love comes from the soul, it is natural. When it comes from the body, it depends on something outside: looks, usefulness, agreement. Love of that kind lasts only as long as the reason lasts.

## Hillel and the Torah on one foot

The Talmud (Shabbat 31a) tells how Hillel summed up the Torah for a person who wanted to convert: "What is hateful to you, do not do to your fellow." The Tanya asks a good question: how does love for people help with mitzvot between a person and G-d, such as Shabbat?

The answer is that the Torah's deepest purpose is to lift the soul above the body and bring G-d's light down among the Jewish people. That light dwells where there is unity. The chapter points to a line from the prayers asking G-d to bless us "all as one." Where there is division, the light is held back.

## Draw close with love

Chapter 32 also teaches how to treat a Jew who is far from Torah and mitzvot. The Alter Rebbe says that such a person is not to be pushed away. Following Hillel, who advised being a disciple of Aaron, we are to love peace, love people and bring them closer to Torah. One practical thought helps too: most Jews today were simply not raised in a Torah-observant home, so kindness works better than scolding.

## Three ways to start this week

1. **Look for the soul first.** Before you size someone up, remember what Tanya teaches about the soul within.
2. **Leave out the "but."** Notice when your love comes with a condition, and let it go for a moment.
3. **Do one small kindness** for a Jew you do not know well, and see how it feels.

## Common questions

### What does Tanya chapter 32 say?
It says that love for every Jew is natural because all Jewish souls share one root. It connects this to Hillel's teaching and to bringing G-d's presence among the Jewish people through unity.

### How can the Torah command a feeling like love?
Tanya answers that the command points us to the soul. Look at what is shared at the root, and the feeling grows from there.

### Does Tanya say I must love someone who hurts me?
The chapter says love is directed at the G-dly spark in a person, even when we dislike their deeds. For painful situations, ask the Rabbi. See [loving someone you don't like](/learn/loving-someone-you-dont-like).

### Does this mean I must agree with everyone?
No. You can disagree about practice and still love the person, because the love is for the soul.

### Is chapter 32 only for observant Jews?
No. It speaks of every Jew, great or small, and tells us to draw others close with love.
