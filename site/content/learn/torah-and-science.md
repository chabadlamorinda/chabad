Many thoughtful Jews carry a quiet worry: "I love being Jewish, but I also believe in science. Can I really hold both?" Judaism's answer is a warm yes. The same G-d who gave the Torah created the world that science studies. Truth cannot contradict truth.

## One Author, two books

The Torah opens with G-d creating heaven and earth. Psalm 19 says, "The heavens declare the glory of G-d." Maimonides writes that the way to come to love and awe of G-d is to contemplate His wondrous creations and see His wisdom in them. Studying nature is not a threat to faith; done honestly, it is a doorway to it. Chassidic teaching adds that every created thing exists only because G-d's word keeps creating it at every moment. When a scientist uncovers an elegant law of nature, they are reading a line of that word.

## The Rebbe's approach

The Rebbe, Rabbi Menachem M. Schneerson, studied science and engineering at universities in Berlin and Paris before leading Chabad. Many people wrote to him about the apparent conflict between the Torah and modern theories, and he answered in letters that are still widely read.

His approach, described in general terms, had a few parts. First, respect for science: it has brought enormous blessing to the world. Second, humility about what science can and cannot say. Experiments describe what we can measure now; theories about the distant past, such as the age of the universe or how life developed, rest on assumptions, for example that conditions and natural laws were always as they are today. Scientists themselves present these as the best current models, not final certainties, and models change. Third, the Torah is G-d's own account, and a Jew does not need to rewrite it every time a theory shifts. He also noted that a world created complete, with trees that already bear fruit and a man who is already grown, would look to us as if it had a history. The tone is not "science is wrong," but "let us be honest about what each kind of knowledge actually claims."

## Different views within the tradition

Jews have discussed these questions for a very long time. Some great rabbis read the "days" of creation very literally; others, drawing on the Talmud and Kabbalah, found deeper layers in the text. What unites them is the conviction that the world has a Creator who cares, that human life has purpose, and that we are responsible for our choices. On that there is no conflict with any laboratory finding, because science measures how things work, not why there is anything at all, or how we ought to live.

## What this means for you

- Keep asking. Judaism has never been afraid of questions; the Talmud is a book of arguments.
- Let science deepen your wonder. Learn how a cell works, look at pictures from a telescope, and say a blessing.
- Separate facts from philosophy. A measurement is a fact; "therefore there is no G-d" is a philosophy the measurement does not prove.

## Common questions

### Does Judaism reject evolution?
Judaism insists that G-d created the world and every living thing with purpose. How to read the Torah's account alongside scientific theories is discussed among believing Jews, and the Rebbe was careful to distinguish observed facts from theories about the unobserved past. A Jew can study biology with an open mind and still thank G-d every morning for creating everything.

### How old is the universe according to Judaism?
The Jewish calendar counts the years from creation; this year is in the 5700s. Some understand those years exactly as we count them today; others say the Torah's "days" before the creation of man may not be ordinary days. Both agree that time itself is a creation of G-d.

### Can a scientist be an observant Jew?
Certainly. Many are, in every field. Chabad teaching holds that the deeper one understands the world, the more clearly one can see G-d in it. See [believing in G-d](/learn/belief-in-g-d) and [miracles](/learn/miracles-jewish-view).

### What if I still have doubts?
Doubts are part of a living faith, not the end of it. Learn, ask, and keep doing mitzvot while you think; the doing often brings a clarity that arguments cannot. Rabbi Labkowski is glad to talk through any question.
