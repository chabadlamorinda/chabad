The Tanya has no chapter titled "marriage," but its ideas fit a home well: see your spouse's soul before their faults, pause before anger, and let the mind lead the heart. This guide applies teachings from the Tanya that Chabad.org presents, so that a couple can use them in everyday life.

It is meant as encouragement. For serious trouble, please talk to the Rabbi or the Rebbetzin, and to a professional.

## Seeing the soul in your spouse

Chapter 32 of the [Tanya](/learn/tanya) teaches that every Jew has a G-dly soul that comes from one source, and that love is natural when we look at the soul and treat the body as secondary. According to the chapter, love that depends on outer things lasts only as long as those things. Love rooted in the soul is deeper.

In a marriage, it is easy to see only habits: the dishes, the lateness, the tone of voice. The Tanya invites a different first look. Behind the habits stands a soul. See also [Tanya chapter 32 explained](/learn/tanya-chapter-32-love-every-jew).

## When anger rises: Epistle 25

The Alter Rebbe, Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi, wrote a letter on anger (Epistle 25 in the section called Iggeret HaKodesh). Chabad.org summarizes it this way:

- The Sages compare an angry person to an idolater, because in that moment he has lost sight that all that happens comes from G-d.
- The person who hurt you is still a free agent and responsible for his choices. But what reaches us is also part of what Heaven allowed, so anger at the person is misplaced.
- Anger is justified only in religious duty, such as stopping a fellow Jew from transgressing, never for personal offense.

How can this help a husband and wife? Not by excusing wrongdoing. It helps by lowering the temperature: this is a person I love, in a moment that has also been sent to me, and I choose my reaction. The Rebbe's advice on anger, as Chabad.org quotes it, is simple: "the first step is to wait," and to remember that if you hurt someone you will need to apologize and ask forgiveness in person.

## Mind over heart

Chapter 35 teaches that the mind can move the heart, so one need not wait for feelings to arrive before acting. A person can choose to speak calmly or to do an act of kindness, and feelings follow. See [mind over heart in the Tanya](/learn/mind-over-heart-tanya) for more, and [Tanya on speech](/learn/tanya-on-speech) for the power of words at home.

## Humility and "standing in his place"

In Chapter 30, the Tanya quotes the Sages: "Do not judge your fellow until you have stood in his place." A spouse's tired snap, missed phone call or forgetfulness has a backstory you may not know. Each of us can also ask: am I measuring my own effort fairly? See [judging others favorably](/learn/judging-others-favorably).

## Overlooking small things

Iggeret HaTeshuvah, Chapter 11, says that those who overlook slights and do not hit back are repaid by having their own failings overlooked. This is a good motto for a long marriage. See [forgiving others](/learn/tanya-on-forgiving-others).

## Common questions

### What does Tanya say about marriage?
The Tanya does not have a section on marriage, but its teachings on the soul, humility, anger and the power of the mind apply directly. This guide uses them as encouragement.

### What does Tanya say about anger at your spouse?
In Epistle 25, the Alter Rebbe says anger is a sign that faith in G-d's hand in our lives has slipped, and that the person who hurt us is still responsible for his choices.

### How do two souls fit into one home?
Each spouse has a G-dly soul and an everyday side; Tanya says the G-dly soul is a shared root of all Jews. See the guide on [shalom bayit](/learn/shalom-bayit).

### What should I do after an argument?
Wait, then apologize in person if you hurt your spouse, as the Rebbe advised. See [marriage arguments](/learn/marriage-arguments-jewish-wisdom).
