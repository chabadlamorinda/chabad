According to Jewish tradition, Moses wrote the Torah, but not as an author composing his own book. He acted as a channel: he received G-d's words and wrote them down faithfully. This is the difference between a human author and a prophet who "heard" G-d at Sinai and passed on what he heard.

## What "wrote" means here

The Torah (תּוֹרָה, "teaching") is the Five Books of Moses. Moses was a conduit for Divine communication, relaying G-d's instructions precisely, without adding his own interpretation. That is why the Torah is called the word of G-d, not the words of Moses.

## How and when Moses wrote it

Here is the traditional picture:

- At Sinai, Moses received the Ten Commandments and the laws that follow them, then spent forty days on the mountain.
- Over the following decades in the wilderness, he wrote in stages. Sometimes he wrote and then spoke the words to the people, sometimes he spoke first and then wrote down what he had said, and sometimes he recorded the counts of the people.
- Rashi teaches that Moses first gave the people an official version of Genesis and the part of Exodus up to that point.
- The Talmud records two views on timing: Moses wrote separate scrolls during the forty years and joined them later, or he wrote all five books near the end of his life. In every view, at the end Moses gathered it all together and gave copies to the tribes, with the instruction that each person write a Torah of their own. That is the root of the mitzvah to write a Torah scroll.

## Who wrote the last verses?

The final verses of the Torah describe the death of Moses. The sages discuss who wrote them. Some say Joshua wrote them. Others say Moses wrote them "in tears." Rashi cites both views. Ibn Ezra holds that Joshua wrote them, while Nachmanides holds that the whole Torah is Moses' transcription. Jewish tradition welcomes this kind of discussion, because it takes the text seriously.

## Passages that seem to disagree

People often ask about passages that seem to disagree. Here is an example. Genesis 6:3 seems to say that human life will be limited to 120 years, yet the Torah later reports longer lives. Rashi explains that the verse is about the generation of the Flood: G-d would give them 120 years to repent before the destruction. Such passages are an invitation to look deeper, and that is why there are so many commentaries on them.

## Why it matters

If the Torah is from G-d, then its mitzvahs are not just ancient customs or good advice. They are a conversation with the Creator. That is why Jews have studied it for thousands of years. ## Common questions

### Who wrote the Torah according to Judaism?
Moses wrote it, as a channel for the words G-d gave him. He relayed G-d's words precisely; he was not an author in the usual sense.

### Did Moses write the whole Torah, even the part about his death?
The sages discuss this. Some say Joshua wrote the last verses, and others say Moses wrote them in tears. Nachmanides holds that the entire Torah is Moses' transcription.

### When was the Torah written?
Moses received the first laws at Sinai and wrote over the following forty years of wandering. By the end of his life he had gathered it together and given copies to the tribes.

### Does the Torah contradict itself?
Apparent contradictions point to deeper meanings, and the commentaries explain them. Genesis 6:3 and the 120 years is one well-known example.

### Is it all right to ask these questions?
Yes. Questions are central to Jewish learning, and Torah study includes asking and debating. If a question troubles you, [ask the Rabbi](/help/talk-to-the-rabbi).
