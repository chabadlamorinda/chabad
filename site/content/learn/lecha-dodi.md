Lecha Dodi (לְכָה דוֹדִי), "Come, my beloved," is the hymn sung at the start of the Friday night service to welcome Shabbat as a bride. It was written in Safed in the 1500s by Rabbi Shlomo HaLevi Alkabetz and is the highlight of Kabbalat Shabbat. This Friday night, Kabbalat Shabbat at Chabad of Lamorinda is at {kabbalat-shabbat}.

## What Lecha Dodi is

Its refrain invites us: "Come, my beloved, to greet the bride; let us welcome the Shabbat." The Jewish people go out to meet Shabbat the way a groom goes to meet his bride. Chabad.org explains the image: Shabbat was the only day of Creation without a partner, so the Jewish people become its partner.

The hymn is about getting ready to greet Shabbat, and it looks ahead to a time when all of life, not only Shabbat, will carry the same holiness. In Kabbalah, Shabbat corresponds to the seventh of the divine attributes, malchut, which is connected with the Divine Presence (Shechinah) and with the Jewish people. You can read more in [Kabbalah](/learn/kabbalah) and [the sefirot](/learn/sefirot).

## Who wrote it, and why Safed?

Rabbi Shlomo HaLevi Alkabetz (about 1500 to 1580) was part of the circle of scholars and mystics in Safed (Tzfat) in the Land of Israel. According to Chabad.org, he was the teacher and brother-in-law of Rabbi Moshe Cordovero. He was born in Salonica, settled in Safed around 1535 and is buried there.

Safed's mystics, including Rabbi Yitzchak Luria, the Ari, would go out to the fields around the town on Friday afternoon to greet Shabbat. The Talmud (Shabbat 119a) describes Rabbi Chanina and Rabbi Yannai welcoming Shabbat in a similar way. The Ari included Lecha Dodi in his siddur, and it spread into the Friday night service in Jewish communities everywhere. Learn about the town in [Tzfat](/learn/tzfat).

## The hidden signature

Alkabetz signed his name in an acrostic: the first letters of the first eight stanzas spell "Shlomo HaLevi." If you look at the Hebrew in a siddur, you will see each of these stanzas begins with a different letter of his name.

## Standing and turning at the door

Near the end of Lecha Dodi, at the stanza that begins "Bo'i beshalom" and the words "Bo'i kallah" (come, bride), the congregation turns, and many bow slightly. Customs differ, so here are common explanations:

- It recalls the old practice of going outdoors to welcome Shabbat.
- Many turn toward the entrance of the room, as if welcoming a guest.
- Some face west, where the sun sets.
- Which direction to bow, and which side first, differs among communities.

We could not find a Chabad.org source explaining the reason in full, so the practice at Chabad of Lamorinda is best learned by asking the Rabbi. Nobody will mind if you simply turn with everyone else.

## Singing it together

Lecha Dodi has many melodies. Some are slow and prayerful and others are lively; newcomers usually join the refrain quickly, even without the Hebrew. ## Common questions

### What does Lecha Dodi mean?
Lecha Dodi means "Come, my beloved." It invites the Jewish people to go out and welcome Shabbat like a bride.

### Who wrote Lecha Dodi?
Rabbi Shlomo HaLevi Alkabetz, a mystic in Safed in the 1500s. He signed his name in an acrostic using the first letters of eight stanzas.

### Why do we turn around during Lecha Dodi?
We turn near the end, at "Bo'i kallah," to welcome Shabbat as a bride, recalling the practice of going out to greet it. Customs differ, so ask the Rabbi about ours.

### When is Lecha Dodi sung?
On Friday night, in the Kabbalat Shabbat service, before the evening prayers.

### Do I need to read Hebrew to join?
No. Many people follow in English, learn the refrain first and join in quickly.
