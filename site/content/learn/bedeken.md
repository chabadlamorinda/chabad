The bedeken (בדקן, also spelled badeken) is the moment just before a Jewish wedding when the groom lowers a veil over the bride's face. It recalls Rebecca, who covered herself with her veil when she first saw Isaac, and it is one of the most moving parts of the day.

## Where does the bedeken come from?

The Torah tells that when Rebecca saw Isaac approaching, "she took her veil and covered herself" (Genesis 24:65). Jewish tradition identifies this as the root of the custom. Today the veiling is part of a first marriage.

Many Ashkenazi couples do not see each other for the week before the wedding. The bedeken is the reunion: the groom, accompanied by his father and father-in-law or by whoever will escort him to the chuppah, and by the rabbi and friends, goes with song to where the bride is seated, with both mothers beside her.

## What happens at the bedeken?

1. The groom and his escorts walk to the bride's reception room.
2. The groom himself places the veil over her face. Jewish teaching stresses that his presence is central.
3. He recites the blessing that Rebecca received from her mother and brother before she married Isaac: "Our sister, be thou the mother of thousands of ten thousands" (Genesis 24:60).
4. The fathers and grandfathers, and often the rabbi, then bless the bride. In some families the bride's father also places his hands on her head and gives her a blessing.
5. The groom's party leaves, and everyone prepares for the chuppah.

In Chabad custom, the band plays a solemn melody composed by Rabbi Schneur Zalman of Liadi from the veiling until the ceremony ends. The veil should be thick enough to hide the bride's face completely, and Chassidic custom does not use gold or silver threads in it. The bride stays veiled through the chuppah.

## What does the veil mean?

Tradition gives several meanings:

- **Inner beauty.** The groom shows that he is committed to the bride's inner self, not only to the outward beauty that fades.
- **Modesty and dignity.** The veil expresses modesty and the dignity of a married woman.
- **Devotion.** It signifies the bride's commitment to keep her beauty for her husband.
- **The ring.** Under the chuppah the bride should not see the ring, so that she cannot judge its value and base her decision on a mistaken assumption.

Some rabbis of earlier generations even saw the veiling as a legal part of the chuppah itself; most authorities today see it as a beautiful tradition. Customs differ on the details, so ask the Rabbi what will be done at your wedding.

## Common questions

### What is the bedeken at a Jewish wedding?

The bedeken is the veiling of the bride by the groom shortly before the chuppah, followed by blessings from the parents and rabbi.

### Why does the groom veil the bride?

The custom comes from Rebecca covering herself when she met Isaac. It shows that the groom marries the bride for her inner qualities, not only her appearance.

### What does the groom say at the bedeken?

He recites the words Rebecca's family said to her: "Our sister, be thou the mother of thousands of ten thousands." It is a blessing for many generations.

### Is the bedeken done at a second marriage?

Jewish tradition says the veiling is reserved for a bride's first marriage. Ask the Rabbi about your situation.

### Do bride and groom see each other before the wedding?

Many Ashkenazi couples avoid seeing each other for the week before the wedding and meet at the bedeken. Customs differ, so ask the Rabbi what suits your family.

### Can guests attend the bedeken?

Often guests gather around the room, and the families are present. Ask the Rabbi or your wedding planner how it will be arranged.
