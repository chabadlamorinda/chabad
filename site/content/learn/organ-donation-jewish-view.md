Judaism holds that saving a life is one of the greatest mitzvot, and it also teaches deep respect for the human body, in life and after death. Because organ donation touches both values, Jewish law permits it in some situations and not in others, and each case should be discussed with a rabbi.

## Two great values

**Saving a life (pikuach nefesh).** The Talmud teaches that whoever saves one life is considered to have saved an entire world. Saving a life overrides nearly every other mitzvah, including Shabbat.

**Respect for the body (kavod hamet).** The body housed a holy soul and was created in the image of G-d. After death, Jewish law requires that it be treated with dignity and buried whole, as soon as possible. It forbids unnecessary cutting of the body, deriving benefit from it, and delaying burial. See our guide to the [Jewish funeral](/learn/jewish-funeral).

When a donated organ will directly save a specific person's life, the first value can outweigh the concerns of the second. The details are where the questions lie.

## Living donors

Donating a kidney, part of a liver or bone marrow while alive can save a life, and many rabbis consider it a great act of kindness. At the same time, Jewish law does not require a person to put his or her own life in serious danger to save another. Since medical risks differ from person to person, a living donor should consult both doctors and a rabbi.

Donating blood is widely encouraged.

## Donation after death

Here there are several key questions:

- **When is death?** Many organs can only be used while the heart is still beating, after a diagnosis of brain death. Leading rabbis disagree about whether brain death is death in Jewish law. If it is not, removing the organs would end a life, which is forbidden.
- **Is there a specific person waiting?** Jewish law gives great weight to saving a known, present patient, rather than donating for research or storage.
- **Respect for the rest of the body.** Anything not used for transplant is buried with the person.

Because of these questions, many rabbis advise against signing a general donor card or checking the box on a driver's license, which gives up control over these decisions. Instead, some carry a card or a written instruction stating that any donation must follow the guidance of a rabbi chosen by the family.

## Corneas, skin and tissue

Corneas, which restore sight, and some tissues are viewed differently by different authorities, since restoring sight may be considered saving a life in some cases. Ask the Rabbi.

## Common questions

### Is organ donation allowed in Judaism?
In some cases yes, and in some cases no. Donation that directly saves a life can be a great mitzvah, but questions of when death occurs and how the body is treated must be addressed. Ask the Rabbi about your specific situation.

### Can I donate my body to science?
Donating a whole body for medical research or teaching is generally not permitted, since it prevents proper burial and the body is not used to save a specific life. Ask the Rabbi if you are considering it.

### What should I put in my advance directive?
You can state that all medical and end-of-life decisions, including any organ donation, should follow Jewish law as guided by a rabbi. Rabbi Labkowski can help you with the wording.

### My loved one is in the hospital and we were asked about donation. What do we do?
Call the Rabbi as soon as you can. These decisions are time-sensitive, and he can help you understand the options with care and clarity.
