Simcha means joy, and Judaism teaches that joy is not a luxury but a central part of serving G-d. "Serve G-d with joy" (Psalms 100:2) is a guiding verse in Chassidic life, and Chassidic teaching sees happiness as a source of strength that helps us overcome almost anything.

## Why joy matters in Judaism

- **It is how we serve G-d.** The Torah warns of hardship that comes "because you did not serve G-d your G-d with joy" (Deuteronomy 28:47). The point is not just to do mitzvahs but to do them gladly.
- **Joy breaks barriers.** A Chassidic saying teaches that "simcha poretz geder," joy breaks through limits. A happy person can accomplish things they never thought possible.
- **The Divine Presence rests in joy.** The Talmud teaches that G-d's presence does not rest on a person in sadness, but in the joy of a mitzvah.

## Where real joy comes from

Chassidic teaching, especially the [Tanya](/learn/tanya), explains that true joy comes from knowing that G-d is close, that every Jew has a G-dly soul, and that every mitzvah connects us to Him. This joy does not depend on how the day is going. It rests on something deeper that never changes.

## Sadness, bitterness and real pain

The Tanya distinguishes between:

- **Atzvut (heaviness or sadness):** a mood that makes a person sluggish and stuck. Chassidic teaching urges us to avoid it.
- **Merirut (healthy bitterness):** a short, focused feeling of regret that moves us to change, followed by joy. It belongs to set times, like a bedtime accounting.

Judaism also honors real grief. Mourning a loss, feeling pain and crying out to G-d are part of life. And clinical depression is an illness, not a lack of faith: it should be treated with professional care, just like any other health condition. If you are struggling, please reach out to a doctor and to someone you trust.

## Jewish times of joy

- **Shabbat and holidays** are days of joy, with good food, wine and family.
- **Sukkot** is called "the season of our rejoicing." See [hakafot](/learn/hakafot) on Simchat Torah.
- **The month of Adar** begins a time when "we increase in joy."
- **Weddings and celebrations** are a mitzvah to share and uplift.

## Practical ways to add joy, step by step

1. **Start with gratitude.** Say [Modeh Ani](/learn/modeh-ani) when you wake up, thanking G-d for a new day.
2. **Sing.** A Chassidic [niggun](/learn/niggun) can lift the heart without words.
3. **Do a mitzvah for someone else.** Kindness brings joy to the giver too.
4. **Move your body.** The Sages valued health; a walk around the Lafayette Reservoir can change a mood.
5. **Celebrate small wins.** Notice what went well today.
6. **Come together.** Joy grows in community, at a Shabbat table or a holiday celebration.

## Common questions

### Is it a mitzvah to be happy?
Joy is part of how we are meant to serve G-d, and on festivals there is a specific mitzvah to rejoice. Chassidic teaching makes joy a daily goal, since it gives strength for everything else.

### What if I just do not feel happy?
Start with actions: sing, thank G-d for something small, help someone. Feelings often follow actions. If sadness lasts or feels heavy, talk to a doctor and to the Rabbi; you do not have to carry it alone.

### Does Judaism allow sadness?
Yes. Grief and pain are real and honored, especially in mourning. Judaism asks us not to stay stuck in heaviness, and to find our way back to hope.

### Why is joy so central in Chassidus?
The Baal Shem Tov taught that every person is precious to G-d and that G-d is found everywhere. Knowing that is a deep and lasting source of joy.
