Cheese is kosher only when it carries reliable kosher certification (a hechsher). Milk from a kosher animal is kosher, but cheese is made with added ingredients, such as rennet, that may not be, so Jewish law asks that a Jewish supervisor oversee its production.

## Why does cheese need a hechsher?

Milk, butter and cream are simple. Cheese is different, because it is made by curdling milk with an enzyme called rennet, and then adding cultures and other ingredients. Traditional rennet comes from the stomach lining of a calf, and that is where the problem lies: it may come from an animal that was not slaughtered according to Jewish law. A kosher cheese uses kosher rennet, either from a properly slaughtered calf or produced from microbes.

Because it is hard to know what went into a cheese just by reading the package, Jewish law relies on a supervisor, a **mashgiach** (מַשְׁגִּיחַ), who watches over the process. Chabad.org teaches that only cheese with reliable kosher certification should be eaten. See [kosher symbols](/learn/kosher-symbols) to learn how to recognize one.

## What about "vegetarian" cheese?

A label that says "vegetarian rennet" or "microbial enzymes" is a good sign, but it is not enough. Other ingredients, the equipment and the cultures can still be a concern, and some cheeses are flavored or processed with non-kosher additives. The cheese still needs a kosher symbol.

## Hard cheese, Parmesan and cheese on the shelf

Cheeses that are aged for a long time, such as Parmesan, Gruyere or aged cheddar, are traditionally made with animal rennet, so they are among those where it is most important to look for a hechsher. Do not rely on the name. Two Parmesans from the same store can differ completely.

Hard cheese also raises a question about waiting between cheese and meat. Because some authorities treat very aged hard cheese like meat, some people wait before eating meat afterwards. Customs differ, so ask the Rabbi what to do in your family. The basic rules are in [meat and milk](/learn/meat-and-milk).

## Chalav Yisrael and cheese

Chalav Yisrael (חָלָב יִשְׂרָאֵל) means milk supervised by a Jew from the time of milking. Cheese made from this milk is called **gevinat Yisrael** (גְּבִינַת יִשְׂרָאֵל). Some families choose chalav Yisrael products, and others rely on regular kosher-certified dairy. Read more in [chalav Yisrael](/learn/chalav-yisrael), and ask the Rabbi which way to go.

## How to read a cheese label

1. **Look for a kosher symbol** on the package, not only the word "kosher." See [kosher certification](/learn/kosher-certification).
2. **Check that it is a dairy symbol.** A "D" or the word "Dairy" tells you it is meant to be dairy.
3. **For sliced, shredded or deli cheese,** ask who certifies the store's counter. Cheese cut with a knife that also cut non-kosher cheese is not kosher.
4. **For pizza and restaurant dishes,** see [eating out kosher](/learn/eating-out-kosher).

## Common questions

### Is cheese kosher?
Cheese is kosher only with reliable kosher certification. Without a hechsher, the rennet and other ingredients cannot be confirmed.

### Why does cheese need a hechsher when milk does not?
Cheese is made with rennet and other additions that can come from non-kosher sources. Milk from kosher animals is simpler, so it is easier to rely on.

### Is Parmesan kosher?
Only if it has kosher certification. Traditional Parmesan is made with animal rennet, so look for a hechsher on the rind or the package.

### What is rennet?
Rennet is the enzyme used to turn milk into cheese. It traditionally comes from a calf's stomach, and kosher cheese uses kosher or microbial rennet.

### Do I have to wait after eating cheese before eating meat?
After soft cheese, the general rule is to rinse your mouth and check that nothing remains. After aged hard cheese, many wait longer. Ask the Rabbi what your family should do.
