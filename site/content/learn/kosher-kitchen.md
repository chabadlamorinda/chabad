A kosher kitchen is one where only kosher food is prepared, and meat and dairy are kept completely separate, with their own dishes, pots and utensils. Making your kitchen kosher usually takes a day or two of preparation, and the Rabbi can help you through it.

## What makes a kitchen kosher

Utensils absorb the taste of the food cooked in them, especially with heat. So a kosher kitchen needs:

- **Only kosher food.** Everything that comes in has reliable kosher certification.
- **Separate meat and dairy.** Two sets of dishes, cookware, cutlery and dish towels, kept and washed apart.
- **Kosher equipment.** Items previously used for non-kosher food are either koshered (kashered) or replaced.

## Step-by-step setup

### 1. Plan
Decide which cabinets and drawers will be meat and which dairy, and keep a small space for pareve items. Many people color-code: red for meat, blue for dairy. Make a list of what you will kasher, replace and buy.

### 2. Clear out non-kosher food
Go through the pantry and fridge and remove anything without a reliable kosher symbol. See our guide to [kosher symbols](/learn/kosher-symbols).

### 3. Kasher what can be koshered
Kashering uses heat to remove absorbed taste. The general rule is "the way it absorbed is the way it releases."

- **Hagalah (boiling water):** metal pots, cutlery and some other items are cleaned, left unused for 24 hours, then immersed in boiling water.
- **Libun (burning):** items used directly on fire, like grill grates and ovens, are heated to a very high temperature, for example by running an oven's self-clean cycle.
- **Pouring boiling water:** used for sinks and some counters after cleaning and waiting 24 hours.

Some materials, like most ceramics, china, earthenware and certain plastics, generally cannot be koshered. Glass, stoneware, stove tops, dishwashers and different countertop materials each have their own rules. Ask the Rabbi about each item.

### 4. Buy new items as needed
Buy separate sets for meat and dairy. New metal and glass utensils made or owned by a non-Jew need immersion before use (see below).

### 5. Immerse new dishes (tevilat keilim)
Metal and glass dishes, pots and cutlery bought new need to be immersed in a mikvah or other kosher body of water before first use. Remove stickers and glue first. Say this blessing before immersing:

Baruch atah Ado-nai, Elo-heinu melech ha'olam, asher kid'shanu b'mitzvotav v'tzivanu al t'vilat keli (for several items: keilim).

"Blessed are You, L-rd our G-d, King of the universe, who has made us holy with His commandments and commanded us concerning the immersion of a vessel (vessels)."

Ask the Rabbi where to immerse and which items need it.

### 6. Set kitchen habits
- Wash meat and dairy dishes separately, using separate racks or basins in the sink.
- Use separate sponges and towels.
- Keep meat and dairy foods covered and apart in the oven and fridge.
- If a mix-up happens, set the item aside and ask the Rabbi. Very often it can be fixed.

## Common questions

### Do I have to throw out all my dishes?
Usually not. Many metal pots, cutlery and some other items can be koshered. China and ceramics generally cannot. A walkthrough with the Rabbi can save money and stress.

### Can I have one oven for meat and dairy?
Many families use one oven for both, with certain precautions, such as not baking meat and dairy at the same time. Others prefer separate ovens. Ask the Rabbi what works for your kitchen.

### What do I do if I accidentally use a meat spoon in dairy?
Do not panic. Set the spoon and the food aside, and note whether the food was hot or cold. Then ask the Rabbi. Often the food and utensil can still be used or the utensil can be koshered.

### Do I need a separate sink?
Two sinks are convenient but not required. With one sink, use separate dish basins or racks for meat and dairy so dishes never sit directly in the sink. The sink itself can be koshered.
