An easy Shabbat dinner menu has five simple courses: challah, a fish or salad starter, chicken soup, a main dish with one or two sides, and a light dessert, all cooked before Shabbat and kept warm. Chabad.org lists fish, chicken soup, a chicken or meat main, kugel and tzimmes as the traditional Friday night foods, but the only things the meal truly needs are wine or grape juice and two loaves of bread.

For busy families in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda, the secret is not a longer to-do list. It is a short menu, made mostly ahead.

## The five courses

1. **Challah.** Begin with two whole loaves, bought or baked. See [challah](/learn/challah) and [baking challah](/learn/baking-challah).
2. **A starter.** Traditional gefilte fish, or any kosher fish, or a simple salad. Fish is a Shabbat favorite; see [gefilte fish](/learn/gefilte-fish) and [kosher fish](/learn/kosher-fish).
3. **Soup.** Chicken soup, with noodles or matzah balls, can be made days ahead and frozen. See [Jewish chicken soup](/learn/jewish-chicken-soup).
4. **The main dish and sides.** A roast chicken or brisket with a kugel, rice or roasted vegetables. See [kugel](/learn/kugel) and [brisket](/learn/brisket).
5. **Dessert.** Fruit, cake or cookies. If you serve meat for the main course, choose a dessert without dairy (pareve) so it is still kosher to eat after.

You do not need to make all five. Many hosts choose three and buy the rest.

## What to make ahead, what to buy

Chabad.org encourages hosts to keep the food simple. It says takeout, store-bought or potluck food is fine, as long as it is kosher and enjoyable. A good rule: **make one thing you love, buy the rest.**

- **Make on Tuesday or Wednesday:** soup (freeze it), kugel, cookies.
- **Make on Thursday:** a roast, a brisket or another main that reheats well, and vegetables.
- **Make on Friday morning:** salad, and put the chicken in the oven early enough to finish well before candle lighting.
- **Buy ready-made:** challah, fish, desserts. Bay Kosher, a project of Chabad of Lamorinda, offers kosher meat and poultry, groceries and holiday foods, delivered; see [Bay Kosher](/programs/bay-kosher).

## Make it kosher-friendly

If your home is kosher, keep dairy and meat apart, and check that your ingredients carry a reliable kosher certification. If you are just beginning, start with what you can and grow, and ask the Rabbi any questions about your kitchen. See [kosher kitchen](/learn/kosher-kitchen).

## Keep it warm without cooking on Shabbat

Cooking is not done on Shabbat, so the food should be fully cooked before candle lighting. Fully cooked, dry food may be kept warm on a covered stove (a blech) or a single-setting hot plate that was turned on before Shabbat. Soup and sauces have extra rules. Read [how to keep food warm on Shabbat](/learn/keeping-food-warm-on-shabbat) and ask the Rabbi about your setup. Check [this week's candle-lighting time](/shabbat-times) and finish cooking well before.

## Make the table, not the menu, the star

Chabad.org reminds hosts that guests remember how they were treated more than what they ate. Set the table early with [setting the Shabbat table](/learn/setting-the-shabbat-table), put phones away, and bring a few Torah thoughts or questions; see [Shabbat table conversation starters](/learn/shabbat-table-conversation-starters). Ask guests about allergies and preferences ahead of time, and tell them what you are serving.

## Common questions

### What do you serve for a Shabbat dinner?
Traditionally, challah, fish, chicken soup, a chicken or meat main, kugel and a dessert. Chabad.org says any enjoyable kosher food works.

### Can I serve takeout or store-bought food on Shabbat?
Yes, as long as it is kosher and brought home and set up before Shabbat begins. Chabad.org says store-bought and takeout food are fine for a Shabbat meal.

### What is an easy main dish for Shabbat dinner?
A roast chicken is among the simplest, because it can be cooked fully on Friday and kept warm. Brisket and a pot roast also reheat well.

### Can I serve ice cream after a meat Shabbat dinner?
Dairy is not eaten right after meat, so a dairy dessert is a problem. Serve a pareve dessert, such as fruit, or ask the Rabbi about how long to wait.

### How much should I cook for guests?
Plan a bit extra. Chabad.org suggests keeping a few extra servings in the freezer for unexpected guests.
