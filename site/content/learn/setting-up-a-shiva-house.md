When a family is about to sit shiva, there is a lot to arrange in very little time, often while still at the funeral. This checklist covers the practical side of getting a home ready. For what shiva means and how long it lasts, see our guide to [sitting shiva](/learn/shiva); for visitors, see [comforting mourners](/learn/comforting-mourners).

The best first step: ask a close friend or relative to be the "shiva coordinator," so the mourners do not have to manage anything themselves.

## Before the family returns from the cemetery

- **Water at the door.** Place a pitcher of water, a basin and paper towels outside the entrance. It is customary to wash hands after leaving the cemetery, before entering the house.
- **The meal of condolence.** The mourners' first meal after the funeral (seudat havraah) is traditionally prepared by others, not by the mourners. It often includes bread and round foods like hard-boiled eggs or lentils, a reminder that life goes in cycles.
- **Light the memorial candle.** A large seven-day candle, lit in memory of the soul, burns throughout shiva. Place it somewhere safe.
- **Cover the mirrors.** It is customary to cover mirrors in the house with sheets or paper during shiva.

## Seating and the room

- **Low seats for the mourners:** low stools, firm cushions or chairs with the legs shortened. The mourners sit lower than usual during shiva.
- **Regular chairs for visitors,** arranged so people can sit near the mourners and talk quietly.
- **Leave the front door unlocked or open** during visiting hours so mourners do not have to get up to greet people.

## Prayer services

- **Prayer books.** Borrow enough siddurim for a minyan and a few extra. Many prayer books have the special additions for a house of mourning.
- **A minyan schedule.** If the family will hold services at home, post the times for morning and evening prayers so people know when to come. Ten Jewish men over 13 are needed for the mourners to say [Kaddish](/learn/kaddish).
- **Ask the Rabbi.** Rabbi Labkowski helps arrange the minyan and the prayer books, and guides the family through each service. Some families also arrange for a Torah scroll for the Monday and Thursday readings; ask the Rabbi what fits your home.

## Visiting hours and food

- **Set visiting hours** and share them with the announcement, so mourners can also rest and eat in private.
- **A meal sign-up.** Friends usually bring meals for the family through the week. A simple online sign-up avoids too much food on one day and none on another. If the family keeps kosher, say so clearly, and ask that meals come from a kosher source.
- **Flowers are not traditional.** Many people prefer to give tzedakah in the person's memory.

## What the mourners do and don't do

The mourners' only job is to mourn and to receive comfort. They:

- Sit on the low seats and wear the torn garment from the funeral ([kriah](/learn/kriah)).
- Do not wear leather shoes, cook, host or serve guests.
- Do not work, shave, cut hair or bathe for pleasure.
- Usually stay home, apart from Shabbat, when public mourning is set aside.

## Common questions

### Who should coordinate the shiva house?
A close friend, neighbor or relative who is not one of the mourners. They receive meals, keep the minyan schedule and gently end visiting hours.

### Do we need to hold services at home?
It is a great comfort and lets the mourners say Kaddish without leaving the house. If it is hard to gather a minyan every time, call the Rabbi and he will help.

### Can shiva be held somewhere other than the deceased's home?
Yes. The custom is to sit shiva where the person lived, but often families sit at a mourner's home instead, or in more than one place. Ask the Rabbi about your situation.

### What do we do with the candle and coverings when shiva ends?
On the morning of the seventh day, after services, the mirrors are uncovered and the low seats put away. The family often takes a short walk around the block together, a sign of returning to daily life.
