Most people know about the morning prayers. Fewer know that a Jewish day has two more stops: **Mincha** in the afternoon and **Maariv** (also called Arvit) at night. They are short, they fit into almost any schedule, and they may be the easiest way to bring G-d into the middle of a busy day.

## Where they come from

The Talmud teaches that our three daily prayers were set by the Patriarchs: Abraham prayed in the morning, Isaac went out "to meditate in the field toward evening," which is Mincha, and Jacob prayed at night, which is Maariv (Berachot 26b). The sages also connected them to the daily offerings in the Holy Temple. Every time we pray Mincha, we are standing where Isaac stood.

## Mincha: a pause in the middle of the day

Mincha is prayed in the afternoon, from about half an hour after midday until sunset. Its heart is the [Amidah](/learn/amidah), the silent standing prayer, with a short psalm (Ashrei) before it and a closing prayer (Aleinu) after. On a weekday the whole thing takes about ten minutes.

The sages say Mincha deserves special care, because it comes in the middle of work and errands, when it is easiest to forget. Stopping to pray at that moment says: whatever I am doing, G-d is part of it. Many people pray Mincha in an office, a quiet corner at school, or in the car before heading home. (Not while driving.)

## Maariv: closing the day

Maariv is prayed after nightfall. It opens with the [Shema](/learn/shema) and its blessings, which thank G-d for bringing on the evening and recall the Exodus, and continues with the Amidah and Aleinu. On weekdays it also takes about ten minutes. Many communities pray Mincha and Maariv one right after the other, around sunset, so a person only comes out once.

## How short can it be?

Shorter than you think. If you are just beginning, or truly pressed for time:

- **Mincha:** the Amidah alone is the core. Even saying it slowly takes about five minutes.
- **Maariv:** the Shema (at least its first paragraph) and the Amidah.
- No siddur handy? Most prayer apps have both, and the Rabbi is happy to give you a pocket siddur.

A short prayer said with attention is worth more than a long one rushed through. Chassidic teaching stresses that the goal is not to get through the words but to stand before G-d for a few minutes.

## Alone or with a minyan?

Both Mincha and Maariv can be prayed alone, anywhere clean and quiet. With a [minyan](/learn/minyan) of ten, the prayer gains Kaddish, the repetition of the Amidah at Mincha, and the strength of a community praying together. Mourners saying [Kaddish](/learn/kaddish) need a minyan, which is one reason afternoon and evening minyanim matter so much: they are often the only time a working person can say Kaddish for a parent.

## How to begin

1. Pick one. Many people find Mincha the easiest to add, because it is the shortest.
2. Set a reminder on your phone for the same time each afternoon.
3. Open a siddur to the Mincha Amidah and say it, in Hebrew or English, at your own pace.
4. After a few weeks, add Maariv at night, or the [bedtime Shema](/learn/bedtime-shema) if that fits better.

For the bigger picture of the three daily prayers, see [daily prayers](/learn/daily-prayers).

## Common questions

### What time is Mincha?
From about half an hour after halachic midday until sunset, and the times shift through the year. The preferred time is the later afternoon. Our [Shabbat times](/shabbat-times) page shows sunset for Lafayette; for exact daily times, ask the Rabbi.

### Can I pray Mincha and Maariv together?
Yes. Many minyanim pray Mincha just before sunset and Maariv right after nightfall, with a short break or a few minutes of learning between them. If you are praying alone, you can do the same.

### I missed Mincha. Can I make it up?
Yes. Jewish law allows a prayer missed by accident to be made up by saying the Amidah twice at the next prayer, in this case at Maariv. Ask the Rabbi how it works in your situation.

### Do women pray Mincha and Maariv?
Women are obligated in daily prayer, and many women pray Mincha every day; some add Maariv as well. Any amount is a mitzvah. See [learning to pray](/learn/prayer).
