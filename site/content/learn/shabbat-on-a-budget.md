You can have a beautiful Shabbat on a budget, because the only things Shabbat truly requires at the table are candles, wine or grape juice, and two loaves of bread. Everything else, from fish to dessert, is a bonus. Chabad.org teaches that the meal should feel special, not that it must be expensive.

For families in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda, where the cost of living is high, this is good news. A simple Shabbat done every week is worth far more than an impressive one done once a year.

## What Shabbat actually needs

Here is the true minimum for Friday night:

- **Candles** and holders (any holders will do; see [Shabbat candles](/learn/shabbat-candles)).
- **Wine or kosher grape juice** for Kiddush (קִדּוּשׁ, the blessing that sanctifies the day), and a cup. See [Kiddush](/learn/kiddush).
- **Two whole loaves** of bread or challah, recalling the double portion of manna. See [challah](/learn/challah).

That is it. Chabad.org even says takeout, store-bought food or a potluck is fine, as long as it is kosher and enjoyable. The special feeling comes from the candles, the blessings, the people and the conversation.

## Plan the way families have for generations

Chabad.org notes that Jewish families have saved all week to buy fish for Shabbat for generations, so Shabbat food was never only for the wealthy. A few simple habits help:

1. **Decide the menu on Sunday or Monday**, and buy once instead of several times.
2. **Keep one "Shabbat" item in mind** as you shop, such as a good fruit or a recipe you want to try, and set it aside for Shabbat. This is an old Jewish idea of saving something special for the day.
3. **Choose traditional, inexpensive dishes.** Chicken soup, a roast chicken, kugel and cholent are all economical and made for Shabbat. See [chicken soup](/learn/jewish-chicken-soup), [kugel](/learn/kugel) and [cholent](/learn/cholent).
4. **Skip the extras.** One good main dish and two or three sides is plenty.

## Cook once, eat twice

A chicken or a big pot of soup made on Friday can become Shabbat lunch and Sunday dinner. Cholent, which simmers from Friday to Saturday lunch, is the classic example: one pot feeds a whole table. A blech or a hot plate and an urn for hot drinks are relatively inexpensive ways to serve warm food, and you may already own what you need. See [how to keep food warm on Shabbat](/learn/keeping-food-warm-on-shabbat).

## Invite guests, and let them bring something

Hosting does not have to cost more. Chabad.org suggests starting small, with one or two friendly people, and potluck is welcome. Ask each guest to bring a dish, a dessert or a bottle of grape juice. Invite early in the week, and tell guests the few Shabbat basics, like phones put away, so everyone is comfortable. More tips are in [hosting your first Shabbat dinner](/learn/hosting-your-first-shabbat-dinner) and [Shabbat guest guide](/learn/shabbat-guest-guide).

## Common questions

### How can I have Shabbat dinner on a budget?
Keep it simple: candles, grape juice, two loaves of bread and one or two homemade dishes. Chabad.org says store-bought, takeout or potluck food is fine, as long as it is kosher.

### What is the cheapest kosher Shabbat dinner?
Chicken soup, roast chicken, a vegetable and kugel or rice are among the most economical. The exact price depends on where you shop, so we cannot name one.

### Do I need to buy expensive candlesticks or a kiddush cup?
No. Any holders and any cup will do to begin. You can buy something nicer later. See our [Shabbat essentials](/learn/shabbat-essentials-for-your-home).

### Can I use grape juice instead of wine?
Yes. Kosher grape juice may be used for Kiddush, and many families use it every week.

### Can guests bring food to a Shabbat meal?
Yes, if it is kosher and arrives before Shabbat begins, since carrying and cooking are not done on Shabbat. If you keep a kosher home, tell guests what to bring, and ask the Rabbi about anything you are unsure of.
