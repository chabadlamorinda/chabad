Judaism teaches that caring for an aging parent is one of the most important mitzvot a person can do. The commandment to honor your father and mother does not end when you grow up; it often becomes most meaningful later in life, when parents need their children most.

## The mitzvah of honoring parents

The fifth of the [Ten Commandments](/learn/ten-commandments) is "Honor your father and your mother." It is one of the few mitzvot for which the Torah promises a reward: "so that your days may be long." The Talmud explains that honoring parents includes feeding them, giving them drink, dressing them, and helping them come and go. It also includes speaking to them with respect and protecting their dignity.

The Sages also teach that when a person honors their parents, G-d says, "It is as if I dwelled among them and they honored Me." Caring for a parent is a way of serving G-d.

Read more in our guide to [honoring parents](/learn/honoring-parents).

## What caring looks like in practice

- **Your time and presence.** Visits, phone calls and simply being there matter enormously. Loneliness is one of the hardest parts of aging.
- **Practical help.** Appointments, medications, shopping, paperwork and household needs.
- **Dignity.** Include your parent in decisions as much as possible. Speak gently, even when you disagree. Avoid treating them like a child.
- **Their Jewish life.** Help them keep the traditions that matter to them: Shabbat candles, kosher food, a mezuzah on their door, holidays with family, and a chance to hear the shofar or a Megillah reading.
- **Prayer.** Say [Tehillim](/learn/tehillim) for them, and ask that a [Mi Sheberach](/learn/mi-sheberach) be said in synagogue when they are unwell.

## Money and care

Jewish law says that, where possible, a parent's own funds pay for their needs, while the child gives personal effort and attention. If a parent cannot afford care, children should help according to their means. Every family's situation is different, so ask the Rabbi for guidance.

## When a parent has dementia

Honoring a parent applies even when they no longer recognize you or behave as they once did. Maimonides writes that a child should care for such a parent with patience as much as possible. He also teaches that if the child truly cannot cope, it is right to arrange for others to give the care that is needed.

This means that hiring caregivers or choosing a good care facility is not a failure. It can be the most loving and responsible way to honor a parent.

## Caring for yourself too

Caregivers often feel exhausted, guilty or alone. Judaism does not ask you to destroy your health, your marriage or your family. Accept help, share responsibilities with siblings, and take time to rest. Talking to the Rabbi or a counselor can make a real difference.

## Medical and end-of-life decisions

Serious medical choices, such as treatments near the end of life, raise important questions in Jewish law. Do not face them alone. Speak with the Rabbi early, and see our page on [end of life](/help/end-of-life) for guidance.

## Common questions

### Is it wrong to place a parent in a nursing home?
No. If a parent needs care you cannot provide safely at home, a good facility can be the right choice. Keep visiting, stay involved, and help them keep their Jewish life there.

### What if my parent was hard to live with or hurt me?
This is painful, and you are not alone. Jewish law has guidance for difficult relationships, and you may need to set healthy boundaries. Please speak privately with the Rabbi.

### How can I bring Jewish joy to a parent who is homebound?
Bring Shabbat candles, a challah, holiday foods or a short Torah thought. If you would like ideas or support, call us. We are happy to help.

### What if my siblings disagree about our parent's care?
Disagreements are common. Try to talk with respect and focus on your parent's wellbeing. If you need help, see our page on [resolving disputes](/help/disputes) or call the Rabbi.
