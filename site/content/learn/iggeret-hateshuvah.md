Iggeret HaTeshuvah (אגרת התשובה), "the Letter on Repentance," is a section of the Tanya, added to it by the Alter Rebbe, Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi. It teaches that teshuvah means returning to G-d with all your heart, and that it should be done with confidence and even joy, not with despair.

This guide gives a simple overview of what Chabad.org's texts of Chapters 1 and 11 say. For the general guide, see [teshuvah](/learn/teshuvah). To apply it to a fresh start, read [starting over after you fall](/learn/starting-over-after-you-fall).

## What is teshuvah, according to Iggeret HaTeshuvah?

Chapter 1 gives a plain definition. The mitzvah of teshuvah is to abandon sin: a person decides, sincerely and fully, not to go back to it and not to rebel against G-d again. At its heart, says the chapter, teshuvah means to return to G-d wholeheartedly, to serve Him and to keep His commandments.

So teshuvah starts with a decision of the heart. It is not first of all about a feeling or a ritual.

## Does teshuvah mean fasting?

Many people think so. Chapter 1 rejects the idea that fasting is the essence of teshuvah. The chapter explains that fasts and self-denial can have other purposes, such as helping avert a decree or compensating for repentance that came from fear instead of love. But the mitzvah itself is the return, and, where it applies, confession.

## Why confession?

The chapter explains that a sin has two parts: the act, and the pleasure and desire behind it. Regret, which is painful, works against the pleasure. Confession in words addresses the act itself. So the chapter treats verbal confession as part of teshuvah.

## Forgiveness comes with confidence

Chapter 11 teaches that a person should pray for forgiveness with complete certainty that G-d forgives once we ask. It points to the blessing in the daily Amidah that praises G-d as the One Who pardons abundantly. People may refuse to forgive someone who repeats a wrong; the Alter Rebbe says that G-d's mercy is infinite, so repetition makes no difference to Him.

The chapter is also clear that this is not a license to sin. It speaks of the person who sincerely decides to change, and says "whoever comes to purify himself is given assistance."

## Lower and higher teshuvah, and joy

Chapter 11 distinguishes two levels. Lower teshuvah is humility, contrition and self-examination. Higher teshuvah is the return that happens in prayer. Because worship should be done with joy, the Alter Rebbe advises moving the time of contrition to the previous midnight, so that we come to prayer joyful.

Can sadness and joy live in the same heart? According to the chapter, yes, because they come from different sources: contrition from the body and the animal soul, and joy from the G-dly soul. This matches the Tanya's teaching about [sadness and bitterness](/learn/sadness-versus-bitterness).

## "My sin is always before me"

The chapter reads this verse (Psalms 51) in a way that gives hope. The word it uses can mean "at a distance," so the awareness of past mistakes should stay in the background as humility, not as constant despair. It also helps a person take hardships with joy and avoid anger.

## Common questions

### What is Iggeret HaTeshuvah?
It is the "Letter on Repentance," a section added to the Tanya by the Alter Rebbe. It explains what teshuvah is and how to do it with confidence and joy.

### What does Tanya say teshuvah is?
It says teshuvah is to abandon sin and return to G-d with all your heart, to serve Him and keep His commandments.

### Does teshuvah require fasting?
Chapter 1 says fasting is not the essence of teshuvah. Ask the Rabbi about fasts or customs in your own situation.

### Is confession part of teshuvah?
According to Chapter 1, verbal confession is part of teshuvah.

### Can I feel joy and regret at the same time?
Chapter 11 teaches that contrition and joy can live together because they come from different parts of us.
