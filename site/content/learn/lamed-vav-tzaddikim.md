The Lamed-Vav Tzaddikim (לו צדיקים) are the "thirty-six righteous people" that the Talmud says exist in every generation. The tradition teaches that most righteous people are humble and unnoticed, which is why the best lesson is never to judge anyone by how they look.

## What the Talmud says

The sage Abaye teaches that the world has no fewer than thirty-six righteous people in every generation who greet the Divine Presence (Talmud, Sukkah 45b and Sanhedrin 97b). The number comes from the Hebrew letters lamed (ל, 30) and vav (ו, 6), which add up to 36, and appear together as the word "lo" in the verse "Happy are all who wait for Him" (Isaiah 30:18). Other sources give different numbers, so this is not one fixed count. Chabad.org points out that the popular idea of exactly thirty-six hidden people may blend several traditions, and for the deeper sources, ask the Rabbi.

## What is a tzaddik?

A tzaddik (צדיק) means a righteous person, someone whose life is guided by what is right. In the [Tanya](/learn/tanya), the Alter Rebbe describes the tzaddik as a person whose goodness has become natural, not a struggle. Most of us are "in-between," working on ourselves. For how this is understood in depth, ask the Rabbi.

## Why are they hidden?

The Talmud says most righteous people sit unnoticed in the back of the synagogue. A midrash compares them to stars, sometimes seen and sometimes hidden. Real righteousness does not announce itself: the more humble you are, the less you seek attention. This is the quality of [humility](/learn/humility) that Moses had.

## The hidden righteous before the Baal Shem Tov

According to Chabad history, before the Baal Shem Tov there was a circle of hidden righteous men who lived in self-chosen exile and wandered from town to town. They took humble jobs, like chopping wood or drawing water, so as not to be noticed, and quietly encouraged ordinary Jews and shared teaching with scholars. Their last leader was the Baal Shem Tov himself, who brought that inner light to everyone through Chassidic teaching. Read his story in [Baal Shem Tov](/learn/baal-shem-tov).

## The lesson: never judge anyone

The lesson of the 36 is simple. If the world is held up by people who look ordinary, then you can never know who someone really is. The woman at the checkout, the man who fixes your car, a quiet neighbor in Lafayette, Moraga or Orinda: any of them could be holy. So we judge others favorably, a mitzvah in itself; see [judging others favorably](/learn/judging-others-favorably). And each of us can be one of them: lamed-vav also reminds us that you do not need a title to be good.

## Common questions

### Who are the 36 hidden righteous people?
They are the thirty-six righteous people the Talmud says exist in every generation. Jewish tradition says they are humble, and often nobody knows who they are.

### What does Lamed-Vav mean?
Lamed-Vav means thirty-six: lamed is 30 and vav is 6 in the Hebrew alphabet.

### Is the story of the 36 hidden tzaddikim in the Torah?
The Torah does not state it. It comes from the Talmud (Sukkah 45b and Sanhedrin 97b), and later Jewish teachers expanded on it.

### Is the number 36 fixed?
Not exactly. Other sources mention different numbers, and Chabad.org notes that the popular legend combines separate traditions. Ask the Rabbi for sources.

### Can I be a Lamed-Vavnik?
Nobody knows who they are, and nobody should claim to be one. But the idea teaches that every person can do something good and hidden.

### What is the connection to the number 18 and chai?
Thirty-six is double eighteen, which is the value of chai (חי, "life"). See the [chai symbol](/learn/chai-symbol).
