Hachnasat orchim is the mitzvah of welcoming guests into your home, offering them food, rest and warmth. Jewish tradition teaches that welcoming guests is even greater than receiving the Divine presence.

## What is hachnasat orchim?

The phrase literally means "bringing in guests." It includes inviting people for meals, offering a place to stay, and making visitors feel at home. It is part of the broader mitzvah of [chesed](/learn/chesed), loving-kindness, and of loving your fellow as yourself.

## Abraham's open tent

The model of Jewish hospitality is Abraham. The Torah tells that three days after his circumcision, while recovering in the heat of the day, he sat at the entrance of his tent, looking for travelers (Genesis 18). When he saw three strangers, he ran to greet them, bowed, and served them a feast.

The Talmud learns from this that "welcoming guests is greater than receiving the Divine presence," since Abraham stepped away from speaking with G-d to greet them.

Tradition says Abraham's tent was open on all four sides, so guests could come in from any direction.

## Why it matters

- **Kindness.** It is one of the most direct ways to care for another person.
- **Connection.** A shared Shabbat meal builds friendships and community.
- **Example for children.** Kids who grow up with guests learn generosity.
- **Bringing Jews together.** Many people's first warm experience of Judaism is a Shabbat meal in someone's home.

## How to be a good host, step by step

1. **Invite warmly and specifically.** "Would you join us for Shabbat dinner this Friday?" is better than "come by sometime."
2. **Ask about food needs** such as allergies.
3. **Welcome guests at the door**, and make introductions.
4. **Serve food right away.** Abraham brought food quickly. A hungry guest is an uncomfortable guest.
5. **Include everyone.** Explain customs simply, and invite guests to share a thought or song.
6. **Be generous but humble.** Abraham said he would bring "a little bread" and then served a feast.
7. **Escort guests out.** The Sages teach that walking a guest to the door is a key part of the mitzvah.

## Being a good guest

Guests have responsibilities too. Express thanks, follow the host's customs, and include a blessing for the host in Grace after Meals, which has a special paragraph for this.

## Shabbat and holidays

[Shabbat meals](/learn/shabbat-meals) and holidays like Passover and Sukkot are the classic times for hosting. Many families make a point of inviting someone who might otherwise be alone.

## Common questions

### Do I need a big home or fancy food to host?
Not at all. A simple meal shared with warmth is more meaningful than an elaborate one. Guests remember how welcome they felt.

### Can I invite guests who drive on Shabbat?
Hosting Jews who are not yet observant is very important. There are ways to invite guests to stay over or to arrange things so they are welcome without encouraging them to drive. Ask the Rabbi for practical guidance.

### What if I keep kosher and my guest does not?
That is no problem. Your guest will enjoy your kosher food. Simply ask guests not to bring food into your kitchen, or suggest flowers or wine with a reliable kosher certification.

### Is hosting a mitzvah even for non-Jewish guests?
Kindness to all people is a Jewish value. Abraham welcomed every passerby, whoever they were.
