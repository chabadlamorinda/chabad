A kosher symbol, called a hechsher, is a mark on a package showing that a rabbinic agency supervised the product and certifies it as kosher. Learning to read these symbols makes shopping for kosher food simple, in almost any supermarket.

## Why packaged food needs a symbol

Today's processed foods contain many ingredients, flavorings and additives, and they are often made on shared equipment. Reading the ingredient list is not enough to know if a product is kosher. A hechsher (הֶכְשֵׁר, "approval") means a kosher agency has checked the ingredients, the factory and the production process, and inspects it regularly.

## Common kosher symbols

There are hundreds of kosher certifying agencies around the world. Some of the most widely seen in American stores include:

- **OU** (a U inside a circle)
- **OK** (a K inside a circle)
- **Star-K** (a K inside a star)
- **Kof-K** (a K inside the Hebrew letter kaf)
- **cRc** (a triangle with the letters cRc)

These are only examples. Standards vary between agencies, and some communities follow stricter standards for certain products. If you see a symbol you do not recognize, ask the Rabbi.

## What about a plain "K"?

The letter K by itself cannot be trademarked, so any company can print it. A plain K only means that the manufacturer says the product is kosher, without telling you who certifies it. Some plain-K products are well supervised and some are not. Ask the Rabbi before relying on one.

## Reading the letters next to the symbol

Letters and words next to the symbol tell you what kind of product it is:

- **D** or "Dairy": contains dairy ingredients
- **DE**: made on dairy equipment, but no dairy ingredients
- **M** or "Meat": contains meat or poultry, or made on meat equipment
- **Pareve** (or Parve): neither meat nor dairy
- **P**: kosher for Passover (it does **not** mean pareve)
- **F** (on some symbols): contains fish

Products marked DE are generally not eaten together with meat, though rules vary. Ask the Rabbi for specifics.

## Step by step: shopping kosher

1. **Look for a reliable symbol** on every packaged product.
2. **Check the letters** to know if it is dairy, meat or pareve.
3. **For meat, poultry and cheese,** buy only products with reliable kosher certification, since these require special supervision.
4. **Wine and grape juice** must always be certified kosher.
5. **For Passover,** look for products specifically certified kosher for Passover.
6. **When unsure,** do not guess. Ask.

## Products that usually do not need a symbol

Some foods are generally kosher without certification, such as fresh whole fruits and vegetables (checked for insects where needed), raw eggs from kosher birds, and many plain items. However, which plain items are acceptable can change with how they are processed, so ask the Rabbi for an up-to-date list.

## Common questions

### Does a kosher symbol mean the food was blessed?
No. A kosher symbol means trained supervisors checked that the product meets kosher law. Kosher has nothing to do with a rabbi blessing the food.

### Does "P" on a label mean pareve?
No. P means kosher for Passover. The product may be dairy, meat or pareve, so check for other markings or the word "pareve."

### Are all kosher symbols equally reliable?
No. Agencies differ in their standards, and some products from smaller agencies may not meet every community's standard. If you are unsure about a symbol, ask the Rabbi.

### Is "kosher-style" food kosher?
No. "Kosher-style" usually means traditional Jewish dishes like bagels or deli sandwiches, but it does not mean the food is kosher or supervised.
