Mar Ukva was a Talmudic sage who gave money to a poor neighbor in secret, and when the man came out to see who his benefactor was, Mar Ukva hid in an oven so as not to embarrass him. His reason, quoted in the Talmud (Ketubot 67b): it is better for a person to throw himself into an oven than to shame a colleague in public.

## The story

Chabad.org, in its translation of Maimonides' laws of gifts to the poor (Matnot Aniyim), retells it in a footnote: Mar Ukva used to leave money at a poor neighbor's doorstep every day. One day the man ran out to see who his benefactor was. Mar Ukva hid by going into an oven that was still warm but no longer burning. Asked why, he said that it is preferable for a person to cast himself into an oven than to embarrass a colleague in public.

Another Chabad.org article, "Hurtful Words," adds what the Talmud tells next: Mar Ukva's feet were burned, and he became known as "Mar Ukva with burnt feet." His wife hid with him and was not burned. The page explains her merit as feeding poor people directly, while Mar Ukva gave money.

## The lesson: giving without taking a person's dignity

The story is mainly about the person who receives. A gift can help, and it can also hurt, if it makes the receiver feel small. Mar Ukva was ready to go through a hot oven so that his neighbor never had to face his own need in front of someone else.

Maimonides, as Chabad.org presents him, ranks the levels of giving. The highest is helping someone before he falls into need, with a gift, a loan, a partnership or a job, so that he never has to ask. Giving where neither side knows the other is "an observance of the mitzvah for its sake alone." Partial secrecy, where only the giver or only the recipient knows, is also a level. For a how-to, see [Giving Anonymously](/learn/giving-anonymously).

## What we can take from it

- **Think about the receiver.** Ask how a gift will feel to the person who receives it.
- **Do not announce.** Giving quietly protects the receiver and keeps the giver humble.
- **Never shame.** The Talmud's principle applies far beyond charity. See [Lashon Hara](/learn/lashon-hara) for the Jewish teaching on careful speech.
- **Help the whole person.** The story's second half, about the wife who fed people directly, reminds us that kindness can be a meal, a visit or a call, not only a check. See [Chesed](/learn/chesed).

## Common questions

### Who was Mar Ukva?
He was a sage of the Talmud, known for his generosity and for the story of secret giving told in Ketubot 67b.

### Why did Mar Ukva hide in an oven?
So that the poor man he was helping would not discover who gave to him and be embarrassed. He said it is better to throw oneself into an oven than to shame a colleague in public.

### Is it better to give anonymously?
Secret giving is a very high level, and giving in a way that spares the receiver's dignity matters most. Ask the Rabbi about specific situations.

### What does the story teach about shame?
That embarrassing a person in public is extremely serious in Jewish teaching, and protecting someone's dignity is worth real effort.
