Judaism teaches that honesty in business is just as holy as prayer or Shabbat. The Torah has detailed laws about fair dealing, and the Talmud says that one of the first questions a person is asked after life is, "Were you honest in your business dealings?"

## Holiness at work

Jewish life is not only lived in the synagogue. The way we earn a living, treat customers and pay employees is part of serving G-d. A person who does business with integrity sanctifies G-d's name, showing the world what Torah values look like in practice.

## Key principles of Jewish business ethics

**Honest weights and measures.** "You shall have honest scales and honest weights" (Leviticus 19:36). Today this means accurate pricing, honest descriptions of products and truthful advertising.

**Do not deceive.** Jewish law forbids *geneivat da'at*, literally "stealing the mind," which means creating a false impression. Hiding a defect, using misleading marketing, or pretending a product is better than it is all count, even if no money is taken unfairly.

**Fair pricing.** The Torah forbids *ona'ah*, overcharging or underpaying far beyond the fair market price. It also forbids hurtful words, such as embarrassing someone in a negotiation.

**Pay workers on time.** "Do not keep a worker's wages with you overnight" (Leviticus 19:13). An employer must pay workers on time as agreed. A worker, in turn, must give honest effort during working hours.

**Do not give bad advice.** "Do not put a stumbling block before the blind" (Leviticus 19:14). This includes giving advice that benefits you while harming the person who trusts you.

**Keep your word.** A handshake and a promise matter. The Sages praised the person who keeps their word even when it costs them.

**Fair competition.** Jewish law protects people's livelihoods and discourages unfairly undercutting or interfering with someone else's established business. The details are complex, so ask the Rabbi about specific situations.

**Lending without interest.** The Torah forbids charging or paying interest on loans between Jews. Since modern business depends on financing, Jewish law developed a legal arrangement called a *heter iska* that structures a loan as a partnership. Ask the Rabbi if this affects you.

## Words matter too

Speaking badly about competitors, coworkers or clients, even when true, is [lashon hara](/learn/lashon-hara). Jewish ethics asks us to guard our speech at work as much as anywhere.

## Giving from what you earn

Jewish tradition teaches that our income is a blessing from G-d, and part of it belongs to those in need. Many give [maaser](/learn/maaser), a tenth of their earnings, to [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah).

## Common questions

### Is it okay to negotiate a good price?
Yes. Negotiation is normal and fair. What Jewish law forbids is deception, pressure that crosses into unfairness, and prices far beyond the market.

### What if I made a mistake in my favor?
Return the money or correct the error. Being honest when no one would notice is a true sanctification of G-d's name.

### Should I keep my business closed on Shabbat?
Ideally, yes. Shabbat is a gift of rest for owners and workers. If you own a business, ask the Rabbi about the options, including arrangements for partnerships.

### How should Jewish business partners resolve disputes?
Judaism encourages resolving disputes through a rabbinical court or arbitration based on Jewish law, rather than going straight to secular court. See our page on [resolving disputes](/help/disputes).
