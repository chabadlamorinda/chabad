To follow along in synagogue, listen for the page number (it is often announced or posted), look for the landmark prayers that everyone recognizes, and keep your finger on the line when the congregation moves. If you lose your place, quietly ask a neighbor, "What page are we on?" People are glad to help, and it is completely fine to read slowly or to skip ahead.

## What a siddur is and why it feels big

The siddur (סידור) is the Jewish prayer book. The word comes from *seder*, "order," because the prayers are arranged in a set sequence. A siddur holds the prayers for weekdays, Shabbat and festivals, and many editions add an English translation with simple instructions. Chabad communities often use *Siddur Tehillat Hashem*, which follows the Chassidic text; different editions differ slightly in wording, and these versions are called *nuschaot*. If a page looks different from your neighbor's, they may simply be using another edition.

## Step by step: how to follow along

1. **Pick a siddur with English.** Prayer books with English are available to borrow at Chabad of Lamorinda services; just ask for one when you come in. Pages with Hebrew on one side and English on the other make it easy to see the meaning.
2. **Find out the page.** Many synagogues announce the page, or someone points it out. Listen for it when the prayer leader changes sections.
3. **Open to the right section.** The weekday, Shabbat and holiday services each have their own place in the book. Ask which one you need today.
4. **Read at your pace.** You do not have to keep up with the leader. If you read slowly, you can say a smaller part with understanding instead of racing through the whole thing. This is welcomed, not frowned on.
5. **Watch the room.** When people stand, sit or bow, you will see the moment has come.

## Landmarks you can listen for

Once you know a few signposts, the service stops feeling like a blur. On Shabbat morning, listen for:

- **The morning blessings and psalms** at the start, ending with a call to prayer by the leader. See [morning blessings](/learn/morning-blessings) and [Ashrei](/learn/ashrei).
- **Barchu**, where the leader calls the congregation to bless G-d and everyone answers. Read about [Barchu](/learn/barchu).
- **The Shema**, said aloud together, usually with a hand over the eyes. See [Shema](/learn/shema).
- **The Amidah**, the silent standing prayer. People pray quietly and then take three steps back at the end. See [Amidah](/learn/amidah).
- **The Torah reading**, when the scroll is taken from the ark.
- **Aleinu**, near the end, said standing. See [Aleinu](/learn/aleinu).

For the full order of events, see [Shabbat Morning Services Explained](/learn/shabbat-morning-services-explained).

## Tips for when you feel lost

- **It is normal.** Even people who have prayed for years sometimes lose their place.
- **Use the translation.** Reading the English meaning is a real way of praying. Learn more in [Can I Pray in English?](/learn/can-i-pray-in-english).
- **Say "Amen."** When you hear a blessing, you can answer "Amen" even if you are lost on the page.
- **Ask the Rabbi.** If you want to know exactly where things are, ask beforehand and he will show you.

## Common questions

### How do I follow along in synagogue?
Listen for the announced page number, keep your finger on the line, and watch the people around you. Ask a neighbor if you get lost.

### How do I find the right page in a siddur?
Use the page number announced or posted, or look for the title of the service in the table of contents. The weekday, Shabbat and holiday services are in different sections.

### What if I cannot read Hebrew?
Use a siddur with English translation or transliteration, or simply read the English. You can also listen and say "Amen." See [Hebrew Reading for Adults](/learn/hebrew-reading-for-adults).

### Do I need to keep up with the prayer leader?
No. Pray at your own pace. Saying less with attention is better than rushing through.

### Why do some siddurim look different?
Different editions follow slightly different traditions, and the wording varies a little between them. Chabad communities commonly use Siddur Tehillat Hashem.

### Is it all right to take a siddur home?
Many people want to. Prayer books with English are available to borrow at services. Ask the Rabbi about it, or call 925-384-7242.
