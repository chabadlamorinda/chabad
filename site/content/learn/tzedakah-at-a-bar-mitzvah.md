On the day of his Bar Mitzvah, a Jewish boy is encouraged to give tzedakah from his own money, ideally to an institution that teaches Jewish children, and his parents give as well. Gift money he receives is generally subject to maaser, so many families set aside a tenth for tzedakah.

## The first tzedakah of an adult

When a boy becomes Bar Mitzvah (בַּר מִצְוָה, "son of the commandment") at thirteen, he begins to be responsible for the mitzvot. The Bar Mitzvah custom is that he should give tzedakah from his own money on the day of his Bar Mitzvah, in the morning before Shacharit (the morning prayer) and before Mincha (the afternoon prayer). If he usually gives at those times, he should give more on that day.

According to the same guide, the tzedakah should preferably go to an institution involved with *chinuch* (חִנּוּךְ, Jewish education). If the Bar Mitzvah falls on Shabbat, when money is not handled, he gives on the Friday before and on the Sunday after, and his parents should do the same. See [Bar Mitzvah](/learn/bar-mitzvah).

For a Bat Mitzvah, many families follow the same spirit: a girl's first tzedakah as a young adult is a beautiful beginning. Ask the Rabbi how your family would like to mark it. See [Bat Mitzvah](/learn/bat-mitzvah).

## What about the gift money?

Cash gifts are a common part of the celebration. Jewish law teaches that maaser (a tenth) is usually given from all income, including gifts. Cash gifts are normally subject to maaser, but if money is given for a specific purpose, such as tuition or another basic need, it is exempt. If the giver is not particular about how it is spent, maaser should be taken. It is a good idea for a young person who has just received a bar mitzvah check to choose a charity for the tithe. If you are unsure about a particular gift, ask the Rabbi.

The Torah invites us to test G-d with maaser (Malachi 3:10), and the Talmud (Taanit 9a) teaches: give a tenth so that you will become rich. The Torah and Hashem promise success for all giving; maaser is unique because the promise is stated clearly and we are invited to test it, and the Rebbe taught that we may test G-d with all tzedakah. For any personal question, ask the Rabbi. For a young person this can be the first lesson in how giving and blessing go together. See [Maaser](/learn/maaser) and [Children, Money and Tzedakah](/learn/children-money-and-tzedakah).

## Giving in multiples of 18

Many families give gifts and tzedakah in multiples of 18, the numerical value of *chai* (חַי, "life"). The custom is that this blesses both the giver and the receiver with a good, long life, and that the Rebbe sometimes advised other amounts: the main thing is to give as much as one can. See [Gifts in Multiples of 18](/learn/gifts-in-multiples-of-18).

## The family gives, too

Parents can give in honor of the day, to a Jewish school, a synagogue or a cause close to the child's heart. Because Jewish custom is that the parents should also give when the Bar Mitzvah falls on Shabbat, giving is clearly meant to be a family act. For ideas about a project, see [Bat Mitzvah Projects](/learn/bat-mitzvah-projects).

## Common questions

### Does a bar mitzvah boy give tzedakah?
Yes. The custom is that he should give tzedakah from his own money on the day of his Bar Mitzvah, before the morning and afternoon prayers, preferably to an institution involved with Jewish education.

### What if the Bar Mitzvah is on Shabbat?
Money is not handled on Shabbat, so he gives the Friday before and the Sunday after, and his parents do the same.

### Do we owe maaser on bar mitzvah gift money?
Cash gifts are normally subject to maaser, unless given for a specific purpose such as tuition. Ask the Rabbi about your situation.

### Does the same custom apply to a Bat Mitzvah?
Many families follow the same spirit for a girl. Ask the Rabbi how your family would like to do it.

### How much should we give?
The main thing is to give as much as one can, and many give in multiples of 18. Giving warmly matters more than the sum.
