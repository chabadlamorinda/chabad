Simchat Beit Hashoevah (שמחת בית השואבה, "the celebration of the water-drawing house") is the great Sukkot celebration with music and dancing, recalling the joyous festivities held in the Holy Temple in Jerusalem during Sukkot. Our sages said that anyone who did not see it "has never seen joy in his life," and Chabad communities everywhere bring it back with live music and dancing.

## What was the water-drawing celebration?

Chabad.org describes how, from the time of King Solomon's Temple until its destruction by the Romans, Jews from across the land gathered in Jerusalem for an eight-day Sukkot celebration centered on the Temple Mount, with music and dancing day and night.

The spectacle was remarkable. Huge candelabras lit up Jerusalem. Elders danced and performed acrobatics. Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel juggled eight flaming torches, and none of them touched.

## Why is it called the "water-drawing"?

On Sukkot, water from the Siloam spring (the *Mayan Hashiloach*) was poured on the altar along with wine. The celebration takes its name from this rite.

The Talmud teaches in the name of Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi that the word *Shoevah* also hints at the "drawing" of divine inspiration. The prophet Jonah is said to have received prophecy at the celebration, and from this we learn that the Divine Presence rests on a person only through joy. See [the Book of Jonah](/learn/book-of-jonah).

## What does water teach about joy?

Water takes the shape of whatever holds it and flows to the lowest place. Our teachers say this expresses simple acceptance and humility, a joy that doesn't depend on being clever or having a reason. We are used to joy with a cause, such as a birthday or good news. This kind of joy comes from simply being connected to G-d. For more, see [simcha](/learn/simcha).

## The Rebbe and the streets

Without the Levite orchestra of the Temple, the Rebbe, Rabbi Menachem M. Schneerson, noted that we can celebrate with our own voices. In 1980, in Brooklyn, a crowd began dancing on the first night of Sukkot, and it grew into a street procession that lasted until dawn. The Rebbe said, "If you want to find G-d this Sukkot, you'll find Him dancing on the streets." Chabad communities around the world have celebrated Simchat Beit Hashoevah ever since.
