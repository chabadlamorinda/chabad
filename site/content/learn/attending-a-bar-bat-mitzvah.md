A Bar or Bat Mitzvah is a thirteen-year-old boy's or twelve-year-old girl's coming of age: the day they become responsible for the mitzvot as a Jewish adult. The family has often prepared for a year or more, and your presence is part of the gift. Here is what to expect.

## The service

Most Bar Mitzvahs are on Shabbat morning, when the Torah is read; some are on a Monday, Thursday or Rosh Chodesh, when it is also read. The service is long, often two to three hours, and people come and go quietly. You will be handed a prayer book (siddur) and a Chumash (the Torah in book form) with English alongside; page numbers are announced. Stand when the congregation stands, sit when it sits. See [your first time at a synagogue](/learn/first-time-at-synagogue).

In an Orthodox synagogue men and women sit separately; men and boys wear a kippah (provided at the door).

## The aliyah and the Torah reading

The heart of the morning is the Torah. The scroll is carried from the ark with singing, and people reach out to touch and kiss it. It is read in sections, and before each one a man is called up (*aliyah*, "going up") to say the blessings. The Bar Mitzvah boy's aliyah is the moment everyone has waited for: he says the blessings, often reads from the Torah himself in the ancient melody, and sometimes the haftarah (a reading from the Prophets) too. His father says a short blessing, and the boy may give a speech. See [the Torah reading](/learn/torah-reading) and [Bar Mitzvah](/learn/bar-mitzvah).

A Bat Mitzvah is celebrated differently in different communities. In Chabad and other traditional communities a girl often marks it with a celebration at which she shares words of Torah and a project or mitzvah she has taken on; see [Bat Mitzvah](/learn/bat-mitzvah) and [Bat Mitzvah projects](/learn/bat-mitzvah-projects).

## Candy throwing

In many communities, when the boy finishes his aliyah, the congregation showers him with soft candies for a sweet life, and the children scramble to collect them. Wait for the cue and aim gently. Then everyone sings "*Siman tov u'mazal tov*."

## After the service: Kiddush and the celebration

After the service there is a Kiddush: a blessing over wine and a festive lunch for everyone. The main party may be that day, that night after Shabbat ends, or another day, and ranges from a simple lunch to a big evening with music; the invitation will tell you which.

## What to wear

For men a suit or jacket and a kippah; for women a dress or skirt to the knee or below with covered shoulders; in traditional congregations married women often cover their hair. For a party, follow the invitation.

## Phones and cameras on Shabbat

If the celebration is on Shabbat, there is no photography, writing or phone use in the synagogue or at a traditional family's celebration. Keep your phone silent and out of sight; families often hire a photographer for a weekday instead. How you get there is your own matter; you are welcome however you arrive. See [Shabbat](/learn/shabbat).

## Gifts

A check or gift card in a multiple of 18 ($36, $54, $180), the numerical value of *chai*, "life," is the classic gift. Meaningful Jewish gifts are a Kiddush cup, a siddur, books, a tallit for a boy, candlesticks for a girl, or a donation in the child's name. Gifts are not handled on Shabbat itself; give them at the party or mail them. See [Jewish gift ideas](/learn/jewish-gift-ideas).

## What to say

"Mazal tov!" to the child, parents, grandparents and siblings. To the parents and grandparents you can add, "May you have much *nachas*."

## Common questions

### Do I have to be Jewish to attend?
No. Guests of every background are welcome at the service and the celebration. Follow the people around you and enjoy.

### Can I bring my children?
Children are usually welcome at the service, and a Kiddush is a family event. Check the invitation for the party. See [the kids' corner](/kids).

### What if I arrive late?
It happens all the time. Enter quietly, take a seat, and try not to walk in during the silent prayer (when everyone stands quietly) or the rabbi's speech.

### How is the service different for a girl?
In traditional congregations a girl's celebration is held apart from the Torah service, often with a speech from her and a festive meal. When in doubt, ask the family, or [ask the Rabbi](/help/bar-bat-mitzvah).
