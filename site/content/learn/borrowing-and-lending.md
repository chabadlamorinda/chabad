Judaism considers lending money to someone in need a great mitzvah, in some ways greater than giving charity, and repaying a loan on time is also a mitzvah. The Torah's approach is to lend generously, write it down, and never pressure a borrower who truly cannot pay.

Neighbors, relatives and friends in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda often help one another with loans, so it helps to know the Jewish guidelines.

## Why lending is so special

The Torah says not to "close your hand from your needy brother" (Deuteronomy 15:7-8) and to lend what he needs. According to Chabad.org, interest-free loans are a Torah obligation, and lending is considered greater than charity because borrowing is less embarrassing than accepting a gift. A timely loan can also keep a person from falling into poverty.

Communities are encouraged to set up free loan funds, called [gemachs](/learn/gemach-interest-free-loans). Charging interest to a fellow Jew is a serious prohibition, which is explained in the guide on [ribbit](/learn/ribbit). Ask the Rabbi about any particular arrangement.

## Who comes first

Chabad.org lists an order of priority: family first, then people in your city, then Jews in Israel, then Jews elsewhere. Lending to a wealthy person with a real need is also a mitzvah, but the poor come first. Poor non-Jews may also receive loans. One is asked to lend up to a fifth of one's wealth, no more, and only when confident the borrower will repay. If it would put your own family in difficulty, ask the Rabbi.

## Write it down

The Torah encourages recording loans in writing, even between close friends. This protects the relationship, not just the money. A short note with the amount, date and repayment plan, signed by both sides, prevents forgotten details and hurt feelings later.

1. State the amount and the date.
2. Agree on a repayment date or schedule.
3. Both sign, and each keeps a copy.
4. If plans change, update the note.

## The borrower's duties

Repaying is a mitzvah, and the Torah takes a debt seriously. Chabad.org lists several obligations for borrowers:

- Do not gamble the money on risky ventures without telling the lender.
- Do not waste it on non-essentials so that you cannot repay.
- Repay when it is due. If you cannot, use available resources, and put earnings and gifts toward the debt.
- If you are in debt, avoid spending on luxuries.
- Keep the lender informed. If you cannot repay on time, say so honestly.

## The lender's duties

A lender may not pressure a borrower known to be unable to repay, or even act in a way that reminds him of the debt, such as walking past him pointedly. A debtor cannot be forced to work, but should do what he can. Collateral may be requested, but collecting it follows specific rules, usually through a rabbinical court. For a real dispute, do not take matters into your own hands: ask the Rabbi.

## Lending to family and friends

Money can strain a good relationship. Before lending, ask yourself: could I afford for this to be a gift? Many people lend only what they can afford to lose, and are relieved when it is repaid. If the loan is hard to get back, talk calmly and kindly, and ask the Rabbi to help mediate, often preserves both friendship and money. See [money worries](/learn/money-worries) if a loan is part of a bigger squeeze.

## Common questions

### Is it a mitzvah to lend money?
Yes. Interest-free loans to those in need are a great mitzvah. Chabad.org notes that lending is considered greater than charity because it protects the borrower's dignity.

### Do I have to repay a loan on time?
Yes. Repayment is a mitzvah. If you truly cannot repay on time, inform the lender, do what you can and ask the Rabbi for guidance.

### What if a friend owes me money and cannot pay?
The Torah forbids pressuring a borrower who cannot repay. Be patient and kind, and ask the Rabbi how to proceed.

### Should I write a loan down?
Yes. The Torah recommends writing loans down, even between friends, to protect both sides.

### Can I charge interest?
Charging interest to a fellow Jew is forbidden by the Torah, with details and permitted arrangements. Read the guide on ribbit and ask the Rabbi before arranging anything.
