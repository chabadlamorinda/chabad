An upsherin (Yiddish for "shearing") is a boy's first haircut, traditionally held at age three. It is a joyful celebration that marks the start of his formal Jewish education, when he begins wearing a kippah and tzitzit and learning the letters of the Hebrew alphabet. In Hebrew it is also called a chalakah.

## Why wait until age three?

The custom draws on a verse in the Torah comparing a person to a tree (Deuteronomy 20:19). The Torah says that the fruit of a young tree may not be eaten for its first three years. In the same way, a child's hair is left to grow during his first three years, and then the first "harvest" is a time of celebration.

There is also a lovely idea here: until age three, a child mostly absorbs. From three, he begins to learn actively. The upsherin is the moment when a toddler becomes a student.

## What happens at an upsherin

Most upsherins include these elements:

1. **Cutting the hair.** Guests are invited to snip a small lock of the child's hair. Often a Rabbi or respected person makes the first cut.
2. **Leaving the peyot.** The hair at the sides of the head (peyot, sidelocks) is left uncut, as the Torah instructs.
3. **Kippah and tzitzit.** The boy begins wearing a kippah and a small tallit, or [tzitzit](/learn/tzitzit), every day.
4. **Learning the aleph-bet.** The child is shown the Hebrew letters, sometimes on a card covered with honey, so that his first taste of Torah is sweet. Learn more in our [Hebrew alphabet](/learn/hebrew-alphabet) guide.
5. **Tzedakah.** Many families give charity, sometimes the weight of the hair in coins, and the child places coins in a tzedakah box.
6. **Blessings and food.** The child may recite simple blessings, and everyone enjoys a festive meal or treats.

## When and where

The upsherin is usually held on or soon after the child's third Hebrew birthday. Use our [Hebrew date converter](/tools/hebrew-date) to find it. If the birthday falls during a period when haircuts are not customary, such as the Omer period between Passover and Shavuot or the Three Weeks in summer, the celebration may be moved. Ask the Rabbi about the right date.

Some families hold an upsherin at home, some at a synagogue, and some travel to Meron in Israel on Lag BaOmer, a traditional upsherin day.

## Common questions

### Do girls have an upsherin?

The haircut ceremony is for boys. Many families mark a daughter's third birthday in a special way too, often by beginning to light a Shabbat candle each Friday, which Chabad encourages for girls from a young age.

### Is upsherin a requirement?

It is a cherished custom rather than a biblical commandment. It is widely practiced, especially among Chassidic and Sephardic families, and is a beautiful way to begin a child's Jewish journey.

### Who cuts the hair?

Parents, grandparents, a Rabbi and guests usually each take a small snip. Afterward, many families take the child to a barber to finish the haircut neatly, leaving the peyot.

### What should we do with the cut hair?

There is no strict rule. Some families keep a lock as a keepsake. Others dispose of it respectfully.
