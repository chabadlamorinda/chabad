Yes, a Jew can have doubts about G-d, and doubt does not make you any less Jewish. Chabad teaching treats honest questions as part of the journey: the very name Israel means one who wrestles with G-d, and a Jew who struggles is still a Jew who belongs.

## Doubt is human, and very old

Jewish history is full of people who argued with Heaven. Abraham questioned G-d's justice, Moses asked "why have You done evil to this people?", and the Talmud is built on questions. The name Israel itself is explained as "one who wrestles with G-d." Chabad.org describes real faith as often including this kind of struggle.

You are in good company. Asking is not the opposite of faith. In one answer from the Rebbe, a person who had doubts was told that even to question G-d shows some belief is already there, because you must accept something in order to question it.

## Faith is not only an argument

Many people expect faith to be won by a perfect proof. Chabad teaching describes it more like a relationship, and like the soul itself: we know we have one not by seeing it but by feeling it. Faith is an inner knowing that is already within every Jew, sometimes buried under busyness, hurt or disappointment.

The approach recommended is to give that inner voice room to speak, and to feed it with real information about G-d and about ourselves.

## Four ways to strengthen faith

1. **Learn.** Read and study. Start with [belief in G-d](/learn/belief-in-g-d) and [why be Jewish](/learn/why-be-jewish).
2. **Pray, even briefly.** Talk to G-d honestly, including your doubts and anger. See the guide on [hope in hard times](/learn/hope-in-hard-times).
3. **Do.** Chabad's approach is to invite a Jew, even one who says "I don't believe," to do a mitzvah first, and talk about faith afterwards. Light Shabbat candles, give tzedakah, put up a mezuzah. Action often opens the heart.
4. **Talk it over.** Share your thoughts with family, friends and a teacher you trust.

## When suffering is the reason

Many doubts begin with pain: an illness, a loss, an injustice. These are the hardest questions, and no short answer is honest. Chabad teaching says that when people cause suffering, our anger belongs at what people did, not at G-d. For tragedy that no one caused, there are no easy words, and the guide on [hope in hard times](/learn/hope-in-hard-times) is a gentle place to begin. Ask the Rabbi to sit with you; he will listen before he speaks.

## Common questions

### Can a Jew have doubts about G-d?
Yes. Judaism has always made room for questions, and the name Israel means one who wrestles with G-d. Doubt can be the beginning of a deeper faith.

### What if I don't believe in G-d at all?
You are still Jewish and still welcome. Chabad's approach is to start with a mitzvah and with learning, and to take your questions seriously rather than push you.

### How do I strengthen my faith in G-d?
Study, pray, do mitzvot and talk about your faith with people you trust. Chabad teaching says faith grows through all four, much like a muscle that is used.

### Is it wrong to be angry at G-d?
Jewish tradition has many examples of honest arguing with G-d. Bring your anger into prayer instead of hiding it; if it is heavy, ask the Rabbi.
