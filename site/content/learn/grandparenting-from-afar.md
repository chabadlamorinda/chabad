Many Jewish grandparents today live a plane ride away from their grandchildren. Children move for work, for school, for love, and the Friday night table that once held three generations is now spread across time zones. It hurts, and it is also an invitation. A grandparent who cannot be there for every soccer game can still be the person who gives the grandchildren their Jewish memories. Here is how.

## Be the Shabbat voice

Pick a weekly moment that is yours. For many families it is Friday afternoon, before Shabbat begins in the grandchildren's time zone. A short video call: light your [Shabbat candles](/learn/shabbat-candles) together if the hour allows, or simply say the blessing with them, and then bless each grandchild with the words parents use on Friday night. See [blessing your children](/learn/blessing-your-children); grandparents have always given this blessing too, and a child who hears "May G-d bless you and keep you" from Grandma every week carries her voice for life. If Friday is hard, Saturday night after Shabbat ends, with [Havdalah](/learn/havdalah), works just as well.

Keep it short and regular. Five minutes every week beats an hour once a month, especially with small children.

## Send the holidays

A box that arrives before a holiday says "I am part of this" better than any phone call. Ideas that need no special skill:

- **Chanukah:** a small [menorah](/learn/chanukah-menorah) of their own, candles, a dreidel and some gelt, plus a recording of you singing the blessings. Then light together on video. See [Chanukah for kids](/learn/chanukah-for-kids).
- **Purim:** [mishloach manot](/learn/mishloach-manot) for the grandchildren, and a costume piece.
- **Passover:** a children's Haggadah with your name inside, and the family [Four Questions](/learn/four-questions) practiced over video the week before.

Ask the parents first about timing and what the children already have, so the box adds to the family's celebration rather than competing with it.

## Tell the family stories

Grandparents hold what no book holds: who the great-grandparents were, where they came from, what they survived, what made them laugh. Children who know their family's story feel rooted, and Jewish identity grows in that soil. Record yourself telling one story a month, or tell them live and let the child ask questions. Explain whom each grandchild's [Hebrew name](/learn/hebrew-names) honors; a child named for a great-grandmother wants to know who she was. Old photos and a family tree you build together are part of this. See [Jewish genealogy](/learn/jewish-genealogy).

## Learn together

A grandchild in Hebrew school is proud to teach Grandpa the letters. Read the week's [parsha](/learn/weekly-parsha) story together, practice a blessing, or do a Jewish craft at the same time in two kitchens. If you are learning Judaism yourself later in life, say so; "we are learning together" is a gift to a child, not a weakness.

## Make visits count

When you do come, plan around Jewish time. Arrive for a Shabbat and be there for Friday night; come for a holiday rather than a random weekend; sit together at a Seder. Visit the grandchildren's synagogue or Chabad center and meet the people who know them. A visit that holds a Jewish moment becomes a memory the child can return to all year.

## Stay in the parents' corner

Grandparents from afar do best when they strengthen the parents rather than compete with them. Praise what the parents are doing, ask before sending big gifts or adding traditions, and never criticize their level of observance in front of the children. Shalom in the family is itself a Jewish lesson. See [Jewish grandparents](/learn/jewish-grandparents) for more on this role.

## Common questions

### Can we use video on Shabbat itself?
Shabbat-observant families do not use phones or video on Shabbat, so call before candle lighting on Friday, or after Havdalah on Saturday night. If the family's practice differs from yours, talk it over kindly and ask the Rabbi for guidance.

### My grandchildren are being raised with less Judaism than I would like. What can I do?
Give without pressure. Blessings, stories, holiday boxes and your own joy in Judaism plant seeds; criticism closes doors. Many adults trace their Jewish return to a grandparent who simply loved them Jewishly.

### What if my grandchild's other grandparents are not Jewish?
Be warm toward them and toward their traditions, and quietly be the family's Jewish home base. Children can hold a lot of love. If questions of identity come up, see [who is a Jew](/learn/who-is-a-jew) and talk with the Rabbi.
