A minhag (מִנְהָג) is a Jewish custom: a practice that the Jewish people took on over the generations, which then becomes binding like law. The Talmud's phrase is "the custom of Israel is Torah," meaning that a custom accepted by the community carries real weight, even though it is not written in the Torah itself.

## Halacha and minhag: what is the difference?

Halacha (הֲלָכָה) is Jewish law, derived from the Torah and the Talmud. See [what is halacha](/learn/what-is-halacha) and the [Code of Jewish Law](/learn/code-of-jewish-law). A minhag is a practice that grew up within a community or family, often to protect or beautify a mitzvah, and which the people accepted upon themselves.

Some examples of customs:

- **Slanting the mezuzah.** Chabad.org explains that Rashi and his grandson Rabbeinu Tam disagreed about whether a mezuzah should hang vertically or horizontally. Ashkenazi Jews hang it at a slant, which fits both views. See [mezuzah](/learn/mezuzah).
- **The first haircut at three.** Many families give a boy his first haircut at age three, leaving the sidelocks. See [upsherin](/learn/upsherin).
- **Holiday foods.** Apples and honey on Rosh Hashanah, latkes on Chanukah, and the foods your grandmother made are all customs, and they carry memory.
- **Not eating legumes on Passover.** Some communities avoid rice and beans, others do not. See [kitniyot](/learn/kitniyot).

## Why customs matter so much

Chabad.org, in an article by Tzvi Freeman, gives a beautiful image. The written Torah is like a seed, and the Jewish people are the soil, with G-d as the gardener. When the community, scholars and ordinary people together, takes on a practice grounded in their understanding of Torah, it shows that the seed grew. The article says that accepted customs have the import of law because the Torah itself recognizes them as law.

That is why customs are not dropped lightly. Throwing one away would be like saying that a generation's work did not matter. It is also why a custom keeps its force even when the original reason is forgotten. Customs also bind us to those who came before. When you light the candles the way your grandmother did, you are standing with her.

## Different communities, different customs

Jews have lived in many places, and each community developed its own customs: prayer melodies and text (nusach), foods, and ways of marking holidays. Ashkenazi and Sephardi communities in particular have distinct customs, which you can read about in [Ashkenazi and Sephardi](/learn/ashkenazi-and-sephardi). Both are completely valid. Jewish law gives the principle that people keep the custom of their own community, and a person who visits another place usually follows where they are, but this has details, so ask the Rabbi in a specific case.

At Chabad of Lamorinda, every Jew is welcome, whatever customs their family followed.

## What if I do not know my family's customs?

Many Jews in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda grew up without much Jewish tradition, or come from families whose customs were lost through immigration, loss or assimilation. That is nothing to be ashamed of.

Here is a gentle way to begin:

1. **Ask your relatives.** An older aunt or grandfather may remember what was done on Friday night or on a holiday.
2. **Write it down.** A custom you record is a custom you can pass on.
3. **Ask the Rabbi.** If nothing is known, the Rabbi can guide you; many people begin with the Chabad customs, which are widely taught and clearly written. Where your family's custom is known, he will usually encourage you to keep it. Ask about your own case.
4. **Start small.** One new custom, kept consistently, is better than ten you do not keep.

## Taking on a custom as an adult

You can adopt a new custom, such as lighting Shabbat candles or putting up a mezuzah, at any age. This is how many customs began. For personal questions about changing or dropping a family custom, ask the Rabbi. Teenagers and young adults trying to understand their identity may also like [Jewish identity for teens](/learn/jewish-identity-for-teens).

## Common questions

### What does minhag mean?
Minhag means "custom." It is a practice that Jewish communities or families have taken on over time, and which carries weight like law.

### What does "the custom of Israel is Torah" mean?
It is a saying from our sages, meaning that a custom accepted by the Jewish people has the force of Torah law. Ask the Rabbi about how it applies in a specific case.

### Is a custom the same as a law?
No, but accepted customs are taken very seriously and are not dropped lightly. Chabad.org explains that they have the import of law.

### Should I keep my family's customs?
Generally yes, and Jewish tradition values doing so. If you are unsure about a specific custom, ask the Rabbi.

### What if my family's customs are different from my spouse's?
This is common and has a practical answer. Speak to the Rabbi, who can help you decide for your home. Call 925-384-7242.

### Can I change a custom?
This depends on the custom and your situation, and it should not be done lightly. Ask the Rabbi.
