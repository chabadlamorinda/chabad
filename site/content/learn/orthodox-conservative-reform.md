If you have ever been asked "Are you Orthodox, Conservative or Reform?" and were not sure what to answer, you are not alone. Here is a short, fair explanation of the main Jewish movements in America, and how Chabad sees the question.

## One Torah, one people

For most of Jewish history there were no "movements." Jews everywhere shared the same Torah, given at Mount Sinai, and the same way of life explained by the Sages: Shabbat, kosher, prayer, the holidays. Some kept more and some kept less, but the standard was the same for all. Jews differed in customs (Ashkenazi, Sephardi and others), not in their basic view of the Torah.

## How the movements began

In the 1800s, as Jews in Western Europe gained citizenship and entered modern society, new approaches arose:

- **Reform Judaism** began in Germany in the early 1800s. It holds that Jewish law can change with the times and that each person may choose which practices are meaningful to them.
- **Conservative Judaism** grew in the late 1800s and early 1900s, especially in America, as a middle path: it keeps Jewish law as important, but views it as developing over history and open to change by its own rabbis.
- **Orthodox Judaism** is the name that came to be used for traditional Judaism: the belief that the Torah, written and oral, was given by G-d at Sinai and that its laws (halacha) remain binding for every generation.

There are other groups too, such as Reconstructionist and Renewal, and many Jews today call themselves "just Jewish."

## The main difference

The heart of the difference is a single question: **Is the Torah G-d's word, given at Sinai and binding for all time, or is it a human tradition that each generation may change?** From that question come the differences in practice: how Shabbat and kosher are kept, how services are run, and how Jewish identity is defined.

## Where does Chabad fit?

Chabad teaches and practices traditional Judaism: the Torah and its mitzvot, as Jews have kept them for over 3,000 years. But Chabad does not divide Jews into camps. The Lubavitcher Rebbe taught that every Jew has a holy soul, and that a Jew is a Jew, with no labels needed. There is no "Orthodox Jew" or "Reform Jew," only Jews, each doing more mitzvot or fewer, and each invited to take one more step.

That is why Jews of every background come to Chabad: people who grew up Reform, Conservative or with no affiliation at all, Israelis, Russian-speaking Jews, and Jews who have not been inside a synagogue in years. ## Respect for every Jew

Loving every Jew, [ahavat Yisrael](/learn/ahavat-yisrael), is a core Chabad teaching. Disagreeing about ideas never means looking down on a person. Many families in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda belong to a local temple and also come to Chabad for a class, a holiday or a Shabbat dinner, and they are always welcome.

## Common questions

### What is the difference between Orthodox, Conservative and Reform Judaism?
They differ mainly in how they see the Torah and Jewish law. Orthodox (traditional) Judaism holds that the Torah was given by G-d at Sinai and its laws are binding for all time. Conservative Judaism sees Jewish law as important but evolving, and Reform Judaism leaves practice to personal choice.

### Is Chabad Orthodox?
Chabad teaches and practices traditional Judaism, so it is usually counted as Orthodox. But Chabad does not label Jews. Every Jew is welcome, whatever their background or level of observance, and no membership is required.

### Can I come to Chabad if I belong to a Reform or Conservative temple?
Of course. Many people who belong to other congregations come to Chabad for classes, holidays and Shabbat dinners. Every Jew is welcome.

### Which movement do most American Jews belong to?
Surveys show that Reform is the largest movement in America, followed by Conservative and Orthodox, and that a large and growing number of Jews say they are "just Jewish." At Chabad, all of them are equally welcome.
