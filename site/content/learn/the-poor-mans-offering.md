In the Torah, a poor person could bring a simple offering of fine flour instead of an animal, and Rashi, quoting the Talmud, teaches that G-d regards it as though he had offered his own soul. So in Judaism a small gift, given sincerely, is as precious as a large one.

## The poor man's offering

The book of Leviticus (Vayikra) describes the *minchah* (מִנְחָה), a meal offering of fine flour, which is brought by someone who cannot afford an animal. Chabad.org, in "The Poor Man's Soul" by Rabbi Shimon Posner, points to Rashi on the opening verse. The Torah says "a soul" (*nefesh*, נֶפֶשׁ) when it speaks of one who brings this offering. Rashi, citing the Talmud, explains that the poor man's soul is in his humble offering, and that it is as dear to G-d as that of a person who has given his life.

Another Chabad.org article (KabbalaOnline.org, "Loyal Pigeons and Devoted Doves") describes the Torah's sliding scale: the rich bring a bullock, the average person a flock animal, and the poor a bird, "because G-d does not overload anyone beyond what they can bear." It teaches that the poor man's offering, even a little flour, brings forgiveness and is equally esteemed.

## A true story: the sukkah slats

Rabbi Posner begins his article with something he heard while waiting for a speaker at a conference. An unannounced speaker spoke about his father, a large man with crutches who had lost a leg fighting for Russia in World War II. After the war the family wanted to leave the Soviet Union, and the mother proposed selling everything they owned.

The father agreed to sell it all, except for the wooden slats he used as roofing, *schach*, for a sukkah. In the Soviet Union, building a sukkah was a crime. Still, every Sukkot he would sneak off to a hidden booth, place the slats overhead and spend a few moments keeping the holiday. If something had to be sold, he said, sell his coat, but not the slats.

Then Rabbi Posner returned to his Chumash and found Rashi's words on the poor man's soul. He drew the lesson: a modest offering from someone with little can matter as much to G-d as a grand one, because of the devotion and sacrifice behind it.

(This is the story as the article tells it, in the speaker's words as Rabbi Posner heard them.)

## What small gifts teach

- **Size is not the measure.** A gift is measured by the heart and the sacrifice behind it.
- **Everyone can take part.** A coin in a [pushka](/learn/pushka), a Shabbat meal for a neighbor, a few minutes of learning.
- **Every person counts.** The Torah gives the poor man an offering of his own, and calls it a soul.

This is why a monthly gift of any size matters to a community. For Jewish life in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda, many gifts, large and small, together support Jewish life. See the [Chai Club](/chai-club) and [/donate](/donate).

## Maaser and small beginnings

About maaser, giving a tenth, the Torah makes a promise: G-d says to bring the tithes and "test Me" (Malachi 3:10), and the Talmud (Taanit 9a) teaches "give a tenth so that you will become rich," as Chabad.org presents it. Maaser is G-d's promise of success. A tenth of a modest income is still a tenth. See [Maaser](/learn/maaser). If money is tight, call the Rabbi: 925-384-7242. See also [Giving When Money Is Tight](/learn/giving-when-money-is-tight).

## Common questions

### Does a small donation matter in Judaism?
Yes. The Torah and Rashi teach that a poor person's small offering is as dear to G-d as a life given, because it comes from the soul.

### What was the poor man's offering?
A meal offering of fine flour, brought by someone who could not afford an animal, as described in Leviticus.

### What does Rashi say about it?
Rashi, quoting the Talmud, says the poor man's soul is in his humble offering and that it is as dear to G-d as a person who gave his life.

### Is the sukkah slats story true?
It is the story as Chabad.org's Rabbi Shimon Posner tells it, as he heard it from a conference speaker.

### How do I give if I have very little?
Give what you can with a full heart. See [Giving When Money Is Tight](/learn/giving-when-money-is-tight) and ask the Rabbi.
