This is an old question: if G-d loves the poor, why doesn't He feed them directly? There are two classic answers. The Talmud tells of Rabbi Akiva's reply to a Roman governor, and a modern article explains that G-d built a world where people are His partners in delivering what everyone needs.

## The question in the Talmud

The Talmud (Bava Batra 10a) tells that Turnus Rufus, a Roman official, asked Rabbi Akiva why, if his G-d loves the poor, He does not feed them. The Talmud tells the exchange this way:

1. Rabbi Akiva answers that G-d leaves people poor so that those who give charity are saved, through that merit, from the judgment of Gehinnom.
2. Turnus Rufus objects. He says giving charity earns punishment, and tells a parable: a king, angry at his slave, jails him and forbids food and water. Someone feeds the slave anyway, and the king is angry with that person. He applies it to Israel, citing "the children of Israel are My slaves" (Leviticus 25:55).
3. Rabbi Akiva replies with his own parable. A king, angry at his child, jails him and forbids food and water. Someone feeds the child, and the king rewards that person. He cites "You are children of the L-rd your G-d" (Deuteronomy 14:1).

The two parables show that the same act of feeding looks different depending on the relationship. Rabbi Akiva's answer rests on the Jewish people being G-d's children. See also [Rabbi Akiva](/learn/rabbi-akiva).

## "Because the system needs people"

Rabbi Aron Moss answers with a picture. You want to help a struggling family. You mail a check, but the mailman keeps it. You order a meal, but the restaurant sends it elsewhere. You try a bank transfer, and the app crashes. You meant well, but the help never arrived because others did not do their part.

His point: G-d wants everyone helped, so He made a world with enough resources for everyone, and a way to deliver them. That way needs people. There is enough money, love and time in the world. What is missing are people with goodwill and a sense of responsibility. Hunger persists because people are not doing their part, not because G-d is failing. G-d could feed the hungry directly, but that would defeat the purpose of creation, which is a world where people freely choose kindness.

## Wealth as a trust

Judaism has a clear view of money. Wealth is a deposit from G-d, held for a time and meant to be used where it is most needed, as taught in the *Arba'ah Turim*, a classic code of Jewish law. The Mishnah (Avot 3:7) teaches that what we have ultimately belongs to G-d. A wealthy person is thus G-d's trusted "banker," entrusted with resources to distribute. This is why tzedakah means "justice," not "charity." See [Tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah) and [the Jewish view on wealth](/learn/jewish-view-on-wealth).

The Baal Shem Tov, the founder of Chassidism, taught this too. He was asked: the Talmud says that for everything G-d forbade, He permitted something like it, so what is permitted that corresponds to denying G-d? He answered: acts of kindness. When you see someone suffering, do not say "G-d will take care of it." Act to relieve the suffering as though there were no G-d and it all depended on you. The lesson is that need is a call to act.

## Common questions

### If G-d loves the poor, why doesn't He feed them?
There is the Talmud's answer through Rabbi Akiva, and a modern one: G-d built a world with enough for all and made people His partners in delivering it. Hunger persists because people are not doing their part.

### What did Rabbi Akiva tell Turnus Rufus?
The Talmud records that he said giving charity saves the giver from the judgment of Gehinnom, and that the Jewish people are G-d's children, so G-d rewards those who feed them.

### Does G-d need our money?
G-d could feed the hungry directly, but made a world where people freely choose kindness. Wealth is a deposit from G-d, entrusted to us to use where it is needed.

### Why is tzedakah called justice and not charity?
Because the money is not only ours. Chassidic teaching explains that giving returns a portion of G-d's bounty that was always meant for others.

### Why does G-d allow poverty?

### What does the Baal Shem Tov's story teach?
The Baal Shem Tov taught: when you see someone suffering, act to help as though it all depended on you. Need is a call to act.
