Keeping kosher does not have to be expensive. Most kosher food in a regular supermarket costs the same as non-kosher food, and the biggest savings come from planning meals, cooking at home and buying meat and specialty items wisely.

## Start with what is already kosher

Many staples are kosher by nature or carry a common kosher symbol at no extra cost:

- **Fresh fruits and vegetables** are kosher. Leafy greens and some produce need checking for insects; see [checking for insects](/learn/checking-for-insects).
- **Eggs** from kosher birds are kosher; check each egg for blood spots when you crack it.
- **Dry goods** like rice, beans, lentils, oats, pasta, flour and sugar are cheap, filling and widely certified.
- **Canned and frozen foods** with a reliable hechsher are often store brands. Learn the common symbols in our [kosher symbols guide](/learn/kosher-symbols).

Store brands often carry the same certification as name brands. Read the label rather than assuming.

## Buy meat and chicken wisely

Kosher meat costs more because of the careful slaughter, inspection and salting. A few ways to manage:

1. **Eat meat less often.** Plan several dairy or pareve meals each week: bean soups, pasta, eggs, fish, vegetable stews.
2. **Buy whole chickens** and cut them yourself, or buy family packs and freeze portions.
3. **Choose cheaper cuts** for slow cooking, like chuck or flanken for cholent.
4. **Stock up** when there are sales and use your freezer. In Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda, Bay Kosher, a project of Chabad of Lamorinda, delivers kosher meat, poultry and groceries, which saves trips to a distant kosher store.

Read more in our guide to [kosher meat](/learn/kosher-meat).

## Cook at home and plan ahead

- **Make a weekly menu** and shop from a list. Impulse buying is the biggest budget breaker.
- **Cook once, eat twice.** Make big pots of soup, chili or kugel and freeze some.
- **Bake your own challah.** Flour, yeast, oil and eggs cost far less than bakery challah, and you get to do the mitzvah of separating challah.
- **Use leftovers from Shabbat** for easy weekday lunches.

## Set up your kitchen without overspending

You do not need all new dishes on day one. Some things can be kashered, and inexpensive sets work well. Many families start with one set of meat dishes, one set of dairy dishes and a few labeled pots. See our [kosher kitchen guide](/learn/kosher-kitchen), and ask the Rabbi what can be kashered before you replace anything.

## Save on wine, cheese and specialty items

- **Kosher wine and grape juice** come in every price range. A simple bottle is perfect for Kiddush.
- **Cheese** needs certification, so it is one of the pricier items. Buy blocks and grate them yourself.
- **Passover** is the most expensive time. Plan early, buy only what you need, and cook simple, fresh food.

## Common questions

### Is kosher food more expensive than regular food?
Mostly no. Packaged foods with a hechsher are usually the same price as other brands. Meat, cheese and wine cost more, so planning those purchases saves the most.

### Do fruits and vegetables need a kosher symbol?
Fresh, uncut produce does not need a symbol. Some vegetables, like leafy greens, must be checked for insects. Cut or processed produce may need certification.

### Can I keep kosher if there is no kosher butcher nearby?
Yes. Many families buy packaged kosher meat and chicken, or order from Bay Kosher, a project of Chabad of Lamorinda that delivers in the East Bay, and freeze it. Call us and we can share ideas.

### Do I have to throw out all my dishes to start keeping kosher?
No. Many metal pots, utensils and some other items can be kashered. Ask the Rabbi before you buy new things, and we can help you kasher what you have.
