Gelatin is not kosher unless it carries a reliable kosher symbol, because most gelatin is made from the skin and bones of non-kosher animals. The same is true of other hidden ingredients, such as carmine (a red dye made from insects), glycerin, rennet and "natural flavors": the label alone cannot tell you, so look for a trusted [kosher symbol](/learn/kosher-symbols).

## Why reading the ingredients is not enough

Kosher (כָּשֵׁר) means "fit" for a Jew to eat. In a factory, kosher depends on more than the list on the box. It depends on where each ingredient comes from, what else is made on the same equipment, and what processing aids are used that never appear on the label.

That is why a product with no obviously forbidden ingredient can still need supervision, and why "vegan" or "all natural" does not mean kosher. A rabbinic agency (a [kosher certification](/learn/kosher-certification)) checks the whole chain, and its symbol tells you that it was done.

## The usual suspects

**Gelatin.** Gelatin is a protein made by boiling animal skin, bones and hides. In most of the world it comes from pork or from animals that were not slaughtered in the kosher way. It hides in marshmallows, gummy candy, yogurt, jello, some vitamins and capsules, and cake mixes. Kosher gelatin exists, made from the bones and hides of kosher-slaughtered cattle or from fish, but only a reliable symbol tells you which kind is in the package. Whether a specific beef gelatin product is considered meat, pareve or neither depends on the product and its certification: ask the Rabbi.

**Rennet.** Rennet is the enzyme that turns milk into cheese. Traditionally it comes from the stomach of a calf. Cheese made with it needs supervision, which is why [kosher cheese](/learn/kosher-cheese) is its own subject.

**Glycerin.** Glycerin (a sweetener and moisturizer in candy, frosting, toothpaste and cosmetics) may come from animal fat or from plants. The label often just says "glycerin."

**Carmine and cochineal.** These natural red dyes are made from crushed insects. The Torah forbids eating insects, so carmine is not kosher. It can be found in red candies, drinks, yogurt and some juices, listed as carmine, cochineal, or "natural red 4."

**Natural flavors.** This phrase can cover hundreds of compounds, some from animal or grape sources. A reliable symbol means the formulas were checked.

**Processing aids and equipment.** Oils sprayed on baking pans, cleaning agents, and lines shared with dairy or non-kosher products rarely appear on a label. Some juices, for example, are processed on equipment also used for grape products, which has its own rules (see [kosher wine](/learn/kosher-wine)).

## What to look for on the package

1. **A reliable kosher symbol.** Learn the main ones in [kosher symbols](/learn/kosher-symbols). If you do not recognize a symbol, ask.
2. **Meat, dairy or pareve.** A "D" or the word dairy means milk; "pareve" means neither meat nor dairy (see [pareve](/learn/pareve)).
3. **No symbol, no guessing.** Plain fresh fruit and vegetables, and most plain raw ingredients, need no symbol. For anything processed, a symbol is the answer.

## Fish gelatin and other alternatives

Fish gelatin made from kosher fish is pareve, and there are certified products. Agar and pectin are plant-based gels, though they still need a symbol on a processed product, because of equipment and flavors. For a particular medication or capsule, see [medicines and vitamins](/learn/kosher-medicine-and-vitamins).

## Common questions

### Is gelatin kosher?
Gelatin is kosher only if it is certified. Most gelatin comes from non-kosher animals, so without a reliable symbol, do not assume it is kosher.

### Is carmine kosher?
No. Carmine (cochineal) is made from insects, which the Torah forbids. It appears in some red foods and drinks, so check the label and the symbol.

### Why does apple juice need a kosher symbol?
Juice can be made on equipment shared with grape products, and ingredients and flavors are added in processing. A symbol shows that the whole production was checked. Ask the Rabbi about a particular bottle.

### Are "natural flavors" kosher?
Not necessarily. Flavors can come from many sources, including animal and grape products, so a reliable kosher symbol is what tells you they are fine.

### Is vegan food automatically kosher?
No. Vegan food can still contain insects, grape products or be made on non-kosher equipment, and it must also be checked for the rules of Passover and of cooking. Look for a symbol.
