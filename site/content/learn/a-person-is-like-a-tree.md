"A person is a tree of the field," says the Torah (Deuteronomy 20:19), and Tu BiShvat, the 15th of Shevat, is the "new year for trees" and the day to ask what that means. A tree has roots, grows, and exists to bear fruit that holds new seeds; a person, too, is meant to be rooted, to grow and to produce good deeds that inspire more good.

## The new year for trees

Tu BiShvat (ט"ו בשבט) is the day when the earliest-blooming trees in the Land of Israel wake up from winter and begin a new fruit-bearing cycle. In the middle of a Bay Area winter, it is a good time to think about growth. The customs are to eat fruit, especially those praised in the Torah for the Land: grapes, figs, pomegranates, olives and dates, see [the seven species](/learn/seven-species) and the [Tu BiShvat seder](/learn/tu-bishvat-seder). The month is [Shevat](/learn/shevat); see the [holiday guide](/holidays/tu-bishvat).

## Roots: the heart that is attached to its source

The Rebbe teaches that a tree draws its life from the earth, and in the same way our emotions are always attached to our essence. This is why a kind person finds it hard to become harsh, and why our character traits are stronger than our passing thoughts. Faith is our root. Nothing grows without it, even when the roots are hidden underground.

## Growth: refine the heart, not only the mind

The Rebbe explains that the traits of the heart "embody growth and development." Ideas can change in a minute; feelings change slowly. So the work of becoming a better person focuses on refining our feelings, and understanding is incomplete until it reaches the heart. The Patriarchs' qualities of kindness, strength and compassion are within every Jew, and need to be revealed and developed.

## Fruit that makes more seeds

In a Tu BiShvat talk, the Rebbe taught that a tree exists to bear fruit whose seeds grow into new trees. In the same way, learning Torah is meant to lead to action: the fruit is mitzvahs. And a person is fulfilled when his fruit bears more fruit, when his good example influences others. A good deed done today may be the seed of a good deed a stranger does next year.

## A season of quiet growth

On Tu BiShvat, in the middle of winter, the trees look bare, yet the sap is rising unseen. The Rebbe pointed out that we eat ripe fruit on a day when the new fruit is only beginning to form, to express that the mature fruit is already contained in its beginnings. If you feel you are in a quiet or hard season, this is a message of hope: growth is happening even when you cannot see it. Trees endure frost and heat and still bear fruit.

## A resolution for the year

The Rebbe suggested that the day is a time to make good resolutions, especially in Torah study. Pick one: a daily page of learning, a Shabbat dinner a month, a weekly call to someone alone. Small steps taken with all your might grow.

## Common questions

### What does "a person is like a tree of the field" mean?
It means a person, like a tree, has roots, grows and is meant to bear fruit, which in Judaism means good deeds that inspire others.

### Why is Tu BiShvat called the new year for trees?
It is the date from which the age of fruit trees is counted for Jewish law, and the time when the first blossoms wake in the Land of Israel; ask the Rabbi for the laws about trees.

### What do the roots of a tree represent in Jewish teaching?
The Rebbe teaches that the roots represent the attachment of our emotions to our essence, and faith is the root of everything.

### What does fruit represent?
Fruit represents mitzvahs and good deeds, and the best fruit contains seeds that grow into new fruit by inspiring others.

### What should I eat on Tu BiShvat?
It is customary to eat fruit, especially grapes, figs, pomegranates, olives and dates from the seven species of the Land of Israel.
