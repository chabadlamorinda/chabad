In the Tanya, "mind over heart" means that by nature the mind rules the heart: you may not control what you feel, but you can choose what you think about, say and do. This is Tanya's practical answer to anger, jealousy and temptation.

This guide looks at the tool, as the Tanya presents it. For the person who uses it, see [the beinoni](/learn/the-beinoni).

## The mind naturally rules

Chapter 12 explains that the brain rules the heart by nature, and that the will of the mind can turn attention away from what the heart craves. The chapter gives an image: light drives out darkness without a struggle, so a small ray of holiness pushes away much of the animal soul's foolishness.

Chapter 17 builds on this with the verse the whole Tanya rests on: "For the matter is very near to you, in your mouth and in your heart, that you may do it" (Deuteronomy 30:14). How can serving G-d with the heart be "very near," when a burning love of G-d is hard to reach? Because the mind is under a person's control even when the heart is not. Thinking about G-d's greatness brings at least a love in the mind, and that is enough to guide what you say and do. Chapter 41 returns to the same verse: serving G-d with love and awe is within simple reach.

## Feelings come; you choose what to do

The chapter says that improper thoughts may rise by themselves from the heart. What matters is the response. The beinoni pushes such a thought away at once, does not dwell on it or plan it, and does not speak of it. A person who willingly entertains it is, in that moment, not a beinoni.

The same goes for feelings about other people. According to the chapter, when jealousy, anger or resentment arise, the beinoni keeps them out of his mind and answers with kindness, as Joseph did toward his brothers.

The Tanya's teaching on the beinoni adds that this does not need the feelings to disappear: a person "copes, but doesn't triumph," and keeps striving. It also says (citing Chapter 13) that the divine soul receives heavenly help.

## Choosing your clothes

The Tanya's teaching on the soul's "garments" (see [three garments of the soul](/learn/three-garments-of-the-soul)) says that at any moment you can choose your thoughts, words and actions. Acting kindly and speaking kindly even in a low mood gradually shifts your character.

## Prayer helps

Chapter 12 says that during the Shema and the Amidah the beinoni meditates on G-d's greatness, which awakens love in the heart and quiets the animal soul for a time. After prayer, the desires may return, but the impression of the prayer helps him resist. See [the Shema](/learn/shema), [kavanah in prayer](/learn/kavanah-in-prayer) and [changing a bad habit](/learn/changing-a-bad-habit).

## A practical way to start

Our suggestion, not a quotation:

1. Notice the feeling without acting.
2. Ask: what do I want to think, say and do now?
3. Do one small right thing, even without the feeling.

## Common questions

### What does "mind over heart" mean in Tanya?
It means that the mind can guide the heart. You may not control your feelings, but you can control what you think about, say and do.

### Can I control my emotions according to Judaism?
Not fully, says the Tanya, which is why it focuses on thought, speech and action. The beinoni feels the desire but does not act on it.

### What should I do with an unwanted thought?
Chapter 12 says the beinoni pushes it away at once and does not dwell on it.

### What if I feel angry or jealous?
Chapter 12 says the beinoni keeps the feeling from taking over and answers with kindness. For your own situation, ask the Rabbi.

### Does this mean I should hide my feelings?
The Tanya speaks about not acting on harmful desires. For personal questions, ask the Rabbi.
