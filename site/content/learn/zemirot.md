Zemirot are the songs sung around the table at the Shabbat meals. Between courses, families and guests sing together, in Hebrew and Aramaic, about the joy of Shabbat, G-d's kindness and the hope for redemption. Singing turns a dinner into a Shabbat meal.

## What zemirot are

The word zemirot (זְמִירוֹת) means "songs." Most were written as poems by great rabbis and Kabbalists over the past thousand years, and many spell out the author's name in the first letters of their verses. They are printed in most siddurim and bentchers (small booklets for the blessings after meals), and every community has its favorite melodies.

The Sages teach that the Shabbat meal should be more than food. Words of Torah and songs of praise lift the table into something holy, and singing together brings warmth and closeness to everyone sitting around it.

## When we sing them

- **Friday night:** the meal opens with [Shalom Aleichem](/learn/shalom-aleichem) and [Eishet Chayil](/learn/eishet-chayil) before Kiddush, and more songs are sung between courses.
- **Shabbat day:** at lunch, after Kiddush and between courses.
- **The third meal** in the late afternoon, when the songs are often slower and full of longing as Shabbat draws to a close.
- **[Melave Malka](/learn/melave-malka)** on Saturday night, with songs that escort the Shabbat Queen as she leaves.

## A few classic songs

- **Shalom Aleichem:** greets the angels who accompany us home on Friday night and asks for their blessing.
- **Eishet Chayil:** "A Woman of Valor," from the Book of Proverbs, sung in honor of the woman of the house and, on a deeper level, of Shabbat and the Torah.
- **Yah Ribon:** an Aramaic song by Rabbi Yisrael Najara of Safed, praising G-d, the "Master of the world," for His wonders. It is one of the most widely sung zemirot.
- **Kah Echsof:** "G-d, I long," a tender song of yearning for closeness to G-d, written by the Chassidic master Rabbi Aharon of Karlin. It is a Chabad favorite, usually sung slowly and with feeling.
- **Menucha V'simcha:** "Rest and joy, light for the Jews," a lively Friday night song describing the gifts of Shabbat.
- **Dror Yikra:** "He will proclaim freedom," an old poem by Dunash ben Labrat, often sung at Shabbat lunch, with a prayer for the Jewish people's freedom and peace.

## Niggunim: melodies without words

In Chassidic homes, and especially in Chabad, a great deal of the singing is [niggunim](/learn/niggun), melodies sung without words. The Chassidic masters taught that a melody can say what words cannot, reaching the soul directly. Some niggunim are joyful and get the whole table singing; others are slow and thoughtful. Many Chabad families sing niggunim at the Shabbat table more than the printed zemirot, and some of these melodies were taught by the Rebbes themselves.

A wordless niggun is also the easiest way to join in: there is nothing to read, so anyone can hum along from the first time.

## Singing at home if you do not know Hebrew

- **Start with one song.** Pick Shalom Aleichem, or one simple niggun, and sing it every week until it feels natural.
- **Use transliteration.** Many bentchers print the Hebrew words in English letters, with a translation, so you can sing and understand at the same time.
- **Listen first.** Find a recording of the melody and play it during the week so the tune is familiar by Friday night.
- **Do not worry about your voice.** The Shabbat table is not a concert. Children love to sing, and guests usually join in once someone starts.
- **Come and hear it.** The fastest way to learn is to sit at a Shabbat table where people sing, and pick the songs up by ear.

## Common questions

### Do I have to sing zemirot at my Shabbat meal?
There is no strict requirement, but singing is a beautiful custom that adds joy to Shabbat. Even one song makes the meal feel different.

### Are musical instruments used?
No. We do not play instruments on Shabbat, so zemirot are sung with voices alone.

### Where can I find the words?
Most siddurim and bentchers include the zemirot. Choose one with transliteration and English if you are still learning Hebrew.
