The manna (מָן, "mon") was the miraculous food that fell from heaven every morning for the 40 years the Jewish people traveled in the desert after leaving Egypt. The double portion that fell on Friday is the reason we place two challahs on the table every Shabbat.

## What was the manna?

About a month after the Exodus, the food the people had brought from Egypt ran out. They cried out, and the next morning the ground was covered with dew, and when the dew lifted there lay "a fine, flaky substance, fine as frost on the ground" (Exodus 16:14). The people asked, "Man hu?", "What is it?" and that is how it got its name. (Another explanation is that the word means "a portion.")

According to Chabad.org's summary of the sources, it was about the size of a coriander seed and looked like crystal. A north wind swept the ground clean first, rain rinsed it, and dew settled above and below, so the manna lay as if wrapped in a package.

## What did the manna taste like?

The Torah says it tasted like a wafer made with honey, or like something rich with oil. The Sages add that it took on the taste each person wanted: adults tasted the foods they loved, and a baby tasted like mother's milk. The Talmud also says that the manna was completely absorbed by the body.

## The rules of the manna

- **One omer per person.** Each person gathered about one omer, enough for that day.
- **No saving for tomorrow.** Anyone who kept some overnight found it spoiled. Only on Friday was it different.
- **A double portion on Friday.** On the sixth day the people gathered twice as much, and it stayed fresh through Shabbat, because none fell on Shabbat.
- **Gather early.** When the sun grew hot, the manna melted.

The Sages teach that how the manna arrived reflected each person's spiritual level: for the righteous it lay at the tent door, others had to go out and gather it, and some had to go farther.

## Why we have two challahs on Shabbat

On Friday night and on Shabbat day, we begin the meal with two whole loaves, [challah](/learn/challah). They recall the double portion of manna that fell on Friday. We cover them with a cloth because the manna was covered above by dew and below by dew, and before we say [hamotzi](/learn/hamotzi) we uncover the loaves. ## The Rebbe's lesson: bread from heaven

The Lubavitcher Rebbe taught, as presented on Chabad.org, that there are two kinds of bread: "bread from the earth," which comes through plowing, planting and work, and "bread from heaven," the manna, which came with no human effort at all. When bread comes from the earth, a person can think, "I made this happen." The manna removed any such illusion: all of our needs come from G-d.

The manna also tested the people. Would they trust G-d to provide each day, without storing up? The Midrash teaches that a person who has food today and worries "What will I eat tomorrow?" has a lack of faith. That is not an excuse not to work. The Rebbe explained that we work because G-d tells us to act in the natural world, but we remember that the real source of our income is G-d. This is the core of [bitachon, trust in G-d](/learn/bitachon). ## The jar of manna

G-d told Moses to save a jar of manna for future generations. Tradition tells that the prophet Jeremiah later showed it to people who said they had no time for Torah because they had to earn a living. "See what your ancestors ate," he said: G-d provides for those who put Him first. Tradition holds that the jar is hidden, waiting for Moshiach.

## Common questions

### What was manna in the Bible?
Manna was the miraculous food that fell from heaven every morning for 40 years in the desert. It looked like a small white seed or crystal, and it tasted like whatever each person wished.

### What does the word manna mean?
Manna comes from the Hebrew "mon" (מָן). The Torah connects it to the people's question "Man hu?" ("What is it?"), and another explanation is that it means "a portion."

### Why do we have two challahs on Shabbat?
The two loaves remind us of the double portion of manna that fell on Friday. We also cover them, recalling the layer of dew above and below the manna.

### Why couldn't the Israelites keep manna overnight?
The manna was a daily lesson in trusting G-d. It spoiled if saved, except for the Friday portion that was kept for Shabbat.

### What did manna taste like?
The Torah says it tasted like honey wafers, and the Sages teach that it tasted like whatever food each person wanted.

### What does the manna teach us today?
It teaches that livelihood comes from G-d, even as we work for it. We make an honest effort, and we trust G-d, one day at a time.
