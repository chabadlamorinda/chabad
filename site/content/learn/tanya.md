The Tanya is the foundational book of Chabad Chassidic philosophy, written by Rabbi Schneur Zalman of Liadi and first published in 1796. It is a practical guide to the human soul, explaining how every person can serve G-d with both mind and heart, even while struggling with everyday challenges.

## Who wrote the Tanya?

Rabbi Schneur Zalman of Liadi (1745 to 1812), known as the Alter Rebbe, was the founder of the Chabad movement. He was a student of the Maggid of Mezeritch, who in turn was the main student of the Baal Shem Tov, the founder of [Chassidus](/learn/chassidus).

The book's official name is Likkutei Amarim ("Collected Teachings"), but it is known as Tanya after its first word, meaning "it was taught."

## The big idea: two souls

The Tanya explains that every Jew has two souls:

- **The animal soul (nefesh habehamit):** the source of natural drives, self-interest and physical desires. It is not evil, but it is focused on the self.
- **The G-dly soul (nefesh ha'Elokit):** "a part of G-d above," which naturally longs to connect with G-d and do good.

The two souls compete for control of a person's thoughts, words and actions. Life is a journey of letting the G-dly soul lead.

## The beinoni: a goal for everyone

One of the Tanya's most famous teachings is about the beinoni, "the intermediate person." This is someone who still feels negative urges, but never acts on them. The Tanya teaches that this is a level every person can reach.

This idea is freeing. Having bad thoughts or temptations does not mean you are failing. The struggle itself is precious to G-d, and every time you choose good, you bring joy above.

## Mind over heart

A central principle of the Tanya is that "the mind rules over the heart." By thinking deeply about G-d's greatness and closeness, a person can awaken genuine feelings of love and awe. Even when feelings are not there, a person can always control what they think, say and do.

## The five parts of the Tanya

1. **Likkutei Amarim:** the main section, on the soul and serving G-d.
2. **Shaar Hayichud Vehaemunah:** on G-d's unity and faith.
3. **Iggeret HaTeshuvah:** on [teshuvah](/learn/teshuvah), returning to G-d.
4. **Iggeret HaKodesh:** letters of guidance.
5. **Kuntres Acharon:** additional essays.

## Studying Tanya today

The Tanya has been printed thousands of times in many places around the world, a project the Rebbe encouraged. Many people follow a daily study cycle that completes the Tanya each year. Classes for beginners are taught in many communities, often using English translations.

## Common questions

### Is the Tanya hard to understand?

Some sections are deep, but its core ideas are practical and relatable. With a teacher or a good translation, beginners can gain a lot from it.

### Do I need to know Kabbalah to study Tanya?

No. The Tanya draws on [Kabbalah](/learn/kabbalah) but explains its ideas in a way that applies to daily life. It is often called the "Written Torah of Chassidus."

### Is the Tanya only for Chabad?

It is the foundational text of Chabad, but people from many backgrounds study it for its insight into the soul and personal growth.

### Where is a good place to start?

Many people start with the first chapters of Likkutei Amarim, especially those about the two souls and the beinoni.
