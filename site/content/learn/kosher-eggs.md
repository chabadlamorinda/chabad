Eggs from kosher birds, like chickens, are kosher and pareve (neither meat nor dairy), but Jewish law forbids blood, so Chabad custom is to check each egg before using it. Crack every egg into a clear glass on its own; if you see a blood spot, throw that egg away.

## Why eggs are kosher and pareve

An egg from a kosher bird (such as a chicken, duck, goose or turkey) is kosher even though it comes from a living animal, and it is pareve (פַּרְוֶה), which means it may be eaten with meat or with dairy. You can read more in [pareve](/learn/pareve) and [meat and milk](/learn/meat-and-milk). Eggs from a non-kosher bird are not kosher.

## Why we check eggs

The Torah forbids eating blood. Occasionally an egg has a small spot of blood, usually a tiny red or brown speck on the yolk or in the white. It is most often caused by a small blood vessel breaking as the egg forms, and it is not a sign that the egg is unhealthy.

Chabad.org teaches that every egg should be opened into a clear dish and checked before cooking or mixing, and that an egg with a blood spot is not to be eaten. By the strict letter of the law some authorities permit removing the spot and eating the rest, but the accepted custom, and the Chabad practice, is to discard the whole egg. Brown eggs tend to have somewhat more blood spots than white ones, so check them with extra care.

Commercial eggs are scanned (candled) at the farm, so spots are rare in store-bought eggs, but they still happen, and the custom is to check anyway. Eggs from a backyard or a small farm deserve special care.

## How to check an egg, step by step

1. **Use a clear glass or small white dish,** not the mixing bowl, so one bad egg does not spoil the others.
2. **Crack one egg into it** and look at the yolk and white in good light.
3. **If it is clean,** pour it into the pan or the bowl.
4. **If you see a blood spot,** throw the egg away and wash the dish before the next one.
5. **Check on Shabbat too,** but do it carefully: ask the Rabbi about how to check eggs on Shabbat, because it is a question of laws that apply only on that day.

## Hard-boiled eggs, pots and eggs for Shabbat

A hard-boiled egg cannot be checked after it is cooked, so a spot cannot be seen. If you boil eggs in the shell and later find a blood spot, ask the Rabbi. When you need many hard-boiled eggs, for Shabbat, a Seder or a bris, check them raw by cracking, or ask for help.

If you cook eggs in a pot that was used for meat or for dairy, or mix eggs with meat or dairy, the dish takes that status. Pareve eggs cooked in a clean pot remain pareve. If a pot's status is unclear, ask the Rabbi.

## Common questions

### Are eggs kosher?
Yes, eggs from kosher birds such as chickens are kosher. Each egg should be checked for a blood spot before use.

### Are eggs pareve?
Yes. Eggs are pareve, so they can be eaten with meat or dairy, as long as they were not cooked or mixed with either.

### What do I do if I find a blood spot in an egg?
Throw that egg away. According to Chabad custom, the whole egg is not used, and the dish should be washed before you crack the next one.

### Do I need to check eggs from the store?
Yes, the custom is to check every egg, even store-bought ones, because blood spots are rare but still happen.

### Can I eat a hard-boiled egg if it has a blood spot?
Ask the Rabbi. Once an egg is cooked, you cannot check it, so a spot found afterward is a question about that specific egg.
