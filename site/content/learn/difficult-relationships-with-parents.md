"Honor your father and your mother" is one of the Ten Commandments. For many people it is a joy. For others, whose parents are critical, controlling, unwell or hurtful, it can be one of the hardest mitzvahs. Judaism speaks to both.

## What honoring parents means

Jewish law defines two parts of this mitzvah (see [honoring parents](/learn/honoring-parents)):

- **Kavod, honor:** helping them, feeding and clothing them when needed, speaking respectfully.
- **Mora, reverence:** not contradicting them harshly, not sitting in their place, not calling them by their first name.

The mitzvah is about your actions and words, not about feeling a particular way. You can honor a parent while feeling hurt.

## What it does not require

- **It does not require accepting abuse.** If a parent is abusive, your safety and your family's safety come first. Jewish law allows keeping distance, and arranging for others to care for a parent when being close is not possible.
- **It does not require doing something wrong** because a parent asked.
- **It does not override your marriage.** Your spouse and your own home come first in daily decisions.

These questions are delicate and personal. Talk them through with the Rabbi.

## Practical steps

- **Set kind, clear boundaries.** "I'd love to visit on Sunday for two hours" is honoring and protective.
- **Choose respectful words,** even when you disagree.
- **Find what you can do:** a phone call, help with a task, a visit with the grandchildren.
- **Get support for yourself:** a friend, a counselor, the Rabbi or the Rebbetzin.

## Estrangement and reconciliation

Some families stop speaking for years. Jewish tradition always leaves the door open to [teshuvah](/learn/teshuvah) and peace. A small step, a holiday card, a short call, a message on a birthday, can begin to thaw things. If a parent is ill or aging, don't wait too long. See [forgiveness and making peace](/learn/forgiveness-and-making-peace).

## After a parent passes away

Even when the relationship was painful, saying [Kaddish](/learn/kaddish) and keeping the [yahrzeit](/learn/yahrzeit) can bring healing, for the parent's soul and for you.

## Common questions

### Do I have to honor a parent who hurt me?
The obligation remains, but its form can change. Keeping distance for your safety is permitted. Ask the Rabbi about your situation.

### My parent criticizes my religious choices. What do I do?
Stay respectful, keep your practice, and avoid arguments at holiday tables. Shared warm moments speak louder than debates.

### Can I say Kaddish for a parent I was estranged from?
Yes. It is a merit for their soul and often brings peace to the mourner.
