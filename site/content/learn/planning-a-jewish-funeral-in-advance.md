To plan a Jewish funeral in advance, write down your wishes (burial in the ground, tahara, a plain casket, no embalming or cremation), consider a plot in a Jewish cemetery, and tell your family whom to call first, which should be your Rabbi. It is a kindness to your family, who will not have to guess in a hard moment.

Many people in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda put this off because it is uncomfortable. Judaism sees it differently: planning ahead is a mitzvah of caring, and many people find it brings peace of mind.

## Why plan ahead?

When a death comes, the family is in shock, and the first decisions have to be made quickly, since Jewish burial is traditionally held as soon as possible. If your wishes are written and your family knows whom to call, they are spared guessing, disagreeing, or doing something you would not have wanted.

Chabad.org recalls that the Midrash advises buying a grave even while we are alive and well, and that it is an ancient custom (Abraham bought a burial place for Sarah). Some say it may even be a sign of blessing, and one writer suggests that facing mortality helps us use our time wisely.

## What to write down

Put your wishes on paper and keep them where your family can find them. Consider:

1. **Burial, not cremation.** Jewish tradition teaches burial in the ground. See [cremation, a Jewish view](/learn/cremation-jewish-view).
2. **Tahara and a shomer.** Ask that the Chevra Kadisha prepare you and that someone stay with you until burial. See [the Chevra Kadisha and tahara](/learn/chevra-kadisha-and-tahara).
3. **A plain casket and white shrouds.** Simple and the same for everyone.
4. **No embalming, no autopsy** unless the law requires it. In that case the family should call the Rabbi at once.
5. **A Jewish cemetery.** Chabad.org describes a proper Jewish cemetery as reserved for Jews, with permanent burial rights and fully separate sections.
6. **Whom to call first**: the Rabbi's number, 925-384-7242, your funeral home, and family members.
7. **Burial in Israel**, if that is your wish. Burial in the Holy Land is considered an act of special devotion. It requires planning and is best discussed with the Rabbi.
8. **Your Hebrew name.** Your Hebrew name and your parents' names are used at the funeral and on the grave. If you do not know them, see [getting a Hebrew name](/learn/getting-a-hebrew-name-as-an-adult).

## Should I buy a burial plot?

Chabad.org's answer is yes. Buying a plot spares your children the task and the cost during their grief. If you are married, a plot beside your spouse is a common choice. Customs differ about where relatives are placed, so ask the Rabbi. Do not buy a plot in a cemetery that is not Jewish without asking the Rabbi first, because the rules about a Jewish cemetery matter.

## Legal documents and your Jewish will

A letter of wishes does not replace your legal will, power of attorney or healthcare directive, and you should speak with a lawyer about those. A [halachic will](/learn/halachic-will) can help your estate follow Jewish law and fairness, and an [ethical will](/learn/ethical-will) lets you leave your values and your love in words. All of these belong in your plan.

## Tell your family

The best plan is a talk. Sit with your spouse and children, tell them your wishes, and give them the paper. Tell them that the first phone call should be to the Rabbi. It will give them peace to know that they are doing what you wanted.

## Common questions

### How do I plan a Jewish funeral in advance?
Write down your wishes (burial, tahara, plain casket, no embalming or cremation), choose a Jewish cemetery, and tell your family to call the Rabbi first. Call Chabad of Lamorinda in Lafayette at 925-384-7242 and we will talk it through privately.

### Should I buy a burial plot before I need one?
Yes. Chabad.org says to, since it spares the family the burden, and the Midrash recommends it even in times of health. Ask the Rabbi about the cemetery.

### Does buying a plot bring bad luck?
No. Some people believe it brings a blessing of long life. Chabad.org says this is commonly believed but not found in classical sources. In any case, it is an act of caring for your family, not a danger.

### Can I write down my wishes for a Jewish funeral?
Yes, and you should. Put it in writing, keep a copy with your will, and tell your family where it is.

### Can I be buried in Israel?
Burial in the Holy Land is considered an act of special devotion. It takes advance arrangements, so speak with the Rabbi. Do not rely on your family to arrange it without a plan.

### Is a Jewish funeral plan different from a will?
Yes. A will covers property; your funeral wishes cover your burial. Both are important and do different jobs. See [halachic will](/learn/halachic-will).
