Shatnez is the Torah's prohibition against wearing clothing that contains both wool and linen. A suit, coat or sweater that mixes these two fibers, even in a hidden collar or lining, may not be worn, so many Jews have new clothing checked before wearing it.

## Where it comes from

The Torah says, "You shall not wear shatnez, wool and linen together" (Deuteronomy 22:11; see also Leviticus 19:19). Shatnez is one of the mitzvahs called a chok, a law whose reason is beyond human understanding. We keep it because G-d asked, and Jewish teachers have offered ideas to help us connect with it. Read more about [why we do mitzvahs](/learn/why-mitzvot).

## Some meanings

- **Cain and Abel.** The Midrash teaches that Cain brought flax (linen) as an offering and Abel brought sheep (wool). Their conflict led to the first murder, so the two are kept apart.
- **The Holy Temple.** The priestly garments contained wool and linen together. What is holy in the Temple is not for everyday wear.
- **Order in creation.** Each thing has its own place and purpose.

## What counts as shatnez

- **Wool** means wool from sheep. Other animal hairs, like camel or alpaca, are not wool for this purpose, though fabrics labeled with them sometimes include sheep wool too.
- **Linen** means fiber from the flax plant.
- **Any amount** counts: one linen thread in a wool garment makes it shatnez.
- **Other combinations are fine.** Wool and cotton, linen and cotton, wool and polyester, or silk with either are all permitted.

## Where it hides

Labels list the main fabric but often not the inner parts. Linen can show up in:

- The stiffening canvas inside the collar and lapels of suits and jackets.
- Padding in shoulders, pockets and waistbands.
- Threads used to sew buttons or decorations.

Men's suits, sport jackets, wool coats and some women's suits and coats are the items most often checked. Sweaters and knitwear are checked when they contain linen or have mixed fibers.

## How to keep the mitzvah, step by step

1. **Read the label.** If it lists wool and linen together, do not wear it.
2. **For wool suits and coats**, have them checked by a shatnez testing service, which examines threads under a microscope.
3. **If shatnez is found,** the linen parts can often be replaced, making the garment wearable.
4. **Check before wearing,** not after. It is best to have new tailored clothing tested before alterations.

## Beyond clothing

The prohibition includes items you wear or wrap yourself in, like scarves and some blankets you lie under. Some items, like a decorative wall hanging, are not included. Ask the Rabbi about specific items.

## Common questions

### Is a wool and cotton sweater a problem?
No. Only wool and linen together are forbidden. Wool with cotton, silk or synthetic fibers is fine.

### Do I need to check clothing that does not contain wool?
Generally no. If there is no sheep wool in the garment, it cannot be shatnez. Some fabrics described as other animal hair may include sheep wool, so read labels carefully.

### Can I trust the label?
Labels are helpful but not always complete, especially for inner parts of suits and coats. For tailored wool clothing, a professional check is the safest way.

### Can I give or sell a shatnez garment?
You may sell or give it to someone who is not Jewish. Do not give it to a Jew who might wear it without knowing.
