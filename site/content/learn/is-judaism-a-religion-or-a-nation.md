The Jewish people are best described as a family bound by a covenant with G-d, made at Mount Sinai. That is the answer to the question: Jewishness is not only a religion, not a race and not simply a nationality. A person is Jewish by being born to a Jewish mother or by converting according to Jewish law, and that identity lasts for life.

## Why "religion" is not the whole answer

Jews cannot be defined by religion alone, since many Jews are not observant. A Jew who does not believe in G-d is still a Jew. Once someone is Jewish, the identity is permanent. No one can revoke it.

Religion is still part of the picture, because the Jewish people were formed by a covenant with G-d. Becoming Jewish through conversion means accepting that covenant, with its beliefs and practices. But the bond itself is deeper than what any one person believes or does on a given day.

## Why "race" does not fit

DNA does not make someone Jewish. Under Jewish law, a DNA test showing Jewish markers does not by itself make a person Jewish. What counts is a Jewish mother or a valid conversion. Converts have also joined the Jewish people throughout history, including Ruth, the ancestor of King David.

## A nation, in a special sense

"Nation" fits in one sense: Jews can be thought of as permanent citizens of a nation. At Sinai, every Israelite became a participant, with responsibilities as well as privileges, and that participation continued through centuries of exile without a king or a central authority. Unlike many nations, the Jewish people were formed by covenants before they had a land. The Abraham covenant created an eternal people. At Sinai, the people accepted the Torah with the words "We will do and we will obey."

## A family

The traditional Jewish self-image is a family. Like a birth family, it is a family you cannot leave. A convert is described as adopted into the family, joining the same covenant that the Jewish people entered at Sinai, before a rabbinic court.

A family can have members who are close and members who are distant, and everyone still belongs. All Jews are equally Jewish, though levels of engagement differ. A Jew who returns after years away does not have to "become Jewish" again. Their return is welcomed like a child coming home.

## What this means for you

If you are Jewish, you are part of this family whether you are a lifelong member of a synagogue or are walking into a Jewish event for the first time. Mitzvahs are how the family lives together: [Shabbat](/learn/shabbat-in-lamorinda), a [mezuzah](/learn/mezuzah) on the door, [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah). If you are wondering about your own status, see [Who is a Jew?](/learn/who-is-a-jew) and ask the Rabbi privately.

## Common questions

### Is Judaism a religion or an ethnicity?
Judaism is a religion, and being Jewish is also a matter of belonging to a people. The Jewish people is a family bound by the covenant at Sinai, so Judaism is not only a religion and not a race.

### Are Jews a nation?
In a certain sense, yes. Jews are permanent citizens of a nation formed by covenant at Sinai, even without a land or a king. But "family" is the traditional image for how the bond works.

### What makes someone Jewish?
A person is Jewish if born to a Jewish mother or if converted according to Jewish law. A child of a Jewish father and a non-Jewish mother is not Jewish unless converted. Ask the Rabbi if your own situation is unclear.

### Can you stop being Jewish?
No. Jewishness is lifelong, regardless of belief or practice.

### Can someone become Jewish?
Yes, through conversion under a recognized Orthodox rabbinical court. See [converting to Judaism](/learn/converting-to-judaism).
