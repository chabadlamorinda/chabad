The siddur is the Jewish prayer book, containing the daily, Shabbat and holiday prayers in their set order. Its name comes from the Hebrew word seder, "order," because it arranges our conversation with G-d into a clear daily path.

## Where the prayers come from

Prayer is as old as the Jewish people. Abraham, Isaac and Jacob each prayed, and the Sages teach that they established the morning, afternoon and evening prayers. About 2,400 years ago, the Men of the Great Assembly, led by Ezra, composed the core text of the [Amidah](/learn/amidah) and many blessings, so every Jew would have the right words.

Over the centuries more prayers were added: psalms, poems and passages from the Torah. The earliest known written siddurim come from the Geonim of Babylonia, more than a thousand years ago.

## How a siddur is organized

Most siddurim follow the day and the calendar:

- **Morning blessings and Shacharit,** the morning prayer, including the [Shema](/learn/shema) and the Amidah
- **Mincha,** the afternoon prayer
- **Maariv,** the evening prayer
- **Shabbat and holiday prayers,** including Kiddush and Musaf
- **Blessings** for food, [Birkat Hamazon](/learn/birkat-hamazon) and other occasions
- **The [Bedtime Shema](/learn/bedtime-shema)**
- **Special sections** such as Pirkei Avot, [Tehillim](/learn/tehillim) and prayers for life events

## Different versions (nusach)

Jewish communities developed slightly different versions of the prayers, called nusach. Ashkenazi, Sephardic and Chassidic siddurim are very similar, with small differences in wording and order.

Chabad uses Nusach Ari, arranged by Rabbi Schneur Zalman of Liadi, the first Chabad Rebbe, based on the teachings of the Arizal. The standard Chabad siddur is called Tehillat Hashem. Many editions include English translation and instructions.

## Tips for using a siddur

1. **Start small.** You do not need to say every word. [Modeh Ani](/learn/modeh-ani) and the Shema are a beautiful beginning.
2. **Use a siddur with translation** so you understand what you are saying.
3. **Read in any language you understand.** Hebrew is ideal, but the Sages allow prayer in any language.
4. **Follow the page numbers.** In synagogue, page numbers are often announced or posted.
5. **Add your own words.** The siddur gives structure, and personal prayer fills it with feeling.

## Treating a siddur with respect

A siddur contains G-d's Name and holy words, so it is treated with care. It is not placed on the floor or left face down. If one falls, people pick it up and kiss it. An old or damaged siddur is not thrown away but buried or placed in a genizah.

## Common questions

### Do I need to read Hebrew to use a siddur?
No. Many siddurim have English translation and transliteration. Learning to read Hebrew over time is a wonderful goal, and our [Hebrew alphabet](/learn/hebrew-alphabet) guide is a good start.

### Which siddur should I buy?
If you pray with a Chabad community, a Tehillat Hashem siddur with English translation is a great choice. Any reliable traditional siddur is fine for personal prayer.

### How long does the morning prayer take?
A full weekday Shacharit with a minyan takes about 30 to 45 minutes. At home you can start with a few minutes and grow from there.

### Can I use a siddur app on my phone?
Yes, on weekdays many people do. On Shabbat and holidays, use a printed siddur.
