There is no loss quite like this one. The person who shared your bed, your table, your decisions and your memories is gone, and the house is suddenly too quiet. Judaism does not rush you through this. It walks beside you, with a clear path for the first days, a rhythm for the first year, and a community that is meant to hold you up.

## The first days

In Jewish law a husband or wife is one of the seven relatives for whom we formally mourn. From the passing until the funeral you are an *onen*, free of other obligations so you can arrange the burial ([aninut](/learn/aninut)). The funeral is held as soon as possible, and at it you tear a garment ([kriah](/learn/kriah)). Then come the seven days of [shiva](/learn/shiva) at home, when friends come to you, and the thirty days of [sheloshim](/learn/sheloshim).

You do not have to know any of this in advance. Call the Rabbi first, before the funeral home if you can, and he will help with the arrangements and explain each step. See [when someone has passed away](/help/someone-passed-away) and, for the whole path at a glance, [the Jewish mourning journey](/learn/mourning-journey).

## After the thirty days

For a parent, formal mourning lasts a year; for a spouse, it ends after sheloshim. Many widows and widowers find this surprising, even hurtful, as if Judaism expects them to be finished. It does not. The law limits the outward signs of mourning so that life can gently call you back, but grief has no deadline, and the sages compared losing a spouse to seeing the Temple destroyed in one's own lifetime. Keep saying [Kaddish](/learn/kaddish) if you wish, light a [yahrzeit candle](/learn/yahrzeit-candle) each year, say [Yizkor](/learn/yizkor), and give tzedakah in their memory. See [the year of mourning](/learn/year-of-mourning).

## The loneliness

The hardest part often begins when the casseroles stop. Judaism's answer is not a slogan; it is people. Come to Shabbat dinner, even once, even if you cry. Accept invitations before you feel ready. Tell Rebbetzin Labkowski you would rather not be alone for the holidays; she will gladly help you find a table. A community that does not leave a widow or widower alone is doing one of the greatest mitzvot there is. If grief turns into a darkness that does not lift, please speak with a doctor or a grief counselor as well as with the Rabbi; asking for help is strength. See [feeling lonely](/learn/feeling-lonely) and [Shabbat when you live alone](/learn/shabbat-alone).

## Practical matters

Grief makes paperwork cruel. Judaism counts practical help as a mitzvah too, so let people help: a friend beside you for the bank and insurance calls, a child to sort the mail, a neighbor to drive. Your spouse's Hebrew name and the Hebrew date of passing matter for the yahrzeit; the Rabbi can work it out for you, and you can save it in your account on this website for a yearly reminder.

## The holidays

The first Rosh Hashanah, the first Seder, the first Chanukah without them can feel impossible. Plan ahead so that you are not alone: a child's home, a friend's table, or Chabad, where no one sits by themselves. See [grief during the holidays](/learn/grief-during-the-holidays).

## Children and grandchildren

Your children have lost a parent while you have lost a partner; each of you grieves differently, and that is all right. Grown children often avoid mentioning the loss to protect you, and you may be doing the same for them. Say the name aloud; it gives everyone permission. With grandchildren, simple honesty works best: Grandpa's body stopped working, his soul is with G-d, and we remember him by doing good things. See [talking to kids about death](/learn/talking-to-kids-about-death).

## Common questions

### Do I say Kaddish for my husband or wife?
Kaddish is traditionally said for a spouse during the thirty days, and many continue for the full year as an expression of love. Ask the Rabbi about the custom and about a minyan.

### When may a widow or widower remarry?
Jewish law asks a widower to wait until three festivals have passed (with exceptions, for example when there are young children) and a widow about three months. Every situation is different; ask the Rabbi.

### How do I mark the yahrzeit?
Light a 24-hour candle the evening before, say Kaddish, give tzedakah, learn something in their memory and, if you can, visit the grave. See [yahrzeit](/learn/yahrzeit) and [visiting a grave](/learn/visiting-a-grave).
