Mazal tov! An engagement is the beginning of a new Jewish home. Jewish tradition surrounds this time with joy, a few lovely customs, and a focus on preparing not just for a wedding but for a marriage.

## The l'chaim or vort

When a couple decides to marry, family and friends gather to celebrate. In Chabad this is usually called a **l'chaim**, after the toast "to life"; in other communities it is called a **vort**, Yiddish for "word," because the families give their word. It can be a small gathering at home or a larger party, with food, words of Torah, blessings and a toast. There are no required rituals; the point is to share the happiness and wish the couple well.

## Tenaim

Tenaim means "conditions." Traditionally it is a short written agreement between the two families about the coming wedding. After it is read, the two mothers break a plate together, a reminder that even at the happiest moments we remember the destruction of the Holy Temple, and that a broken engagement cannot be fully repaired.

Customs vary. Some communities sign tenaim at the engagement; today many sign them on the wedding day, just before the ceremony. Ask the Rabbi what fits your family.

## The ring

An engagement ring is a lovely custom, but it is a gift, not a Jewish requirement. The ring that matters in Jewish law is the one the groom gives the bride under the chuppah. Traditionally that wedding ring is a plain band, owned by the groom, without stones, so its value is clear and simple. Many couples choose a separate engagement ring with stones and a plain band for the ceremony.

## The time between engagement and wedding

In Chabad, engagements are usually short, often a few months. There is wisdom in this: once a couple is ready to commit, there is no reason to wait, and a long engagement can bring needless stress. Every couple's situation is different, though, and the Rabbi is glad to help you think it through.

Use the time well:

- **Set a date** with care. See [Choosing a Wedding Date](/learn/choosing-a-wedding-date).
- **Learn.** Begin [kallah classes](/learn/kallah-classes) and [chosson classes](/learn/chosson-classes) a few months before the wedding.
- **Ask for blessings.** Many couples ask parents and grandparents for a blessing, and many write to the Rebbe or visit his resting place to pray for a blessing.
- **Keep it meaningful.** Use this time to get to know each other's values and dreams, and to prepare for the home you will build together.
- **Plan the home, not just the party.** Talk about Shabbat, kosher, and the kind of Jewish home you want to build.

## Common questions

### Do we need a rabbi involved at the engagement?
Not for the engagement itself, but it helps to talk to the Rabbi early, especially before setting a date and booking a hall.

### Is it bad luck to announce an engagement before a l'chaim?
There is no such rule. Families share the news in their own way. Some keep it quiet until the close family has heard, simply out of courtesy.

### Should the wedding ring have diamonds?
Traditionally the ring used under the chuppah is a plain band without stones. An engagement ring can be anything you like.
