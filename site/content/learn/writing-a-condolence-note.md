Someone you know has lost a parent, a spouse, a child, a friend. You want to reach out, and the pen freezes. What can words do? In Jewish tradition, words do a great deal: they tell the mourner that they are not alone, and that the person they lost is remembered.

## The traditional words

The sentence Jews have said to mourners for centuries is:

**"HaMakom yenachem etchem b'toch she'ar aveilei Tzion v'Yerushalayim."**
*May the Omnipresent comfort you among the other mourners of Zion and Jerusalem.*

G-d is called *HaMakom*, "the Place," because He is everywhere, including in the house of mourning, where He may feel farthest away. "Among the other mourners" reminds the bereaved that they belong to a people that has known loss and endured it, and that their grief is held by a whole community. Write it in Hebrew, English or both. Simpler traditional phrases are "May you be comforted" and "May his (her) memory be a blessing."

## What to write

A good condolence note has three parts, and it can be only a few lines long.

1. **Say that you are sorry.** "I was so sad to hear about your mother." Name the person who died; mourners love to hear the name.
2. **Share a memory or a quality.** This is the heart of the note. "I will never forget how your father greeted everyone at the door as if they were the guest he had been waiting for." If you did not know the person, say what you saw in the mourner: "I know how devoted you were to her."
3. **Offer comfort and presence.** The traditional words above, and something concrete: "I will bring dinner on Tuesday," "I am saying Tehillim for you." If you cannot visit, say that you are thinking of them and will be in touch.

Sign it warmly. It is thoughtful to add that you are giving tzedakah in memory of their loved one, which Judaism considers a real gift to the soul; see [tzedakah](/learn/tzedakah) and [give in their memory](/donate).

## What not to say

- **"They are in a better place," "It was for the best," "At least..."** Even when meant kindly, these ask the mourner to feel better before they are ready. Do not explain the loss away.
- **"I know how you feel."** Each loss is its own.
- **"Let me know if you need anything."** Mourners rarely ask. Offer something specific instead.
- **Long accounts of your own losses.** One sentence of shared understanding is enough.
- **Questions about how it happened.** If they want to tell you, they will.

## When to send it

Send it as soon as you hear. A note that arrives during [shiva](/learn/shiva), the first week, is read and reread. But a late note is never wrong: mourners often say the cards that came after the first month, when the house grew quiet, meant the most. The end of [sheloshim](/learn/sheloshim) (thirty days) and the first [yahrzeit](/learn/yahrzeit) are especially thoughtful times to write again: "Thinking of you and of David this week."

## Text, email or card?

A handwritten card can be kept and reread for years. A text or email is far better than silence, especially in the first days and from friends far away. Do not expect a reply on Shabbat or a holiday, and do not be hurt if it takes a while.

## Common questions

### What if I did not know the person who died?
Write about the mourner: what you know of their love for the person, and that you are there for them. "I never met your grandfather, but the way you spoke of him told me what a remarkable man he was."

### Should I mention G-d or religion if the family is not religious?
"May his memory be a blessing" is loved by Jews of every background. The traditional Hebrew words are always appropriate at a Jewish house of mourning.

### Is it appropriate to send flowers?
Flowers are not a Jewish custom at a funeral or shiva house. Food for the shiva house, a donation in the person's memory, or planting a tree in Israel are the traditional gifts. See [comforting mourners](/learn/comforting-mourners).

### What do I write to a child who lost a parent?
Simply and honestly: that you are sad, that their parent loved them, and one specific memory. Children treasure stories about the parent they lost. See [talking to kids about death](/learn/talking-to-kids-about-death).
