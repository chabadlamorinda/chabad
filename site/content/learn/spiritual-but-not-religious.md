Many people today say they are "spiritual but not religious." They feel there is more to life than work and screens, they want meaning, calm and connection, but "religion" sounds like rules, guilt or a club they do not belong to. If that describes you, Judaism may surprise you.

## Judaism is not a religion in the usual sense

The word "religion" does not appear in the Torah. The Torah speaks of a covenant, a relationship between G-d and a people, and of a way of life. Judaism is less about joining something and more about discovering who you already are: a soul in a body, with a purpose in this world. See [is Judaism a religion or a nation?](/learn/is-judaism-a-religion-or-a-nation)

## You already have a soul

Chabad teaching, based on the Tanya, says that every Jew has a G-dly soul, "a part of G-d above." That inner pull toward meaning, the sense that you were made for something more, is not a mood. It is your soul speaking. Read [the soul](/learn/the-soul) and [part of G-d above](/learn/part-of-g-d-above).

## Spirituality you can do

In Judaism, spirituality is not only a feeling; it lives in actions. A mitzvah is a moment that connects heaven and earth, and the word "mitzvah" is related to the Aramaic word for "connection." Some simple places to start:

- **Gratitude on waking.** Say [Modeh Ani](/learn/modeh-ani) before you check your phone: a one-line thank you for a new day.
- **Quiet contemplation.** Jewish meditation, called hitbonenut, means slowly thinking about G-d's greatness and closeness. See [Jewish meditation](/learn/jewish-meditation).
- **A real day off.** [Shabbat](/learn/unplugging-on-shabbat) is a weekly sanctuary in time: no email, no shopping, just family, food, rest and soul.
- **Blessings on nature.** Judaism has a blessing for a rainbow, for blossoming trees and for the ocean. See [blessings on nature](/learn/blessings-on-nature); the hills of Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda give plenty of chances.
- **Kindness.** Giving tzedakah or visiting someone who is lonely is spirituality in its purest form.

## Do and then understand

At Mount Sinai, the Jewish people said "na'aseh v'nishma": we will do, and we will understand. You do not need to believe everything or have every question answered before you begin. Try one mitzvah, see what it opens in you, and let understanding grow from there. Questions and doubts are welcome too; see [doubts about G-d](/learn/doubts-in-faith).

## Common questions

### Can I be Jewish and "spiritual but not religious"?
Yes. Every Jew has a G-dly soul, whatever they practice or believe. Judaism invites you to express that spirituality through simple actions, a blessing, a Shabbat candle, an act of kindness, at your own pace.

### Does Judaism have meditation?
Yes. Jewish meditation (hitbonenut) is deep, focused thought about G-d, often before or during prayer. Chabad teaching gives it special emphasis.

### I don't believe in G-d. Is there a place for me?
Yes. Many people start with questions or doubts. Judaism values honest questions, and a mitzvah done today counts fully even before every question is answered. Bring your questions to a class or to the Rabbi.
