Tanya's letter on anger (Epistle 25 in the Tanya's section of letters, Iggeret HaKodesh) says that a person in a rage resembles an idolater, because at the moment of anger he has lost his faith that what happened comes from G-d. The Alter Rebbe, Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi, teaches that if we truly remembered this, we would not lose our temper.

The Lubavitcher Rebbe's advice on anger draws on this letter. Our general guide, [anger: Jewish wisdom](/learn/anger-jewish-view), covers many teachings; here we stay with this one letter and how to use it.

## Why anger is compared to idolatry

Chabad.org's presentation of the epistle quotes the Sages' words that whoever is in a rage resembles an idolater. The Alter Rebbe explains the comparison: anger shows a lapse in faith in G-d's individual providence, meaning that G-d guides what happens to each of us.

When someone hurts or insults us, we fix our attention on the person and on our loss. The letter says to remember that the harm itself was already decreed from Above, and that G-d has many agents. The one who wronged us is still responsible for his own free choice. Still, at the moment of anger a person has stopped seeing G-d in the picture.

## When anger can be right

The letter makes an exception. Anger may be proper when it is meant to keep another Jew from breaking a prohibition, as Moses did. The reasoning above is about personal harm, not about Torah and mitzvot, which are in our own hands.

Nothing in the teaching says to accept being abused or to stay in a dangerous situation. If anger, threats or violence are part of your home, please get help right away; call the Rabbi in confidence.

## The remedy in the letter

Epistle 25 offers an inner exercise. Remember that even in the person who wronged you there is a spark of Divine life. And when a disturbance cannot be stopped, such as someone talking during prayer, the letter suggests seeing it as a spark of the Divine Presence in exile, and using that thought to deepen your own concentration.

## The Rebbe's practical steps

Chabad.org's article "How to Deal with Anger" gathers the Rebbe's advice from simple to deep:

1. **Wait.** The first step is to hold back and not to express the anger in words. Pause and breathe.
2. **Remember Who is watching.** The Rebbe once advised a student to memorize the opening of Chapter 41 of Tanya and to review it whenever anger begins, since it speaks of G-d's watching over us at every moment.
3. **Remember the consequences.** Because anger hurts others, the Rebbe advised apologizing in person, even if the hurt was done in anger, and asking forgiveness. Knowing that you will have to apologize can help you hold back.
4. **Remember who is in charge.** The deepest step comes from Epistle 25: the other person is a messenger who is still responsible for his choices. Instead of asking "Why is this person doing this to me?" ask "What is G-d telling me through this moment?"

## At home and at work

This is useful in a marriage or with children, where small things add up; see [marriage arguments](/learn/marriage-arguments-jewish-wisdom) and [patience](/learn/patience). It fits a busy workday too, wherever anger is quick. Nobody can fully master it, so start with step one.

## Common questions

### What does Tanya say about anger?
Tanya's Epistle 25 says that a person in a rage resembles an idolater, because at that moment his faith that everything comes from G-d has left him.

### Is anger a sin?
The Sages compare it to idolatry, so Judaism takes it very seriously. There is an exception for anger meant to stop another person from breaking a prohibition.

### How can I stop being angry according to Judaism?
The Rebbe's advice: wait before you speak, remember that G-d is watching, remember you may need to apologize, and remember that everything comes from G-d.

### Does this mean I should not mind when someone hurts me?
No. The person who hurt you is still responsible for his choice. The letter asks us not to lose our faith and balance while dealing with it.

### What if my anger is out of control?
Please talk to a doctor or counselor, and ask the Rabbi. Tanya's guidance is spiritual and professional help is also right when needed.
