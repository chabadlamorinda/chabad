Many Jewish homes in Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda have a dog at the door or a cat on the windowsill. Judaism has a lot to say about the animals in our care: how to treat them, how to feed them, and how our Jewish calendar touches their lives too. The short answer is that caring for a pet well is a mitzvah, and the details are easier than you might think.

## Kindness to animals is a Torah law

Causing an animal needless pain, *tza'ar ba'alei chayim*, is forbidden by the Torah itself. The Torah tells us to help unload an animal collapsing under its burden, to let the ox eat while it works, and to rest our animals on Shabbat. Moses and King David were chosen as leaders after they showed tenderness to sheep. A pet that is fed, sheltered, exercised and treated gently is a daily practice of this mitzvah. Read more in [kindness to animals](/learn/tzaar-baalei-chayim).

## Feed your animals before yourself

The Talmud (Berachot 40a) teaches that a person should feed their animals before sitting down to eat, learning this from the verse "I will give grass in your field for your cattle, and you shall eat and be satisfied": the animals come first in the verse. Many families make this a habit: the dog's bowl is filled before dinner is served. It is a small, daily lesson in responsibility, and a wonderful one for children.

## Pets on Shabbat

Shabbat is a day of rest for our animals too, and the Torah includes them in the commandment. Day to day, caring for a pet on Shabbat is allowed and expected: feeding, giving water, letting a dog out, walking it. Some questions have details, such as walking a dog on a leash where there is no eruv, handling an animal (the question of [muktzeh](/learn/muktzeh)), or what to do if a pet becomes ill on Shabbat. The principle is clear: an animal's needs are met, and an animal in pain is helped. For your situation, ask the Rabbi.

## Pet food on Passover

On Passover a Jew may not own or benefit from *chametz*, leavened grain, and that includes the food we give our animals. Many dog and cat foods contain wheat or other grains, so before the holiday check the ingredients and switch to a chametz-free food for the week (your pet does not need to eat matzah, and kitniyot, such as rice and corn, are fine for animals). Store opened chametz pet food away and include it in your [sale of chametz](/learn/selling-your-chametz). Start a week or two early so your pet gets used to the new food. More in [what is chametz](/learn/chametz) and the [Passover guide](/holidays/passover).

## Other everyday questions

- **Spaying, neutering and medical care.** Judaism values an animal's health and comfort; some procedures involve halachic questions, so ask the Rabbi before deciding.
- **Kosher and pets.** Your pet does not keep kosher, but a kosher home does not cook meat and milk together for an animal either, and pet food is kept off the dishes you eat from.
- **A pet is not a child.** Judaism treasures pets but keeps human life and human needs first; a lost pet is grieved without the rituals of mourning a person.

## When a pet dies

The grief is real, especially for children, and Judaism takes it seriously while keeping the Jewish mourning laws for people. Good ways to mark the loss: thank G-d for the years together, give tzedakah in the pet's honor, and talk with your children about kindness and about G-d's care for every creature. We have a separate guide on [losing a pet](/learn/loss-of-a-pet), and the Rabbi and Rebbetzin are always glad to talk.

## Common questions

### Can I walk my dog on Shabbat?
In general, yes. In a place without an eruv there are questions about the leash and about carrying anything, and some details depend on where you live in Lafayette, Moraga or Orinda. Ask the Rabbi for your street.

### Does my dog need kosher-for-Passover food?
Your pet's food must be free of chametz, but it does not need kosher certification, and grains that are kitniyot are fine for animals. Read the ingredients carefully; many brands have a grain-free line.

### Should I say Kaddish for a pet?
No. Kaddish and the laws of mourning are for people. You may remember a pet in your own words, give tzedakah in its honor and tell its story to your children. See [losing a pet](/learn/loss-of-a-pet).
