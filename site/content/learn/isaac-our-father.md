Isaac (Yitzchak, יִצְחָק) was the second of the three patriarchs, the son of Abraham and Sarah and the father of Jacob. His name means "laughter," and he is remembered for quiet strength: he was willing to give his life, he kept his father's work alive, and he is credited with beginning the afternoon prayer, Mincha.

## A son born with laughter

Isaac was born when Abraham was 100 years old and Sarah was 90, a year after the angels announced the news. Chabad.org, following Jewish tradition, says he was born on the first day of Passover and circumcised on the eighth day. He was named Yitzchak, "laughter," because of the joy his birth brought. Read about his parents in [Abraham and Sarah](/learn/abraham-and-sarah).

## The Binding of Isaac

In the Torah's account, G-d tested Abraham by asking him to bring Isaac as an offering on a mountain three days' journey away. Jewish tradition teaches that Isaac was 37 and that he was a willing partner, ready to give himself if G-d asked. At the last moment G-d revealed that it was only a test, and a ram was offered instead. This is the Akeidah (עֲקֵדָה, "the binding"), recalled in the Rosh Hashanah prayers and when we blow the [shofar](/learn/shofar), a ram's horn. The same tradition says Sarah passed away soon after.

## Finding Rebecca

Abraham sent his servant Eliezer to Aram Naharayim to find a wife for Isaac, and Eliezer found Rebecca (Rivkah), the daughter of Bethuel. Isaac was 40 when they married. The Torah says Isaac was out in the field praying when Rebecca first saw him, and she covered herself with her veil. After about twenty years without children, Rebecca gave birth to twins, Esau and Jacob. Meet the women of the Torah in [the matriarchs](/learn/the-matriarchs).

## Digging the wells again

Isaac lived in Gerar, where the Philistines had filled in the wells that Abraham's servants had dug. Isaac had them reopened. He dug new wells too. The first two led to quarrels, and he named them Esek ("conflict") and Sitnah ("enmity"). The third caused no fight, and he called it Rechovot ("wide open space"). He then settled in Beer Sheva.

This is a beautiful picture of Isaac's role: he did not invent a new path, he cleared the old wells so his father's waters could flow again. Every Jewish parent who lights candles or tells the Passover story the way their grandparents did is doing the same.

## The blessings

When Isaac was old and blind, he intended to bless Esau. Rebecca helped Jacob receive the blessing instead, and Isaac blessed Jacob. Jacob then left for Padan Aram, and Isaac did not see him for more than 22 years. The Torah shows us a father who cared deeply about blessing his children, a custom many Jewish parents continue on Friday night: see [blessing your children](/learn/blessing-your-children).

## Isaac and the Mincha prayer

The Torah says Isaac "went out to pray in the field toward evening" (Genesis 24:63). From this, our sages learned that Isaac originated the afternoon service, Mincha. You can read how the three daily prayers fit together in [Mincha and Maariv](/learn/mincha-and-maariv).

## Common questions

### Who was Isaac in the Bible?
Isaac was the second patriarch, the son of Abraham and Sarah and the father of Jacob and Esau. He is remembered for his willingness at the Binding, his marriage to Rebecca and his prayer.

### What does the name Isaac mean?
Yitzchak means "laughter." He was named for the joy of his birth to Abraham and Sarah in their old age.

### What was the Binding of Isaac?
The Binding (Akeidah) was G-d's test of Abraham, asking him to offer Isaac. At the last moment G-d revealed it was a test and a ram was offered instead.

### Who was Isaac's wife?
Isaac's wife was Rebecca (Rivkah), daughter of Bethuel. Abraham's servant Eliezer found her for Isaac.

### Why did Isaac dig wells?
Isaac reopened the wells Abraham's servants had dug and the Philistines had filled in. He also dug new ones, naming the third Rechovot, "wide open space."

### Which prayer did Isaac begin?
According to Jewish tradition, Isaac started the afternoon prayer, Mincha, when he went out to the field to pray.
