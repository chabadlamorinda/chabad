To host a guest who keeps kosher in a home that is not kosher, serve sealed, kosher-certified food on disposable plates with disposable cutlery, plus fresh fruit and vegetables. Ask your guest what they eat instead of guessing, because every kosher household has its own level of care.

## Start by asking, gently

The best first step is a short, friendly question: "What would make you feel most comfortable at our table?" Kosher guests are used to this question and usually have simple answers. Many will say that fruit, vegetables, salad and sealed packaged food are fine. Others will bring their own food. Both are common, and neither is an insult. Chabad.org's guide for hosts reminds us to keep questions few and kind and to avoid overwhelming your guest. In Lafayette, Moraga and Orinda, many families have a parent, child or friend who keeps kosher, so you are not alone.

## What to serve: the simple list

1. **Fresh fruit and vegetables.** Whole, washed fruit is always fine. For leafy greens and berries, which can hold insects, ask your guest, or see [checking for insects](/learn/checking-for-insects).
2. **Sealed packaged food with a kosher symbol.** Chips, crackers, juice, cookies and similar packaged foods are fine if they are kosher certified. Wine has extra rules (your guest may prefer to open and pour it), so ask your guest first. Learn the marks in [kosher symbols](/learn/kosher-symbols).
3. **Disposable plates, cups and cutlery.** Because your dishes were used for non-kosher food, your guest will not eat from them. Disposable items solve this.
4. **A kosher meal from a certified source.** Frozen kosher meals, or a meal ordered from a kosher caterer, are the closest to a full dinner. [Bay Kosher](/programs/bay-kosher), a project of Chabad of Lamorinda, delivers kosher food in the East Bay.

## Hot food and the oven

A guest may eat a hot kosher meal that is sealed in its original packaging. Many people allow sealed food to be heated in a non-kosher oven if it stays double-wrapped or sealed, but the rules depend on the kind of food and your guest's practice. The simplest way is to ask your guest how they would like it warmed, and to let them tell you if they prefer to eat it cold or reheat it themselves. If you are unsure, ask the Rabbi.

## What not to worry about

- You do **not** have to make your kitchen kosher for a one-time visit.
- You do not need to buy new pots or a new set of dishes.
- Your guest does not expect you to keep kosher yourself. They are grateful for the effort.
- It is fine for others at the table to eat what they usually eat, as long as your guest has their own plate and food.

Keep the kosher food sealed on the table, and keep the plates and utensils for your guest apart from your own.

## Make your guest feel at home

Hosting is itself a mitzvah, [hachnasat orchim](/learn/hachnasat-orchim). Small touches go a long way: a place for the kosher food, a fresh fruit plate, and a pitcher of cold water. If your guest also keeps Shabbat, see [Shabbat guest guide](/learn/shabbat-guest-guide) for ideas about timing and meals.

If a relative's visit has you thinking about taking on more kosher practice, read [keeping kosher with family](/learn/keeping-kosher-with-family).

## Common questions

### How do I host a guest who keeps kosher?
Ask what they are comfortable with, then serve sealed kosher-certified food, fresh fruit and vegetables on disposable plates and cutlery.

### Can a kosher guest eat in a non-kosher home?
Yes, if the food is sealed and kosher-certified and is served on disposable dishes. Fresh fruit and vegetables are also fine.

### Do I need to make my kitchen kosher for a visit?
No. For a visit, the kosher guest eats food that never touches your pots and dishes.

### Can I heat kosher food in my oven?
Often yes, if the food stays sealed in its package or double-wrapped. The details depend on the food and your guest's practice, so ask your guest and the Rabbi.

### What should I say to my guest?
