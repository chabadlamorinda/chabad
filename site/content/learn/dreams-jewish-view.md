Everyone dreams, and some dreams stay with us long after we wake up. The Torah is full of dreams, and the Talmud discusses them at length. Judaism takes dreams seriously, but not too seriously.

## Dreams in the Torah

- **Jacob** dreamed of a ladder reaching to heaven, with angels going up and down (Genesis 28).
- **Joseph** dreamed of sheaves and stars bowing to him, and later interpreted the dreams of Pharaoh's butler and baker, and then of Pharaoh himself: seven years of plenty and seven years of famine.
- **Daniel** interpreted the dreams of Nebuchadnezzar, king of Babylon, with wisdom from G-d.

In these stories, dreams carry messages. Yet even Joseph said, "Interpretations belong to G-d" (Genesis 40:8).

## What the Talmud teaches

The Talmud (Berachot 55a to 57b) has a long discussion of dreams. A few of its teachings:

- "A dream is one-sixtieth of prophecy." There can be something real in a dream, but only a small part.
- "Just as there is no grain without straw, there is no dream without some nonsense." Even a meaningful dream is mixed with things that mean nothing.
- "A person is shown only what comes from the thoughts of his heart." Much of what we dream reflects what we thought about during the day.
- "A dream that is not interpreted is like a letter that is not read." Dreams tend to follow how we take them, which is a good reason to interpret them for the good.

## After a disturbing dream

First, take a breath. Most bad dreams come from worries, stress or a heavy meal, and do not predict anything.

The Talmud describes a practice called **hatavat chalom**, "making a dream good," in which a person says a special text before three friends, who answer with blessings. Some also give tzedakah or say [Tehillim](/learn/tehillim). The details depend on the situation, so ask the Rabbi.

## The prayer during Birkat Kohanim

The Talmud records a short prayer for one who had a troubling dream, said while the Kohanim bless the congregation: "Master of the world, I am Yours and my dreams are Yours. I dreamed a dream and I do not know what it is..." It asks G-d to turn every dream into good, just as He turned Balaam's curse into a blessing. You will find it in the [siddur](/learn/siddur) next to [Birkat Kohanim](/learn/birkat-kohanim). In the diaspora, where the Kohanim bless the congregation on festivals, many say it then.

## For a peaceful night

The [bedtime Shema](/learn/bedtime-shema) includes a request that "my thoughts, bad dreams and harmful fantasies not disturb me." Ending the day with Shema, a moment of forgiveness and a few words to G-d is the best preparation for good sleep and good dreams.

## Common questions

### Can dreams predict the future?
Rarely, and only in part. The Talmud calls a dream one-sixtieth of prophecy. Do not make decisions based on a dream; ask the Rabbi if one weighs on you.

### I dreamed about a relative who passed away. What does it mean?
Many people find such dreams comforting. Some give tzedakah or learn Torah in the person's memory afterward. If the dream upset you, talk to the Rabbi.

### Should I fast after a bad dream?
There is such a practice in Jewish law, but it applies only in certain cases. Ask the Rabbi before doing anything like this.

### My child has nightmares. What can help?
Say the Shema together at bedtime, with the angels' names ("On my right is Michael..."). Reassurance and a calm routine help most. See [angels](/learn/angels).
