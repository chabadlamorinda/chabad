<!-- ================= BLOCK: Message from the Rabbi ================= -->
<tr><td style="padding:0 0 26px;">
  <p style="margin:0 0 10px 4px;font:600 13px Arial,sans-serif;letter-spacing:.1em;color:#85600f;">MESSAGE FROM THE RABBI</p>
  <table role="presentation" width="100%" cellpadding="0" cellspacing="0" border="0" style="background:#ffffff;border:1px solid #e8dfcc;border-radius:14px;">
    <tr><td style="padding:26px 28px;">
      <p style="margin:0 0 14px;font:600 22px/1.3 Georgia,serif;color:#14284b;">Zevulun: building G-d's home at work</p>
      <p style="margin:0 0 12px;font:16px/1.65 Georgia,serif;color:#1f2433;">In this week's Torah portion, Vayeitzei, Leah gives birth to her sixth son and names him Zevulun, saying, "This time my husband will make his dwelling with me." Rashi explains that the name comes from the word zevul, a dwelling place. It is a short verse, but the Rebbe draws from it a powerful message for every one of us who spends our days in the working world.</p>
      <p style="margin:0 0 12px;font:16px/1.65 Georgia,serif;color:#1f2433;">Our Sages describe a famous partnership between two tribes. Zevulun went out to sea and engaged in commerce, and with his earnings he supported his brother Yissachar, who devoted himself to Torah study. That is why Moshe, in blessing the tribes, mentions Zevulun before Yissachar. The usual takeaway is that the businessperson earns merit by supporting Torah. True, but the Rebbe suggests there is much more to Zevulun than writing the check.</p>
      <p style="margin:0 0 12px;font:16px/1.65 Georgia,serif;color:#1f2433;">Consider the question. Yaakov is the patriarch of Torah, "a dweller of tents," the one who sat and learned. Why would his permanent home be determined by Zevulun, the son associated with trade and business? And Rashi chooses the word zevul, the same word King Solomon used for the Holy Temple, a permanent and lasting home. What does a merchant have to do with permanence?</p>
      <p style="margin:0 0 12px;font:16px/1.65 Georgia,serif;color:#1f2433;">The answer begins with Yaakov's own life. He spent twenty years in the house of Lavan, working as a shepherd day and night, through scorching heat and freezing cold. He worked, as he says, "with all my strength," and the Rambam holds him up as the model of an honest employee. Yet it was precisely there, in the house of a swindler, far from the tents of study, that Yaakov flourished "exceedingly, exceedingly." There he raised all twelve tribes, every one of them righteous. His greatest spiritual achievement came not in the study hall, but in the workplace.</p>
      <p style="margin:0 0 12px;font:16px/1.65 Georgia,serif;color:#1f2433;">This is no accident. The Midrash teaches that G-d desires "a dwelling place in the lower realms." The purpose of creation is not only fulfilled in the synagogue or the yeshiva, but in the marketplace, the office and the home, the places where G-dliness seems most hidden. When we conduct business honestly, use our resources for mitzvos, and do "all our deeds for the sake of Heaven," we turn the ordinary world into a home for G-d. That is the service of Zevulun.</p>
      <p style="margin:0 0 12px;font:16px/1.65 Georgia,serif;color:#1f2433;">One could still object. The work of refining the world is temporary. In the era of Mashiach, the Rambam writes, the occupation of the entire world will be solely to know G-d. Torah is eternal, but commerce will one day be finished. So how can Zevulun's work create something permanent?</p>
      <p style="margin:0 0 12px;font:16px/1.65 Georgia,serif;color:#1f2433;">The Rebbe's answer is beautiful. The true value of Zevulun's work is not the transactions themselves. It is what they reveal. Every Jewish soul is bound to G-d at its very core. A person sheltered from the world has that same bond, but it may never be tested. When someone is immersed in the pressures of business, deadlines and bottom lines, and still sets aside time to learn, still prays, still keeps the mitzvos, that inner, eternal connection is brought out into the open. It is like the Rambam's teaching about the baal teshuvah, whose bond with G-d is all the stronger for having been challenged.</p>
      <p style="margin:0 0 12px;font:16px/1.65 Georgia,serif;color:#1f2433;">This leads to a very practical lesson. Those of us in business should never think of our Torah study and prayer as second-class, as if we only do a little because we lack the time of full-time scholars. Just the opposite. The Alter Rebbe teaches that a businessperson who pulls away from the noise of the day to open a sefer has achieved something special, because he or she had to overcome so much to get there. Fixed times for Torah study, the Rebbe says, should be fixed not only on the calendar but in the soul.</p>
      <p style="margin:0 0 12px;font:16px/1.65 Georgia,serif;color:#1f2433;">The same is true of prayer, especially on Shabbos. During the week we may be rushed. But on Shabbos, free from work, we have the opportunity to pray with greater depth and feeling. The Alter Rebbe writes that businesspeople are mistaken if they think their prayer cannot match that of scholars. Light that shines out of darkness is the brightest light of all, and a heart that has spent the week in the world can reach a love of G-d that is uniquely powerful.</p>
      <p style="margin:0 0 12px;font:16px/1.65 Georgia,serif;color:#1f2433;">So this Shabbos, let's take Zevulun's lesson to heart. Whatever our profession, our workday is part of building G-d's home in this world, and our Torah and prayer are where that hidden strength shines through. May our efforts, in the office and in the shul, bring the day that is entirely Shabbos, with the coming of Mashiach speedily in our days.</p>
      <p style="margin:0 0 4px;font:16px/1.65 Georgia,serif;color:#1f2433;">Shabbat Shalom,</p>
      <p style="margin:0;font:italic 18px/1.5 Georgia,serif;color:#14284b;">Rabbi Israel &amp; Tzipora Labkowski</p>

      <p style="margin:0 0 12px;font:600 22px/1.3 Georgia,serif;color:#14284b;">Vayeitzei <span style="font-weight:normal;color:#85600f;">וַיֵּצֵא</span> · "And he went out"</p>
      <p style="margin:0 0 10px;font:16px/1.6 Arial,sans-serif;color:#1f2433;">Jacob leaves Beersheba for Haran. Stopping for the night, he dreams of a ladder set on the ground with its top reaching heaven, and angels going up and down on it. G-d promises to give him the land, to make his descendants many, and to protect him. Jacob names the place Beth El and vows to dedicate a tenth of all he receives.</p>
      <p style="margin:0 0 16px;font:16px/1.6 Arial,sans-serif;color:#1f2433;">In Haran he meets Rachel at the well. He works seven years for Rachel, but her father Laban gives him Leah instead, and Jacob works another seven years for Rachel. Eleven sons and a daughter are born, the last of them Joseph. Despite Laban's tricks, G-d blesses Jacob's flocks, and Jacob sets out for home with his family.</p>
